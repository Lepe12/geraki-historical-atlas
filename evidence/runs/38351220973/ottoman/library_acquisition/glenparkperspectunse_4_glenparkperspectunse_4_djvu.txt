for  pets,  away  from  holiday  commotion,  so  that  their 
holiday  will  be  happy  and  safe,  too. 


Forked  Tongue 

by  Mike  Hitchcock 

In  BankAmerica  Corporation's  Third  Quar- 
ter Stockholders'  Report  for  1978,  President 
A.W.  Clausen  tells  us,  "Our  society  has  for  too 
long  encouraged  consumption  at  the  expense  of 
savings  and  investment,  and  inflation  is  one  of 
the  legacies."  Then  a  few  pages  later  we  learn 
that  BankAmerica's  consumer  finance  subsid- 
iary is  about  to  embark  on  "an  agressive 
expansion  program.  " 

Expansion  of  consumer  credit  is  a  major 
way  "our  society"  encourages  over-consumption 
and  its  legacy,  inflation.  There  seems  to  be  a 
failure  of  communication  between  the  corpo- 
rate head  and  its  subsidiary... Or  perhaps  Mr. 
Clausen  speaks  with  a  forked  tongue.

In  Memoriam 


George  R  Moscone  — 

A  Personal  Perspective 


by  Lu  Blazej 

Lu  Blaze],  a  neighborhood  planner  for  the 
San  Francisco  Department  of  City  Planning, 
lives  on  Mateo  Street  in  Glen  Park. 


It  was  clear  to  everyone  who  knew  George 
Moscone  that  he  loved  San  Francisco  and  truly 
cared  for  its  people.  His  concern  for  this  city 
and  for  each  of  us  was  not  only  that  of  a  mayor, 
but  also  that  of  a  good  neighbor.  He  took  per- 
sonal interest  in  the  problems  that  face  us-an 
interest  that  compelled  George  to  spend  rainy 
evenings  inspecting  the  muddy  unimproved 
streets  on  Bernal  Heights,  and  meeting  with  resi- 
dents and  city  department  heads  to  resolve  their 
problems.  George  also  took  pride  in  the  successes 
of  city  government,  no  matter  how  small.  He 
came  to  Glen  Canyon  Park  for  example  to 
inaugurate  our  new  neighborhood-built  play 
structure  and  to  inspect  the  improvements  to 
our  recreation  center. 

George  Moscone  loved  us  and  was  proud  of 
the  energy,  concern,  and  commitment  neighbor- 
hoods showed,  not  only  to  meeting  their  own 
needs,  but  to  the  City  as  a  whole.  George  drew 
his  strength  from  the  neighborhoods  not  only 
politically  but  also  spiritually.  He  believed  in  us 
and  gave  us  the  support  and  opportunity  to 
govern  San  Francisco  along  with  him. 

George  brought  a  sparkle  of  new  energy  to 
the  stodgy  corridors  of  City  Hall.  The  doors 
were  opened  to  all,  and  a  new  light  shone  on 
many  old  and  persistent  problems.  George 
looked  to  all  of  us  to  help  solve  our  problems 
and  build  a  better  city.  Where  we  pitched  in  and 
worked  together,  great  progress  was  made. 

George  Moscone  gave  us  all  a  unique  and 
precious  gift— he  gave  us  government  wherein 
we  as  individuals  and  as  neighbors  can  meaning- 
fully participate  in  shaping  and  building  a  city 
that  is  a  pride  to  us  and  a  joy  to  our  children. 

George,  thank  you  for  showing  us  the  way. 
We  sorely  miss  your  leadership.  We'll  continue 
to  nurture  your  gift  and  share  in  your  vision.

Sally  Gearhart,  a  professor  of  speech/communication  at  San  Francisco  State 
University,  was  a  close  friend  of  Supervisor  Harvey  Milk.  As  part  of  the  memorial  service 
at  Temple  Emanuel  on  November  29th,  Ms.  Gearhart  eloquently  presented  the  following... 


EULOGY  FOR  HARVEY 


He  was  a  man-I  had  not  known  him  long. 
And  yet,  you  know,  it  seemed  you'd  known  him  all  your  life- 
He  was  a  man  unlike  most  men  I  knew, 

A  man  of  strength  and  grace,  who  let  me  open  my  own  doors  when  they  were  mine  to  open, 
And  who  knew  instinctively  a  door  too  heavy  for  my  strength  alone. 
And  then  he'd  push  or  pull,  whichever  was  appropriate,  to  help  me  do  the  things  he  knew 
I  had  to  do. 

He  was  a  man  unlike  most  men  I  knew. 
He  stood  tall  even  in  his  stockinged  feet, 
Didn't  dope  and  didn't  smoke  and  didn't  drink. 
You  wondered  where  he  got  his  energy 

Until  you  saw  him  with  a  little  child,  dancing  down  Noe  Street  on  an  impulse  in  the 

San  Francisco  sun, 
On  a  day  in  May.  Or  June.  Or  February. 
For  that  was  Harvey's  way:  to  play. 

He  was  a  man  who  heard  what  women  said, 

Even  when  the  things  they  said  were  painful  things. 

Who  dared  suggest  almost  a  year  ago  that  in  our  coming  battle  for  the  rights  of  man 
Not  a  man's  voice  should  be  heard, 

That  only  women  should  be  in  evidence  in  the  long  campaign  ahead. 
He  knew  it  would  not  be  that  way. 

But  he  had  seized  the  truth  about  that  awful  proposition: 
That  what  it  really  was  about  was  women,  and  the  fear  of  all  that's  womanlike. 
He  was  a  man  to  make  you  stand  and  cheer, 
And  even  separatist  women  turn  to  one  another  saying, 
'Well,  if  that's  what  manhood's  all  about, 
It  might  not  be  so  bad  at  that." 

He  was  a  man  unlike  most  men  I  knew, 

Whose  happiness  would  be  complete  when  he  could  spend  his  days  off  at  the  circus, 
Behind  the  anonymity  of  a  costume  and  a  painted  face, 
Romping  up  clown  alley  to  the  ring 

To  fall  and  laugh  and  shout  and  make  his  nose  go  "honk!"  for  all  the  front  row  children- 
and  their  parents,  too. 

He  was  a  man  unlike  most  men  I  knew, 

Who  lived  so  poorly  in  his  house,  so  richly  in  his  friends, 

Who  rode  the  Muni  every  day 

And  yet  could  travel  at  the  speed  of  light  to  the  scene  of  some  injustice; 
Whose  joy  of  living  outstripped  his  love  of  his  work, 
But  only  by  an  inch  or  two. 

I  think  I  never  met  a  man  who  loved  so  much  to  say  hello. 

I  wonder  where  we'll  find  another 

WhoU  harangue  a  Born  Again  Christian  minister 

And  tell  him  that  he  ought  to  be  a  Jew? 

I  think  I'll  find  no  one  else  who  will  stand  beside  me 

To  celebrate  with  all  our  kin  our  survival  of  our  confrontation  with  an  enemy, 
Both  of  us  that  night  looking  just  straight  enough-right  out  of  Middle  America-just 
straight  enough 

And  just  old  enough  to  be  the  parents  of  most  of  the  folks  gathered  there  at  Mission  High. 
Who  else  will  call  an  hour  before  his  TV  appearance  and  say, 
"I've  lost  my  earrings,  dear.  Whatever  shall  I  do?" 

He  was  a  man  unlike  most  men  I  knew. 

He  was  our  champion,  our  friend,  our  comrade,  and  our  brother. 

And  he  knew  as  we  must  know 

That  eternity  is  measured  not  in  length  of  days 

But  in  quality  of  life. 

And  so,  when  in  all  our  lives  to  come 

We  raise  a  banner  high 

Or  speak  a  word  for  freedom  here  and  there. 
The  world  can  say  of  all  of  us: 
"There  is  some  corner  of  their  fighting  hearts 
That  is  forever  Harvey." 


So  be  it. 


-Sally  M.  Gearhart

Originally,  the  new  project  was  going  to 
consist  of  four  floors,  the  maximum  allowed  in 
Glen  Park.  But  a  look  at  the  neighborhood  told 
Dodson  that  such  a  building  would  be  way  out 
of  scale  with  "downtown"  Glen  Park  as  we 
know  it.  Next,  he  compared  the  merits  of  three 
floors  with  just  two.  Because  of  the  additional 
parking  requirements  for  three  floors,  the 
expense  of  an  elevator,  the  economic  tendency 
for  rents  to  decrease  the  higher  you  go,  and  the 
fact  that  other  buildings  in  the  area  have  only 
two  floors  have  led  to  Dodson's  current 
inclination  toward  a  two-story  building. 

The  first  floor  will  house  the  savings  and 
loan  office  along  with  another  use,  possibly  a 
pharmacy.  Dodson  is  investigating  the  feasibility 
of  a  second  floor  restaurant,  with  offices-maybe 
dental— as  an  alternative. 

The  project  is  still  in  the  early  planning 
stage.  Glen  Park  residents  with  ideas  regarding 
architectural  style,  building  materials,  and 
appropriate  uses  should  send  them  to  the  GPA, 
P.O.  Box  31337,  S.F.  94131,  as  soon  as  possible. 
Dodson  has  accepted  an  invitation  to  appear  at

plans  made  up  to  that  date  and  to  provide 
another  opportunity  for  neighborhood  comment.

Parking  a  Lot 


by  Laura  Goderez 

Two  years  ago,  people  in  over  250  house- 
holds in  the  area  directly  adjacent  to  the  Glen 
Park  BART  station  signed  a  petition  requesting 
Preferential  Parking.  This  was  a  community 
effort  to  reclaim  the  streets  from  commuters 
who  used  all  available  parking,  preventing  resi- 
dents from  parking  near  their  own  homes, 
blocking  driveways,  and  reducing  parking  for 
merchants'  customers.  The  bureaucratic  wheels 
moved  slowly,  and  so  it  is  that  the  system  has 
only  just  been  installed. 

The  Preferential  Parking  system  has  been 
used  in  many  cities  around  the  country  and  in 
other  parts  of  San  Francisco.  It  works  by 
restricting  parking  for  nonresidents  while 
exempting  residents  from  the  restrictions  by 
means  of  a  sticker  displayed  on  the  residents  car. 
Residents  may  then  park  in  the  restricted  area 
without  being  ticketed,  while  long-term  non- 
residential parkers  will  be  fined  for  parking  too 
long  in  restricted  areas.  Residents  who  use  off- 
street  parking  (garages)  need  not  purchase  a 
sticker. 

Publicity  about  Preferential  Parking  in 
North  Beach  may  be  discouraging  until  one 
realizes  that  North  Beach  and  Glen  Park  have 
very  different  parking  problems.  North  Beach 
is  largely  commercial  during  the  day  and  has  an 
active  night  life  as  well,  requiring  extended 
hours  of  restriction  (until  9:00  p.m.).  There 
are  many  multiple  dwelling  units  and  consider- 
able resident  turn-over,  all  resulting  in  confusion 
over  who  is  and  who  is  not  entitled  to  purchase 
a  Sticker.  (Continued  on  page  1 7)

Practical  Uses  of  Leviiation     As  YOU  Like  It  •  •  • 


Levitation-the  ability  to  defy  gravity  and 
float  up  in  the  air  like  a  piece  of  lint  or  a 
sandwich  wrapper  suspended  on  a  breeze— is 
on  the  upswing. 

Millions  of  movie-goers  watched  what's-her- 
name  appear  to  levitate  in  the  movie  The 
Exorcist,  and  apparently  they  liked  what  they 
saw.  Now  the  TM  (Transcendental  Meditation) 
people,  who  believe  in  peace  at  any  price  (or  at 
least  $250),  are  touting  levitation  as  a  means  of 
rising  above  it  all. 

Levitation  has  always  looked  like  fun.  But 
what,  if  any,  are  the  practical,  down-to-earth 
benefits? 

To  find  out,  Swallow's  Guide  talked  to 
Les  Doote,  of  Napa,  Calif.  Mr.  Doote  can,  with- 
out any  visible  ropes  or  wires,  raise  himself 
several  yards  off  the  ground.  As  we  interviewed 
him,  he  was  actually  hovering  about  six  feet  over 
a  barstool  in  the  Valley  of  the  Moon. 

Is  that  very  hard  to  learn? 

"Sort  of.  You  have  to  work  at  it.  I  guess  it's 
like  getting  any  project  off  the  ground.  Could 
you  hand  me  up  my  highball?" 

Sure.  What  are  some  of  the  practical  uses  of 
levitation? 

"Well,  say  you  rent  a  room  with  a  lumpy 
mattress?  There's  one.  Getting  a  kite  started  is 
another.  Sleeping  with  a  child  who  wets  the  bed. 
I  should  also  mention  flood  zones-it's  real 
handy  in  flood  zones.  And  changing  overhead 
lightbulbs.  Levitation  has  virtually  eliminated 
my  need  for  a  stepladder." 

Do  you  ever  have  any  kind  of  trouble  once 
you're  up  there? 

"No,  but  I  used  to  have  trouble  landing. 
What  happened  was,  I  would  come  down  on  my 
stomach  in  a  sort  of  belly  flop.  If  you've  ever 
flopped  onto  the  floor  of  a  den  cluttered  with 
Marantz  stereo  components,  you  know  it's  no 
fun.  The  trick  is  to  swoop  down  on  your  back 
and  sort  of  taxi  to  a  stop." 

That  sounds  complicated. 

"It  is.  You  might  need  training  wheels  at 
first.  What  I  did  was  have  my  wife  sew  little  air- 
plane landing  gear  into  the  shoulders  and  hips  of 
all  my  flight  suits.  It  helped." 

One  more  thing-is  it  true  that  a  lot  of  the 
UFO's  spotted  in  rural  areas  are  actually  hicks 
levitating? 

"Beats  me.  I  guess  anything  is  possible. 
Would  you  mind  reaching  up  here  and  freshening 
my  drink?"  #

Your  Feet  Gel  Wet 
When  You  Try  To  Stop 

by  Pamela  Hitchcock 

The  "wet  season"  arrived  early  at  Walter 
Haas  Playground  this  year.  Even  before  the  rains 
began  to  drench  the  rest  of  the  Bay  Area,  the 
sand  underneath  the  large  play  structure  at  the 
Diamond  Heights  and  Addison  Street  play- 
ground was  covered  with  several  inches  of  water. 
The  Recreation  and  Park  Department  does  not 
know  what  caused  the  water,  but  Assistant 
Superintendent  Jim  Rogers  admits  that  Walter 
Haas  Playground  has  a  "drainage  problem." 

Approximately  two  months  ago,  Rogers 
submitted  a  priority  work  order  asking  for  an 
auger  (driller)  to  go  to  the  playground,  drill  a 
hole,  and  fill  it  with  gravel  hoping  that  this 
would  solve  the  problem.  A  large  number  of 
other  priority  needs,  as  well  as  staff  attrition  due 
to  Proposition  13  and  the  loss  of  Federal  Title  II 
funds  that  could  have  been  used  for  this  purpose, 
have  kept  the  work  order  unfilled. 

Rogers  was  not  optimistic  that  the  anti- 
cipated hole  and  gravel  would  solve  the  problem. 
He  frankly  admitted  he  had  no  idea  what  was 
wrong  but  doubted  that  the  water  came  from 
the  sprinkler  system,  an  idea  I  had  suggested 
to  him.  He  felt  there  might  even  be  an  under- 
ground spring  on  the  site. 

Rogers  was  also  not  very  optimistic  about 
Rec  and  Park's  ability  to  solve  the  problem.  He 
assured  me,  however,  that  they  were  going  to  do 
everything  possible  including  going  to  the  City 
Engineering  Department  and  charging  that  they 
should  have  been  able  to  foresee  the  problem 
when  they  were  planning  and  developing  the 
park.  Rogers  warned  that  any  court  action 
emerging  from  such  a  confrontation  would  be  a 
lengthy  and  expensive  process  and  would  be 
pursued  only  as  a  last  resort.

Some  time  ago,  when  the  Glen  Park  Branch 
Library  was  still  housed  at  the  corner  of  Diamond 
and  Kern  Streets,  CPA  member  and  local  Lorax* 
Frank  Morgan  spotted  a  rare  artifact.  Hiding  behind 
the  pocketbook  display  rack,  torn  and  yellowed  with 
age.  was  a  real  estate  advertisement  dated  1908. 

The  neighborhood  now  called  Glen  Park  was 
then  owned  by  Crocker  Estate  Company,  and  Arthur 
G.  Duncan,  exclusive  selling  agent,  was  offering  choice 
lots  for  $300  at  $5  per  month.  Many  of  the  houses 
pictured  are  still  standing.  Long  gone  is  the 
Toonerville  Trolley  that  carried  weekend  picnickers 
up  Bosworth  Street  to  Glen  Canyon  Park.  Many  of 
the  street  names  have  been  changed,  some  streets 
were  planned  but  never  developed. 

Frank  Morgan  knew  he  had  found  something 
valuable.  He  asked  librarian  Betty  Siedhoff  about  the 
ad,  and  learned  that  a  former  Glen  Park  resident, 
Joan  Andersen,  had  donated  it  to  the  library.  Joan 
had  discovered  it  in  a  vacant  building  in  Glen  Park. 

Hoping  that  the  archivist  at  the  Main  Library 
could  get  the  broadsheet  restored  and  copied,  Frank 
took  custody  of  the  terribly  brittle  and  fragile  piece. 
Unfortunately,  the  Library's  camera  equipment  was 
inadequate  for  an  item  so  large  (20"  X  27").  But 
Morgan  never  gives  up!  When  he  showed  the  adver- 
tisement to  Jim  Gravanis,  Jim  thought  it  should  go 
in  the  GLEN  PARK  PERSPECTIVE,  perhaps  as  a 
Christmas  gift  to  the  neighborhood.  Now  he  was 
stuck  with  the  task  of  finding  a  processor  with  large 
enough  equipment  and  strong  enough  nerves  to  deal 
with  the  tenuous  document.  After  much  run-around, 
some  pleading,  and  with  the  help  of  Dolores  Gap  of 
Green  Mountain  Graphics,  Ruth  Gravanis  finally 
persuaded  Circus  Lithograph  to  take  on  the  job. 

But  the  price!  Even  with  a  thoughtful  discount 
there  was  no  way  that  the  PERSPECTIVE'S  meager 
resources  could  cover  the  cost  of  all  the  "shots" 
required  for  a  good  quality  reproduction.  To  the 
rescue  came  Jerry  Dodson  of  Continental  Savings  and 
Loan,  with  a  generous  donation  to  help  cover  part  of 
the  cost  of  getting  it  into  print. 

It  is  hoped  that  the  reader  will  take  the  time  to 
learn  about  "the  most  attractive  investment  in  real 
estate  that  San  Ffancisco  has  ever  seen... where 
nature's  deft  fingers  have  wrought  her  handiwork." 
If  enough  people  express  an  interest,  the 
PERSPECTIVE  may  have  the  advertisement  printed 
on  a  good  quality  paper.  Please  contact  the 
PERSPECTIVE  if  you  would  be  interested  in  pur- 
chasing a  permanent  copy  of  this  historical  treasure.

McAteer  Club  Recycles 
For  a  Whale  of  a  Time 

by  Pamela  Hitchcock 

Having  trouble  getting  all  your  garbage  into 
one  can?  Well,  the  Freewheelers,  McAteer  High 
School's  Hikers,  Bikers,  and  Recyclers  Club,  has 
the  answer  for  you.  On  the  first  Saturday  of 
every  month  from  9  a.m.  to  12  noon,  they 
collect  glass,  tin,  aluminum,  and  newspapers 
which  they  sell  to  recyclers  to  help  finance 
camping  and  ski  trips  and  other  activities.  A 
small  portion  of  the  money  they  make  is 
donated  to  the  Greenpeace  Foundation  to  help 
save  the  whales. 

To  find  the  Freewheelers,  enter  the  McAteer 
High  School  parking  lot  by  way  of  O'Shaughnessy 
Boulevard  just  south  of  Portola  Drive.  Follow 
the  recycling  center  signs  to  the  collection  area. 

The  Freewheelers  take  all  types  of  glass. 
While  most  of  the  glass  does  not  need  to  be 
sorted,  they  ask  that  you  separate  out  the 
bottles  that  can  be  washed  out  and  reused  (as 
opposed  to  melted  down  and  recycled).  These 
are:  California  wine  bottles,  fifths  that  came 
with  corks,  and  three-litre  bottles.  The  rest  of 
the  glass  is  thrown  into  a  large  bin  where  it  is 
crushed  by  recyclers  and  reused  in  a  variety  of 
ways.  Labels  do  not  need  to  be  removed  from 
bottles,  but  the  Freewheelers  ask  that  you  rinse 
the  bottles  and  take  off  the  caps. 

Newspapers  should  be  tied,  or  stacked  in 
paper  bags,  to  make  them  easier  to  handle.  This 
recycling  center  does  not  take  magazines,  phone 
books,  or  cardboard. 

Aluminum  cans  should  be  rinsed  and 
crushed.  You  can  also  recycle  foil,  pie  plates, 
and  T.V.  dinner  trays.  Tin  should  be  kept 
separate  from  aluminum,  and  tin  cans  should 
also  be  crushed.  You  can  easily  crush  aluminum 
cans  by  stepping  on  them.  To  flatten  the  heavier 
tin  cans,  remove  the  top  and  bottom  first  to 
make  it  easier. 

Bi-metal  cans  are  not  recyclable.  A  bi-metal 
can  is  made  of  aluminum  and  tin  bonded  together 
so  that  it  cannot  be  melted  down  with  either 
metal.  The  natural  resources  in  bi-metal  cans 
cannot  be  reclaimed.  If  a  can  has  both  a  pop  top 
and  a  side  and  bottom  seam,  it  is  bi-metal.  On 
some  bi-metal  cans  the  seams  are  not  obvious. 
Look  for  the  words  "all  aluminum"  to  be  sure 
it's  recyclable.  Cans  that  are  all  tin  do  not  have 
pop  tops. 

If  you  would  like  to  recycle  but  can't  on 
the  first  Saturday  of  the  month,  go  to  Mission 
High  School  at  1 8th  and  Church  Streets  on  the 
second  Saturday  of  the  month  from  8:30  to 
11:30  a.m.  or  to  Mission  Short  Circuit,  1425  San 
Bruno  Avenue  every  Saturday  from  9  a.m.  to 
5  p.m.  Recycling  will  make  you  feel  good  and 
can  eliminate  the  need  to  jump  on  your  garbage 
in  order  to  get  it  all  in  the  can.  Mother  Earth 
thanks  you  too!  •

War  and  Remembrance 

by  Phil  Fanning 

War  and  Remembrance,  by  Herman  Wouk 
1978,  Little,  Brown,  and  Company,  $15.00 

War  and  Remembrance  is  Herman  Wouk's 
magisterial  novel  of  World  War  II  as  seen  through 
the  eyes  of  those  who  played  a  part  in  it,  both 
important  and  unimportant,  both  real  and  ficti- 
tious. It  is  also  one  of  the  best  informal  histories 
of  World  War  II  I  have  seen. 

A  sequel  to  Wouk's  earlier  novel  The  Winds 
of  War,  War  and  Remembrance  continues  the 
story  of  U.S.  Naval  officer  Pug  Henry  and  his 
family.  (It  is  not  necessary  to  have  read  the 
earlier  book  to  understand  and  appreciate  this 
one.)  The  human  side  of  the  book  is  about 
lovers  separated  by  war  and  about  the  effect  that 
separation  has  on  them  and  their  love.  It  has 


many  touching  moments.  Wouk  explores  the 
dying  love  between  Pug  Henry  and  his  unfaithful 
wife,  the  deepening  but  unconsummated  love 
between  Henry  and  a  young  British  journalist, 
and  the  interrupted  love  of  Henry's  son  (also  a 
naval  officer)  and  his  Jewish  wife,  who  is  caught 
inside  Nazi  Europe  with  their  infant  son  and 
who  sinks  deeper  and  deeper  into  the  miasma  of 
Hitler's  "final  solution  to  the  Jewish  question." 
The  historical  side  of  the  book  is  the  whole 
panoply  of  World  War  II,  with  Roosevelt, 
Churchill,  Stalin,  Truman,  and  many  other 
historical  personages  walking  through  Wouk's 
pages  just  as  they  did  through  this  great  world 
drama.  Wouk  takes  us  behind  Russian  lines  just 
after  the  battle  of  Stalingrad,  to  wartime  Wash- 
ington, to  Oak  Ridge,  Tennessee,  where  top- 
secret  work  was  done  on  the  first  atomic  bomb, 
to  submarine  patrol  in  the  Pacific,  to  the 
infamous  Auswitz  extermination  camp,  and  to 
the  "showplace"  camp  at  Treblinka.  Throughout, 
the  author's  style  is  clean  and  spare,  for  the

by  Debbie  Davis 


Glen  Park  Branch  Library  featured  the 
collage  work  of  Jean  Conner  during  the  month 
of  November.  This  display  reflected  a  number  of 
exciting  images  constructed  largely  from  maga- 
zine clippings. 

Jean  has  worked  in  a  variety  of  mediums 
including  watercolor,  pastel,  and  oil.  Her  experi- 
mentation with  collage  work  began  in  1960, 
using  only  black  and  white  images.  She  finds  this 
medium  easy  to  work  with  and  has  collected 
many  magazine  clippings  for  future  collages. 
Animals  abound  in  this  collection,  which  Jean 
plans  to  use  in  a  work  with  the  theme  of  "the 
peaceable  kingdom."  One  particular  piece  in  the 
displayed  collection,  entitled  "Room,"  is 
especially  intriguing  as  it  can  be  viewed  from 
either  a  horizontal  or  vertical  perspective. 

Jean  obtained  her  BFA  at  the  University 
of  Nebraska  and  continued  her  education  at 
Colorado  University  where  she  received  her 
MFA  degree.  Although  not  actively  employed  at 
present,  she  has  taught  at  San  Francisco  State, 
substituted  for  one  year  as  a  multi-cultural 
resource  teacher,  and  spent  one  and  a  half  years 
as  a  para-professional.  She  originally  came  to 
San  Francisco  with  her  husband,  Bruce,  in  1957. 
They  left  for  a  brief  period  of  three  years  (part 
of  which  was  spent  in  Mexico),  and  returned  to 
San  Francisco  in  1965.  Their  last  five  years  have 
been  spent  in  Glen  Park.  Jean's  work  recently 
appeared  at  the  Higher  Grounds  Coffee  House  in 
Glen  Park. 

One  of  Jean's  favorite  artists  has  been 
Redon,  a  worker  of  pastels.  Jean  has  done  some 
pastel  work-in  fact  it  is  very  popular  with  her- 
but  she  considers  it  difficult  to  show  due  to  its 
messy  nature.

THE  WOMAN  WHO  TAUGHT  ME  TO  SEE 


When  I  came  to  her,  I  saw  everything  twice.  She  smelted  of  violets.  The  lights  were  soft  for  our  eyes. 
She  said  I  should  be  well  treated-not  made  to  cry  uselessly. 

She  told  me  about  black  cats  walking  plush  black  carpets  into  black  rooms  issuing  music  played  by  a 
black-gloved  woman  seated  at  a  black  piano.  She  made  me  love  the  black  of  night  wherein  I  safely  slept,  a 
child.  I  think  her  hair  was  red. 

She  stretched  me  from  the  eyes.  She  worked  my  muscles  and  set  me  in  the  sun  to  cure.  She  threw 
away  my  glasses.  Her  house  smelled  of  garlic,  herbs,  and  twilight.  Her  dresses  sounded  silk  as  she  moved 
about  me  in  slippers.  She  stroked  my  temples. 

Leaving  her  was  an  assault  of  white  bleached  white.  The  city  fell  like  rocks  onto  my  softened  face.  I 
rode  home  on  my  bike,  bearing  her  aromas-lemon  verbena  and  violet.  I  rode  home  through  one  vision 
at  a  time.

ends  her  haunting  to  let  words  come  from  a  sultry  sky.

and  she  will  crowd  into  the  mouse 's  hole  clutching  her  cheese.

AAG  Backs  9  Winners 


Action  for  Accountable  Government 
(AAG),  a  new  political  alliance  of  labor  and 
neighborhood  activists  in  San  Francisco,  endorsed 
and  campaigned  for  nine  winners  in  the 
November  7th  election  of  the  new  1  5  member 
Charter  Commission.  Of  the  four  ad  hoc  orga- 
nizations created  to  support  a  slate  of  candidates 
for  the  Commission,  AAG  was  the  most  success- 
ful according  to  SPUR's  comparison  of  the  pro- 
portion of  candidates  elected,  the  proportion 
who  finished  among  the  top  25,  and  the  average 
vote  cast  per  candidate  per  slate. 

The  winning  candidates  backed  by  AAG 
are:  Wilson  Chang,  Frank  Fitch,  Eulalio  Frausto, 
Pat  Jackson,  Leo  Jed,  Rodney  Johnson,  Jack 
Morrison,  Pat  Schultz,  and  Sam  Walker.  Wilson 
Chang,  a  member  of  AAG's  steering  committee, 
was  the  highest  vote  getter  in  the  field  of  104 
candidates. 

"Now  that  the  election  is  over  we  are 
committed  to  ensuring  active  public  partici- 
pation in  the  development  of  a  new  charter," 
said  AAG  convenor  Eric  Craven.  "AAG  will  also 
be  taking  a  close  look  at  the  mayoral  and 
supervisorial  races  in  1979,"  Craven  added.
