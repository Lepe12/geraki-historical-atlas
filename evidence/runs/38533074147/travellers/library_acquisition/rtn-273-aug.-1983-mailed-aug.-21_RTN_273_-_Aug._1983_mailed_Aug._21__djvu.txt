Dallas Votes for Rapid Transit 


Voters in Dallas TX and 13 suburbs voted on Saturday, Aug 13 by a 58% majority 
to approve construction of a 160-mile surface light rail system and to adopt a 1 
sales tax to finance it. Funding will be entirely local, Seven more suburbs opted 
out of the system; they total 6 or 7% of the proposed system's serviced population. 

In 1980 an earlier voter try failed; at that time, Dallas and Houston voters were 
tied together into a vaguely-defined system. Supporters of DART (Dallas Area Rapid 
Trench reportedly spent $1 million to lure voters into approval of the system, 
while opponents spent $60,000. The American Automobile Association (Southwest 
Notor Club} was a prominent opponent of the system, 

The sales tax is to take effect next Jan 1, and construction on the system can 
be expected to begin in about 18 months, while within 6 to 7 years 20-30 miles of 
the system should be completed. The first line might be in operation by 1987, 

Only 3.4 miles, about 2% of the system, will be in subway, a fact which lowers its 
total cost. About 80% of the system will be at street level, largely using exist- 
ing rail rights of way. Besides the rail system, a doubling of the area's 

fleet of buses will be financed by the transportation sales tax. 

Dallas officials were reportedly influenced by the experiences of the city of 
Toronto. In May, Dallas leaders flew to Toronto for a briefing on transportation 
and city design. Toronto officials told them that their city's rail transporta- 
tion system was the key to the city's success. "Dallas is in many respects where 
Toronto was 30 years ago, in terms of transportation, development of the central 
business district and downtown housing," Dallas City Manager Charles Anderson was 
quoted as saying after three days of Canadian briefincs. 

Before passage of the referendum, Texas Governor Mark White signed a bi11 that 
nakes if difficult for DART planners to deviate markedly from the original system 
plan. This act was believed to facilitate the referendum's passage, by reassuring 
citizens that they would get close to what they voted for, "No one can arbitrari- 
ly make changes without the citizens knowing," commented DART Chairwoman Adlene 
Harrison in describing the bill. "We wanted to clean up any loopholes, People more 


than ever today want to believe in that type of credibility.” page 3

On Wednesday, August 3, 145,000 daily Long Island Rail Road riders woke up to 
find that their trains were stilt] running. An after-midnight agreement between the 
Metropolitan Transportation Authority and the four remaining unions, representing 
1900 workers without a contract, prevented a 12:01am walkout. 

The settlement came about largely because management dropped their demands to be 
allowed to contract out certain equipment repair work to subcontractors and to have 
some union employees do incidental work normally assigned to other unions. Both 
changes were designed to reduce costs and increase productivity. The LIRR said 
that no jobs would be eliminated but the unions were not so sure about long-term 
employment security, and they threatened to strike if these demands were not 
taken off the negotiating table. 

Qn money questions, the four unions had been working without a contract since 
the last one expired Dec 31, 1981. The settlement provided for a 20% wage in- 
crease spread over the life of the same three-year contract already accepted by 
13 other unions. The increments were 6% retroactive to Jan 1, 1982, 7% retroactive 
to Jan 1, 1983, and 7% effective Jan 1, 1984.

URBAN-SUBURBAN TRANSIT 


Seven years and nearly $800 million later, the Baltimore Metro (subway) is ready 
for revenue passengers. It will open for business Monday, fiov 21. lnitial opera- 
tions will be limited to weekdays, from about 5am to 8pm, About 25,000 people per 
day are expected to ride-~increasing to 45,000 or 50,000 within a year. 

The line would probably be in operation now, or within a few weeks, except for 
a problem with delivery of cars from the Budd Company in Phitadelphia. Announce- 


nent of the opening date was dependent upon having sufficient cars available. Fif- ~ 


ty-eight are expected to be available for the November opening, out of 72 ordered. 

An extension is already under construction, scheduled for operation in 1987, It 
will run six miles from the northwest Baltimore city line thru Baltimore County to 
Owings Hills. That part of the line will operate in the median strip of an express 
way under construction in conjunction with the fietro line. The eight miles due to 
open this fat] run from the heart of downtown Baltimore to Reisterstown Road Plaza. 
Of that, 4.5 miles are underground, 2.5 miles elevated, and one mite at ground 
level, The above-around portion parallels the Chessie System's former liestern 
Maryland mainline right of way. 

Initially, bus routes will not be adjusted. The fare will be similar to bus 
fares at 75¢. 

Unlike the Washington systen, the Baltimore Metro was not plagued by mammoth 
cost overruns. The initial estimate of $727 million was exceeded by only $70 mil- 
lion, Part of that is accounted for by a larger car order than originally planned. 

Maryland transportation officials recently unveiled a list of transportation 
projects to be funded by at least $800 million worth of funds diverted from high- 
way projects. Leading the list is a short extension of the subway underground 
from downtown Baltimore to Johns Hopkins Hospital. That well-known medical insti- 
tution is one of the largest employers in the city. Also proposed: a busway from 
Amtrak's Penn Station north to the Baltimore Beltway, using the old Northern Cen- 
tral right of way. A light rail proposal for the corridor was rejected. The 
diverted money will also fund a variety of highway-related projects, Transporte- 
tion officials developed a list of priorities after careful negotiation with local 
officials, That included a trip to Pittsburgh by Mayor Schaefer of Baltimore and 
Baltimore County Executive Hutchinson to get a first-hand view of the busway oper- 
ations there. -«Ken Maylath.

T he proposed PhiladelphiaeAtlantic City rail corridor has run into fierce oppo- 
sition from residents living near the track that is to be used by Amtrak 
trains under the rail plan. 

The focus of the anti-train efforts is twofold: the cost, seen by many to be ex- 
cessive at a time of tax hikes and service cutbacks by New Jersey state agencies 
and municipalities; and the fear of 80mph diesel trains passing thru residents! 
communities. 

Opposition is based in a group of area residents and politicians, known as RAGE 
(Residents Against Gamblers’ Express), The group was formed in June when it becane 
apparent to its members and others that the revived rail line would primarily bene- 
fit Philadelphia-area residents destined for Atlantic City and its casinos. Altho 
the operator of one of Atlantic City's casinos, Resorts International, said it will 


use its own funds to build a rail passenger terminal next to a planned casino-hotel , 


the $50 million required to rebuild and operate the line will come from Federal and 
state cofferse-$30 million from Amtrak, $20 million from the New Jersey DOT, 

The plan calls for five daily Philadelphia-Atlantic City round trips. Depending 
upon whom one talks to, trains will make no stops en route, or make only one stop, 
either at Lindenwold, the eastern terminus of a rapid transit line from Philadel- 
phia, or at Atco, a large bedroom community five miles east of Lindenwold. Else- 
where, opponents believe, trains will pass thru densely populated towns such as 
Pennsauken, Merchantville, Cherry Hill, Haddonfield and Berlin at 80mph, polluting 
the air with diesel exhaust and posing a safety hazard to residents, especially 
children, and motorists, 

One of the leaders of RAGE is Joan O'Brien Cohen, who lives in a condominium 
complex in Haddonfield next to the rail line Amtrak says it may use, RAGE's mem- 
bership and support include the mayors of Pennsauken, Merchantville, Haddonfield 
and Cherry Hill, State Assembly Member John A, Rocco, and U.S. Representative 
Chris Smith, whese district includes Pennsauken. 

Cn July 28, members of RAGE met with New Jersey Governor Thomas H. Kean to 
further lobby for a termination of the project. A spokesman for Kean was reported 
afterwards by the Philadelphia Inquirer as saying the Governor considers the rail 
plan "dead" simply because the state cannot come up with the $20 million as re- 
quired by the transport funding bi11 now in Congress that includes $30 million in 
Federal "matching" funds, 

RAGE's views, and those of other opponents, range from "no train, ever" to "if 
there's going to be a train, it should make stops en route." Cherry Hill and Lin- 
denwold, and Hammonton in Atlantic County, have been mentioned as possible stops. 

Other groups share the views of RAGE that the rail line will use public funds 
to serve primarily, if not only, casino-bound tourists. The Boards of Chosen 
Freeholders in both Camden and Atlantic Counties have voted for resolutions to 
oppose the rail line in its present form--not necessarily against the idea of 
restoring train service per se. "We would like to see a plan that has local 
Ae Camden County Freeholder Brenda Bacon told a reporter for the Philadelphia 

nquirer. :

Awrice High Speed Rail Corporation on Aug 8 filed its Project Description 
for the Southern California High Speed Rail System, the first step in the state 
and federal environmental review process, 

The Project Description and letter of intent were filed with Caltrans and the 
Federal Hichway Administration, The Filing requests both agencies to begin the 
environmental process by preparing a scope of work for the review, acting as lead 
agencies to coordinate the involvement of other state and federal organizations 
who will have an interest in the project. 

"We're pleased to have reached this point in development of the country's first 
high-speed rail system," said AHSRC President Lawrence 0. Gilson. "This action is 
part of the commitment we have made to the citizens of Southern California for a 
comprehensive environmental review of our project. Because of the public interest 
in this project, we are most anxious to begin the environmental review so that we 
may formally answer the questions and issues which naturally arise from a project 
of this scope," 

The review will be conducted under the guidelines of the California Environmental

PAUTO-TRAIN® REVIVED: When Amtrak starts up its Lorton-Sanford auto ferry ser- 
vice in late October, the revival will be not only of the car-carrying service 
but also of the name, Amtrak is petitioning the Auto-Train's bankruptcy court to 
let it use the Auto-Train name. The name is "locked into people's minds" said an 
Amtrak spokesman, and identified with the car-carrying passenger train service. 
Amtrak will call the service the "Amtrak Auto Train." Former A-T passengers who 
held tickets that were not honored when the original company closed down will be 
given discounts on the revived train, Amtrak said. But Amtrak and railroad en- 
ployees will not be able to use their passes on the new train. Amtrak will have 
to pay the bankrupt Auto-Train for the use of its name. In addition, Amtrak on 
Aug 12 asked the bankruptcy court to allow it to purchase A-T's Sanford FL facil- 
ity for a price of $2 million, The facility covers 19.8 acres. Amtrak is leas- 
ing the Lorton VA facility. The total startup costs for the service will tetal 
$3—4 million, Amtrak said. That includes payment of $1.7 million in cash for A-T's 
assets. Amtrak will also forgive $100,000 in debts owed it by A-T, These sums 
won't help the bankrupt company very much, as it reportedly was over $20 million 
in debt, Amtrak expects a profit of $4 million on the service during the first 
year of operation. The infrastructure is there already, an Amtrak spokesman noted, 
and the cost of putting another train on the tracks--Amtrak already runs about 250 
trains per day=--is negligible. Auto-Train, however, had a big infrastructure but 
only two trains per day to spread the costs over..o..
