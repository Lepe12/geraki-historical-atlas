The jury were instructed inter alia: 
1. That though William Drysdale acquired the legal title to the land 
in controversy, by the deed of John N. Conyngham, to him dated July 
16, 1828, and recorded Ja’y 13, 1832, yet he held in trust for the estate 
of which he was administrator, or rather for the heirs of the estate, 
(there being no creditors,) and held one undivided eighth part thereof 
for Alexander Turnbull, j’r, or his assigns; that this was not an express 
trust; that it was not declared in the deed to Conyngham, or to Drys- 
dale, but that it was a trust which the law implied, growing out of the 
relation of the parties. Drysdale, then, was the legal owner, while at 
the same time there was an equitable right in the heirs of the elder 
Turnbull to require him to convey the property to them, or if he had 
sold it at an advance, to require him to account for the proceeds of his 
sale. | 
The right of Alex. Turnbull, j’r, and that of the plaintiff, who claims 
as his heir, is merely an equitable one. I will not, however, now instruct 
you that the plaintiff cannot recover in this ejectment, solely 
Ol for that reason, bat 1 call your attention to the question whether 
the equity which was done, when the sheriff’s sale was made to

Conynghain, and subject to which Drysdale took the land, survived un- 
til this suit as brought. Such rights do not live forever. If they are 
not asserted within a reasonable time they die, and generally what is 
a reasonable time is determined in analogy to the statute of limitations. 
If it be an implied equitable right to land, ordinarily, it cannot be en- 
forced after twenty-one years. 

It is true if a party in whom such a right is vested has no knowledge 
of its existence, or means of knowledge, the law admits of longer delay 
in asserting the right. Reference was then made to the evidence which

fore 1871, when this suit was brought, or that they, or either of them, had 
means of knowledge that Drysdale, the administrator, had taken the 
title to himself and therefore held it in trust, the claim was too stale, 
that any equity in the plaintiff’s favor that may once have existed can- 
not now be enforced, and that the plaintiff cannot recover.

It is true no length of possession will protect a party against the entry 
or action of one in subordination to whom the possession has been held, 
and if the entry was in subordination to the title or right of another the 
possession is presumed to be continued subordinate until notice of 
adverse claim. is brought home to the holder of the paramount title. 
But I see no evidence that the defendants entered in subordination to 
any right of the plaintiff. When we speak of a subordinate title 
we mean that the inferior title is in priority with another, as in case of 

a tenancy for years, life, at will, &c. There was no such entry 
o4 in this case. The Mess’rs Roberts put upon record at the begin- 

ning a deed declaring the entire le’se to be in themselves, and there 
is no evidence that either they or their tenants, Pardee and Fell, were 
acknowledged a superior right, or any right, in the plaintiff. 

It is also true that the possession of one tenant in common is gener- 
ally regarded as the possession of his co-tenant until an ouster has taken 
place, and an ouster must be proved or presumed from facts in proof. 
Itis argued on behalf of the plaintiff that he wasa tenant in common with 
the Mess’rs Roberts in 1846, when they entered by their tenants; cer- 
tainly he was not of the legal estate; but if he was then a tenant in

been decided by the supreme court of this State that when one tenant 
in common enters on the whole and takes the profits, and claims the 
whole exclusively for twenty-one years, the jury ought to presume an 
ouster, though none be proved. (10 S. & K., 182; 9 Barr, 226; 155.

This action is one of several instituted by the same plain- 
tiff against these and other defendants, claiming property of 
immense value in the coal fields of Pennsylvania, and bears 
about it every mark of a merely speculative adventure.

hundred and thirty-nine and one-half acres of land surveyed

most productive and valuable sections of the coal field.

the time when the title remained in the ancestors of the plain- 
tiff, it had scarcely more than nominal value.

in 1835 (p. 38), insolvent (Ad., p. 1).

The first legal proposition relied on hy the defendants, 
namely, that “the trust set up by the plaintiff is too stale 
to be enforced by a court of equity,” is fully sustained 
by numerous decisions of the courts, both of the United 
States and of the several states of the Union, and of 
England. It is necessary to the security of title, and to 
maintain the transinissibility of real estate, that the owner 
of the legal title should not have his estate rendered com- 
paratively valueless, or that purchasers should be kept at bay 
for a long series of years by an implied trust depending 
‘solely upon inference, and of which there is no direct evi- 
dence. 

Within some reasonable period of time. he who claims title 


& 


under a resulting trust should be required to assert it, or 
otherwise be barred by laches. 

In Pennsylvania both the Legislature and the courts have 
fully recognized this policy, and its importance in promoting 
the improvement of real property, and the development of 
its resources and its wealth, by removing, after a sufficient 
lapse of time, every cloud upon the title, occasioned by an 
implied trust, which has lain dormant and has not been 
asserted within a reasonable time. 

The following decisions sustain the original proposition: 

In Perry on Trusts, a treatise of great value and of 
acknowledged authority, the author sums up the result of the 
several cases which he cites: 

“Time does not bar a direct trust where the relation of 
trustee and cestuz que trust is admitted to exist, but dili- 
gence must be used to establish a constructive trust on the 
ground of fraud. A court of equity will refuse its aid to 
stale demands, where a party has slept upon his rights, or has 
acquiesced for a great length of time. And so a constructive 
trust will be barred by long acquiescence, although the fraud 
was evident and the relief was originally clear.” | 

“Tt is difficult to state as a general proposition what length 
of time will bar relief from the consequences of a fraud. It 
is necessarily subject to the equitable discretion of the court, 
and must depend upon the nature of each case, and the cir- 
cumstances of the parties.”—Sec. 228. 

And in section 229 a large number of cases are cited in 
illustration of the principle, that whilst diligence is demanded

cording to its own circumstances.

at law, and may undoubtedly proceed according to the forms

this court has prescribed, regulating proceedings in equity in 
the courts of the United States.”
