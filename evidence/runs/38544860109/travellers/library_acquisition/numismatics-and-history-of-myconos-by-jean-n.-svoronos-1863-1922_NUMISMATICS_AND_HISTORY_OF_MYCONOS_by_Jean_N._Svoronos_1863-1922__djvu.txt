CHRONOLOGICAL CLASSIFICATION 
OF THE MYCONIAN COINS BY STYLE 


As the reader sees, we classified the Myconian coins in nine classes 
(A’- ©’), using as a guide this very style of the coins. Indeed, those of 
the class A’, the most ancient of all, are characterized by the almost ar- 
chaic form of Dionysus with pointed beard.' Immediately after these 
we have the class B’ where the god is depicted with rolled hair of ar- 
chaic style. Then follow the coins of I”, with similar but ruder types 
and Dionysus with a thicker beard. The class A’ presents the same 
types again but much more artful, the hair of the god being unbound 
and without braids. With the class E’ the art approaches its highest 
peak, while the type of Dionysus’ head is transformed to a beardless 
one and, on the bigger coins, is not turned to the right but facing; the 
coins are comparable to the best of Amphipolis and Rhodes. In the 
next class C’ the obverse type declines to an artless copy of the previ- 
ous class, and next to the grape and the corn grain, permanently 
found on all previous classes, a thyrsus is added. In the class Z’ 
Dionysus’ head remains unchanged, but almost barbarized, but with 
the class H’ a totally new period begins. The permanent type of the 
grape, on the coins of all previous classes, is replaced by a double ear 
of corn and the inscription

COINS STRUCK IN MYCONOS, DELOS AND TENOS 
BEFORE THE GRECO-PERSIAN WARS 


During the years of the great acme of the Cyclades and mostly 
from the first years of the invention of the coinage at the dawn of the 
7th century B.C. many silver coins were struck; at the islands of the 
Ionian settlers the Aeginetic standard was used, while the Dorians 
used the Phoenician standard. Aglaosthenes of Naxos testifies in his 
book Naxiaca that it was the Naxians who first struck coins and not the 
Aeginetans or the Lydians, as it is generally believed.'’ Of course the 
surviving Naxian coins do not seem to be so ancient as the first Aegi- 
netic or Lydian ones, but there is a didrachm attributed to Naxos on 
which is depicted the most ancient type of human figure ona coin; this 
piece was found in Thera and bears the head of a Silenus.’* Such a 
coin perhaps had in mind Aglaosthenes and, ignoring that the types 
of animals are older, was led to the above conjecture. 

Until now many archaic Cycladic coins have been found, notably 
on these very islands. So, in 1821 a hoard of 760 silver coins was found 
in Thera island, the most of them didrachms, registered in detail by 
H.P. Borell,'° and later discussed by Wroth in his article “The Santorin 
find of 1821”.4 A similar find emerged a few years later in Melos, and 
a third one equally important was recently described by Greenwell 
in his paper “On a find of archaic Greek coins of the islands of the 
Aegean sea”.'° Unfortunately these coins are anepigraphic, so their

MYTHOLOGY AND COIN TYPES 


As it is easily seen in the Plates and the Catalogue, the main type is 
the grape — mostly denoting the jocular element of the island; and, in- 
deed, the wines of the island are still its main product and a famous 
one in the antiquity.” Plinius includes the Myconian wine in the very 
best.” As a natural consequence, Dionysus was the prominently wor- 
shipped god. His ivy-wreathed head or bust were the main types of 
the obverse of all coins struck after the Greco-Persian Wars, on which 
the grape of the pre-war struck coins is transferred to the reverse as 
the main type followed by the Dionysiac thyrsus. On the coins of the 
years of Augustus, Dionysus is standing, naked, holding a phiale and 
a thyrsus, probably copying his main statue in the island. In the im- 
portant decree of sacrifices we read that Semele, his mother, and him- 
self as Baccheus and Lenaeus were also worshipped and because of it 
two of the months were locally called Bacchion and Lenaeon. Even 
more the mythological traditions of the island relate its archegetes 
Myconus to Dionysus, because Myconus was the son of Anius who 
was the father of Thasos and Andros, i.e. the archegetes of the homo- 
nymous islands, where Dionysus was par excellence worshipped, but 
he also was the father of the famous Oenotropoi of Delos, i.e. Spermo, 
Oeno and Elais, to whom their grandfather Dionysus “éyapioato 
onote Bobdoivto oivov, onéppata Kai Edaiov noleiv Kai LapBavelv” 
[= gave the gift of producing and getting wine, corn and olive oil whenever 
they want]. Anius’ parents were Apollo and the nymph Rhoeo.
