Abstract

15

Innovativeness and social learning are the pillars of cultural evolution. While the role of social

16

tolerance in social learning has long been acknowledged, its impact on innovativeness remains poorly

17

understood. Here, we test six groups of captive white-faced capuchins (Cebus capucinus) and brown

18

capuchins (Sapajus apella) to explore and compare the relationship between social tolerance and

19

problem-solving propensities. White-faced capuchins are renowned for their rich repertoire of social

20

behaviours, whereas brown capuchins are known for their diverse foraging repertoire, resulting in a

21

broad range of culturally transmitted behaviours in both species. We performed a co-feeding

22

experiment to assess species differences in social tolerance and a set of open diffusion novel food

23

puzzles to test innovativeness. We measured associations for each possible dyad within each group

24

during both experiments and compared the resulting social networks between conditions. We also

25

measured neophobia in an independent experiment presenting groups with three novel objects.

26

Overall, during both experiments, brown capuchins were more tolerant, and proportionally more

27

individuals spent their time within the testing areas at any given moment. We also found that

28

networks across experimental contexts were more similar in brown capuchins compared to white-

29

faced capuchins. Moreover, although differences in approaching and exploring food puzzles were

30

marginal, the success rate was higher in brown capuchins than in white-faced capuchins. Finally,

31

neither eigenvector centrality within the networks nor neophobia could explain individual problem-

32

solving success. Age was the strongest predictor for successfully extracting a food item. The results

33

indicate that species-level differences in social tolerance may contribute to divergent innovation

34

patterns, whereas individual-level variation within species may have only a minor influence. We

Introduction

62

Social tolerance is widely considered a prerequisite for group living as it supports the emergence of

63

social behaviours that mediate both agonistic and affiliative interactions (De Waal, 1986; van de

64

Waal et al., 2010). Accordingly, social tolerance has been defined as “the willingness of individuals to

65

interact non-agonistically with each other and to spend time in proximity to their social partner”

66

(Rina Evasoa et al., 2019). It has been identified as a key mechanism shaping multiple aspects of

67

social life, including cooperation (Cronin et al., 2005; Hare et al., 2007; Martin et al., 2021; Melis et

68

al., 2006), other-oriented prosocial behaviours (Burkart et al., 2014; Martin et al., 2021; Sehner et al.,

69

2025), reproduction (Schülke & Ostner, 2008), and access to food resources (de Oliveira Terceiro et

70

al., 2021; Fichtel et al., 2018; van Leeuwen et al., 2021). Social tolerance has also been shown to play

71

an important role in social information transfer (Coussi-Korbel & Fragaszy, 1995; Dean et al., 2012;

72

Whiten & van Schaik, 2007). Over recent decades, social tolerance has increasingly been linked to the

73

facilitation of social learning across a variety of species, including chimpanzees (Nodé-Langlois et al.,

74

2025; Whiten & van Schaik, 2007), orangutans (Fröhlich & van Schaik, 2022; Whiten & van Schaik,

75

2007), macaques (Garcia et al., 2022; Joly et al., 2017), capuchin monkeys (Coelho et al., 2024),

76

lemurs (Sehner & Fichtel in prep.) and birds (Miller et al., 2014). Tolerating naïve individuals in close

77

proximity becomes especially important when complex or difficult-to-acquire skills are transmitted,

78

such as manufacturing and using tools (van Schaik et al., 1999) or abstract competencies like gestural

79

communication (Fröhlich & van Schaik, 2022).

80

In this context, social tolerance is increasingly viewed as a potential driver of the

81

development of single traditions and thus of cultural evolution (Burkart et al., 2009; van Schaik et al.,

82

2019; Whiten & van Schaik, 2007). For instance, chimpanzees (Pan spp.) and orangutans (Pongo spp.)

83

that spend more time in association show a more diverse cultural repertoire (Whiten & van Schaik,

84

2007). These results have recently been confirmed by Nodé-Langlois and colleagues (2025), who

85

demonstrated that young chimpanzees benefit from living in more tolerant societies as they provide

86

the opportunity to learn from multiple individuals, which in turn facilitates a rich repertoire of

87

cultural behaviours. Furthermore, the development of new statistical tools, such as network-based

88

diffusion analyses (NBDAs), has provided further insights into how behaviours spread within a society

89

and showed that the likelihood of learning socially from conspecifics increases with physical

90

proximity (Hoppitt et al., 2010).

91

In several species, the diffusion of innovations follow the structure of the social network:

92

such as lobtail feeding in humpback whales (Megaptera novaeangliae) (Allen et al., 2013), the use of

93

marine sponges as foraging tools in bottlenose dolphins (Tursiops aduncus) (Wild et al., 2019), the

94

adoption of moss-sponging behaviour in chimpanzees (Hobaiter et al., 2014), the processing of novel

95

food in vervet monkeys (Chlorocebus pygerithrus) (Canteloup et al., 2020), or the spread of an

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.09.05.674457; this version posted September 7, 2025. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
available under a CC-BY-NC 4.0 International license.

96

opening technique of feeding boxes in red-fronted lemurs (Eulemur rufifrons) and ring-tailed lemurs

97

(Lemur catta) (Sehner & Fichtel, in prep). In addition, in squirrel monkeys (Saimiri sciureus),

98

individuals with higher Eigenvector centrality (a measure of social connectedness) were more likely

99

to acquire a novel foraging technique (Claidière et al., 2013). These findings highlight the importance

100

of the structure of social ties within a network in shaping the transmission of socially learned

101

behaviours.

102

A matter that has hitherto received less attention is how social tolerance may facilitate the

103

initial emergence of an innovation. Innovations describe behavioural processes resulting in new or

104

modified behaviours, including the ability to solve a novel problem (Reader & Laland, 2003). Social

105

tolerance has been acknowledged to facilitate the emergence of material culture in primates (van

106

Schaik et al., 1999) and it has been proposed that social tolerance can mediate the individual

107

propensity to innovate (Sehner et al., 2022). Though individuals may intrinsically differ in their

108

propensity to solve problems, leading to the idea that the probability that a novel problem is solved

109

within a group is based on the available “pool of competences” or “skill pool” (Giraldeau, 1984;

110

Morand-Ferron & Quinn, 2011). Hence, the more individuals a group contains, the more likely it is

111

that a problem will be solved by one of these individuals, which additionally will be reinforced by

112

mechanisms of social learning (Liker & Bókony, 2009; Morand-Ferron & Quinn, 2011; but see Cantor

113

et al., 2020).

114

However, the collective problem-solving continuum hypothesis predicts that the likelihood of

115

an individual solving a problem is not necessarily better in a group compared to when it is alone

116

(Sehner et al., 2022). Based on the social tolerance of a species, the probability of solving a problem

117

can either increase or decrease in a group setting. Groups with higher social tolerance are expected

118

to be more prone to work cooperatively or at least not competitively on a problem, while individuals

119

in less tolerant species will compete over access to a potential resource and block each other from

120

focusing on the actual problem. Among other factors, this notion might explain why humans, which

121

are regarded as hyper-cooperative (Burkart et al., 2014), have more complex innovations and thus a

122

more complex cultural repertoire in comparison to other apes (Migliano & Vinicius, 2022; Sehner &

123

Burkart, 2023). In nonhuman primates, it has been shown that highly socially tolerant primates like

124

callitrichids excel in cooperative problem-solving (Cronin et al., 2005), and that the innovative

125

complexity of groups surpasses that of individuals working alone (Sehner et al., 2022). In contrast, a

126

study on chimpanzees has shown that the maximum complexity that individuals reach within a group

127

reflects the maximum complexity that a single individual can achieve (Vale et al., 2021).

128

A key question in this context is whether all measures of social tolerance truly reflect the

129

same underlying construct (DeTroy et al., 2022). In their review, these authors proposed a distinction

130

between ‘behavioural social tolerance’, which refers to single tolerant (or intolerant) behaviour, and

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.09.05.674457; this version posted September 7, 2025. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
available under a CC-BY-NC 4.0 International license.

131

‘structural social tolerance’, a multi-dimensional construct encompassing both affiliative and

132

agonistic interactions that together reflect the overall hierarchical structure. Importantly, they

133

argued that these constructs do not necessarily align within a species. This distinction also suggests

134

that individual behaviours used to assess ‘behavioural social tolerance’ may not consistently

135

correlate with each other, and that proximity patterns can differ substantially across social contexts.

136

Nevertheless, it is common practice to use measures of tolerance taken outside the specific target

137

context to assess social tolerance. For instance, social networks based on huddling or proximity have

138

been used to test their role in facilitating social learning in foraging contexts (Coelho et al., 2024;

139

Fröhlich & van Schaik, 2022; Garcia et al., 2022; Nodé-Langlois et al., 2025). Yet, assuming that

140

tolerance is consistent across contexts can be misleading, as species like chimpanzees show tolerant

141

behaviour when resources are difficult to monopolize (DeTroy et al., 2021; van Leeuwen et al., 2021,

142

2023), but exhibit a contrasting pattern when resources such as food puzzles can be monopolized

143

(Knofe et al., 2019; Melis et al., 2011; Wittig & Boesch, 2003). Similar patterns have been observed in

144

ring-tailed lemurs, where the monopolizability of food resources affects the proportion of group

145

members permitted to co-feed (White et al., 2007).

146

To test whether tolerance is consistent across different foraging contexts and how it

147

influences innovative behaviour, we conducted two complementary experiments. First, we measured

148

social tolerance in a co-feeding paradigm (see for instance de Oliveira Terceiro et al., 2021; DeTroy et

149

al., 2021; Fichtel et al., 2018), where food could not easily be monopolized by one individual. Second,

150

we assessed tolerance during an extractive foraging task, using food puzzles that allowed dominant

151

individuals to exclude others. We also quantified individual problem-solving propensity and

152

examined whether, and in which contexts, social tolerance predicted successful food extractions. We

153

tested white-faced capuchins (Cebus capucinus) and brown capuchins (Sapajus apella) to examine

154

the relationship between social tolerance and innovation. While both species exhibit a diverse array

155

of traditions (Fragaszy et al., 2004; Fragaszy & Perry, 2003), these traditions differ between these

156

two taxa. Gracile capuchins (Cebus spp.) have a rich repertoire of social traditions (Perry, 2011, 2020;

157

Perry et al., 2017), including behaviours like eye-poking where individuals show high tolerance

158

toward each other. Although white-faced capuchins are less known for using tools (but see Barrett et

159

al., 2018), they regularly engage in object use (Boinski, 1988). Robust capuchins (Sapajus spp.), on

160

the other hand, have fewer documented social traditions but exhibit a wide range of foraging

161

traditions, including tool-use (Falótico et al., 2022, 2024; Fragaszy et al., 2004; Valença et al., 2024)

162

and show notable social tolerance in these contexts, tolerating close proximity of conspecifics.

163

Together, these patterns raise two critical, yet underexplored issues. First, the observed differences

164

in their repertoire of behavioural traditions may reflect a research bias: studies on gracile capuchins

165

focusing more on social traditions, while those on robust capuchins emphasize foraging traditions,

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.09.05.674457; this version posted September 7, 2025. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
available under a CC-BY-NC 4.0 International license.

166

highlighting the need for more comparative research across these two taxa. Second, whether social

167

tolerance in different contexts not only supports behavioural spread but also modulates its

168

innovation remains unclear.

169

The aim of this study was to investigate the relationship between social tolerance in different

170

contexts and innovative behaviour in two species of capuchins. We had no a priori prediction for

171

potential differences in social tolerance between these two species. We expected that social

172

tolerance in the co-feeding paradigm would be higher than in the extractive foraging paradigm,

173

where resources can be more easily monopolized. However, we expected that on a dyadic level,

174

tolerance between the two paradigms would correlate with each other, providing similar network

175

structures within a group. Given that brown capuchins exhibit more foraging traditions than white-

176

faced capuchins, we predicted that a larger number of brown capuchins would solve the test

177

problems and that they would be overall more likely to solve more complex problems than white-

178

faced capuchins. Lastly, we predicted a positive correlation between social tolerance and innovative

179

propensity on an individual and a group level. We expected that more tolerant individuals would be

180

more likely to solve the problem and that more tolerant groups contain more problem solvers. We

181

expected this correlation to be stronger between social tolerance during extractive foraging and

182

innovativeness than between social tolerance during co-feeding and innovativeness.

Methods

185

Study site and subjects

186

This study was conducted in white-faced and brown capuchins housed at the Primate Center of the

187

University of Strasbourg, France. Experiments were conducted between October 2023 and January

188

2024. In total, we tested three groups of white-faced capuchins (n = 23 individuals in total) and three

189

groups of brown capuchins (n = 20 individuals in total). Animals were housed either in outdoor

190

enclosures overgrown with trees and shrubs or in outdoor enclosures enriched with branches, ropes,

191

and diverse climbing structures. Both enclosure types had 24-hour access to indoor facilities that

192

were also enriched with diverse climbing structures. Animals living in outdoor enclosures were

193

housed indoors during winter. Animals had ad libitum access to water and were fed twice per day

194

with commercial primate pellets. In addition, once per week, all groups were provided with fresh

195

fruits and fresh vegetables. Data for three of the six groups (two brown- and one white-faced

196

capuchin group) were collected between October and the beginning of November during a time

197

where they still had access to the outdoor enclosures, whereas for the remaining three groups data

198

were collected between December and January inside the indoor enclosures.

Co-feeding experiment

211

To measure social tolerance, we used a co-feeding paradigm that has been used in other primate

212

studies before (e.g., de Oliveira Terceiro et al., 2021; DeTroy et al., 2021; Fichtel et al., 2018). We

213

prepared an area of 1 m2 (henceforth “co-feeding area”) per five animals in the group in which we

214

distributed apple pieces. To mark the edges of the co-feeding area, we used naturally occurring

215

stones of the enclosure or parts of the wooden enrichment within the enclosures. We used the same

216

amount of apple pieces per group as for the procedure using the food puzzles (1 apple per 5

217

individuals). We chopped the apple into small cubes so that animals would not take larger pieces into

218

their hands and leave the co-feeding area. We based this decision on our observations from the

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.09.05.674457; this version posted September 7, 2025. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made

219

weekly fruit feeding, where animals took larger pieces and ran out of sight. In our approach, animals

220

had to pick small food pieces from the ground, ensuring that feeding required them to remain inside

221

the co-feeding area. A session started once the experimenter distributed the apple within the co-

222

feeding area and ended when no individual was entering the area for five minutes or after a

223

maximum of 30 minutes. We used the five minutes of no individual entering as a proximate

224

estimation, indicating that all the food inside the co-feeding area was consumed. We recorded each

225

session with two video cameras positioned outside the co-feeding area.

Extractive foraging devices and procedure

228

We designed three versions of novel extractive foraging devices (henceforth food puzzles or puzzles)

229

requiring one to three steps to acquire the food reward (Fig. 1a-c). For the first puzzle, animals had

230

to rotate a door to either the left or right to access a hidden reward (1/24 of an apple) by reaching

231

into a box (Fig. 1a). For the second puzzle, animals had to pull on a chain reaching out of a box, which

232

moved a blockade out of the way so that they could push in a door and reach into the box (Fig. 1b).

233

For the third puzzle, animals had to pull a metal rod blocking a slider that had to be pulled upwards

234

and held in position to reach into the box and then pull on a chain to access the hidden reward (Fig.

235

1c).

236

We presented the food puzzles always in the same order to ensure that if there were carry-

237

over effects of learning from one puzzle to the next, it would be the same for all groups. Each puzzle

238

was presented for five sessions on five consecutive days, and each session lasted 24 minutes,

239

totalling 120 minutes of exposure per group per puzzle. Food puzzles were presented either in the

240

morning or afternoon before the pellet feeding to ensure that individuals were motivated to

241

participate. For the same reason, we tested animals before the weekly fruit feeding on the respective

242

days. Before testing, the experimenter gave an audio cue that was associated with food to receive

243

the attention of the individuals within the park. The puzzles were placed at the edges of the

244

enclosure so that the experimenter could take place behind them to refill the food puzzles that were

245

emptied from the other side of the fence. We prepared one apple per five adult individuals within a

246

group by cutting it into 24 equal-sized pieces. A session started when a piece of apple was placed

247

inside the puzzle and ended either after the 24 minutes were over or all food rewards were

248

consumed. We also provided two replicas of each respective puzzle per session for the two larger

249

groups (one group of white-faced and one group of brown capuchins) to reduce monopolization by a

250

single individual. Although more puzzles would have been desirable, this was not possible due to

251

logistical constraints, one of which was that the experimenter had to provide a new food item each

252

time a puzzle was emptied. We recorded all sessions using two GoPro Hero9 black, which were

253

placed outside of the enclosure.

Neophobia

256

We also measured neophobia in an independent experiment using three novel objects. We used a

257

red rubber ball with a small opening where this opening could only be explored with a single finger, a

258

larger purple plastic ball with a large opening that allowed individuals to insert their hands to explore

259

the item, and a teddy-like plush toy (Fig. S1 in the Supplementary Material). We used three objects

260

to test for repeatability of neophobia. Each group was presented with each object once for 20

261

minutes. We pseudorandomized the order in which the objects were presented. We recorded each

262

session with a camera outside of the enclosure. Objects were fixed with zip ties to the enclosure in a

263

way that animals could explore the objects and interact with them within a certain radius. However,

264

objects could not be removed, which served the purpose that interactions could always be recorded

265

and thus ensuring individual identification.

Data analyses

276

All videos were analysed with Mangold interact Version 20.10.3.0 (Table S1 in the Supplementary

277

Material). Animals could be individually identified by the experimenter in front of the enclosure and

278

the experimenter recorded the ID of each animal by naming them. From the video footage of the co-

279

feeding experiment, we extracted the total time of an animal within the co-feeding area. For the

280

extractive foraging devices, we recorded if, when, and how often an animal would approach the box

281

(at least 1m proximity to a box; henceforth food puzzle area) and how long it would stay in the food

282

puzzle area. We also recorded if, when, and how often an animal would explore the box and the

283

duration of each exploration event. Lastly, we recorded if, when, and how often an animal would

284

extract the hidden food reward. The video footage of the neophobia experiment was analysed in the

285

same way, and we recorded which animal approached the novel object and when and for how long.

Statistical analyses

291

Statistical analyses were carried out in R version 4.4.2 (R Core Team, 2024). We used the package

292

“lme4” (Bates et al., 2014) for the generalized mixed models and the package “coxme” for the mixed

293

effects Cox regression models (Therneau, 2024b). Figures were created either with the package

294

“ggplot2” (Wickham, 2016) or the packages “survival” (Therneau, 2024a) and “survminer”

295

(Kassambara et al., 2024). Social networks were created with the package “igraph” (Csárdi & Nepusz,

296

2006) and we used “vegan” (Oksanen et al., 2024) to compare network similarities. We used a

297

likelihood ratio test for all GLMMs to test whether they predict the data better than a reduced

298

model, including only random effects.

299
300

Social tolerance

301

We fitted a generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) with a binomial error distribution to examine

302

the likelihood of individuals being present in the experimental area (Model 1). The response was

303

modelled as a two-column matrix of individuals being present and individuals being absent during

304

each scan (every 15 seconds). Fixed effects included species, paradigm (co-feeding or extractive

305

foraging), and the z-transformed scan number. Random effects included random intercepts and

306

slopes for condition and z-transformed scan number within group identity, as well as random

307

intercepts and slopes for z-transformed scan number within session number nested in group identity.

308

This model allowed us to account for both temporal dynamics within sessions and repeated

309

measures across groups and conditions.

310

We calculated a simple ratio association (SRA) score for each possible dyad within each

311

group to create the social networks for each experimental condition (see van Leeuwen et al., 2021).

312

313

314

315

316

317

318

319

another. However, it cannot reveal whether networks are more similar to each other between

320

conditions. It is possible that the overall coefficient remains rather stable while the connections

Discussion

478

Social tolerance

479

Social tolerance has been shown to facilitate social learning in a variety of species. Here we

480

measured social tolerance across contexts and tested its effect on innovativeness in two species of

481

capuchin monkeys. We found that across contexts, brown capuchins were more tolerant than white-

482

faced capuchins. In addition, social tolerance was more stable across contexts, and social networks

483

showed a higher similarity in the brown than in the white faced-capuchins. We also found

484

behavioural differences between the two species when approaching, exploring, and solving a novel

485

food puzzle. Both species approached and explored the provided puzzles, but brown capuchins

486

showed a higher persistence than white-faced capuchins. Overall, brown capuchins were much more

487

successful problem-solvers. However, our proxies for social tolerance could not explain individual

488

differences in solving success.

489

Brown capuchins showed a higher social tolerance than white-faced capuchins across

490

contexts. We had no a priori prediction for species differences in social tolerance. Some studies

491

reported that brown capuchins are highly tolerant (de Waal et al., 2008) and are almost as tolerant

492

as common marmosets (Burkart & van Schaik, 2013), though less is known about tolerance levels in

493

white-faced capuchins. However, given that white-faced capuchins engage in a variety of social

494

traditions that require close proximity; for instance, eye-, nose-poking (Perry, 2011), this result is

495

surprising. White-faced capuchins have been reported to be extremely tolerant towards each other

496

during feeding contexts (Panger et al., 2002) and to allow other individuals to peer in close proximity,

497

sniff, and even touch the food of their conspecifics (Perry & Rose, 1994) One possible explanation for

498

this difference might be that white-faced capuchins are generally more arboreal than brown

499

capuchins (Fragaszy et al., 2004). Since we conducted our social tolerance experiments on the

500

ground, white-faced capuchins may have been more reluctant to participate in this experiment due

501

to their more pronounced arboreal life-style. However, white-faced capuchins have been reported to

502

be exceptionally terrestrial in the absence of predation pressure (Melis et al., 2014), and all

503

individuals in our study population were observed regularly on the ground. Therefore, arboreality

504

offers an insufficient explanation on its own.

505

Apart from species differences, social tolerance is also influenced by group composition (de

506

Oliveira Terceiro et al., 2021; Kaufhold & van Leeuwen, 2019; van Leeuwen et al., 2021, 2023) and

507

group size (Berghänel et al., 2025; Fichtel et al., 2018). However, since all groups of white-faced

508

capuchins showed lower social tolerance than the groups of brown capuchins, and group size and

509

composition are relatively similar, it is unlikely that this alone can explain the difference. For now, we

510

have no definitive explanation of why brown capuchins were more socially tolerant. The lack of

511

explanation is partly compounded by the fact that previous studies have emphasized different

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.09.05.674457; this version posted September 7, 2025. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
available under a CC-BY-NC 4.0 International license.

512

aspects of social behaviour in each species, limiting direct comparison. In particular, long-term

513

observational work on white-faced capuchins has focused on social traditions (e.g., Perry, 2011;

514

Perry et al., 2017), while studies on brown capuchins have often relied on experimental approaches

515

and lack similarly detailed social data (e.g., Burkart & van Schaik, 2013, 2013; Custance et al., 1999).

516

This observation highlights the need for comparative studies using consistent methodologies across

517

species. As predicted, in both species social tolerance was higher in the co-feeding paradigm

518

compared to the food puzzle paradigm. This difference is most likely due to the possibility of

519

dominant individuals monopolizing the food puzzles, while it was more difficult for them to

520

monopolize all pieces of the apple during the co-feeding experiment (see White et al., 2007 for

521

similar results). This conclusion is supported by the fact that, across groups, mostly the dominant

522

individuals approached and explored the food puzzles, while subordinate individuals approached

523

only when the dominant animals were absent.

524

Since both measurements were taken in a foraging context and represent a specific tolerant

525

behaviour (DeTroy et al., 2022), we predicted that levels of social tolerance would be correlated

526

across conditions. Consistent with this notion, the coefficient of variation did not differ within species

527

between conditions, suggesting that overall levels of social tolerance remained stable. However, a

528

more detailed analysis of the social network revealed that, although individuals spent a similar

529

amount of time interacting with others, the specific dyadic relationships had changed between

530

conditions. The pattern recorded in this study matches observations in other robust capuchin species

531

(S. apella nigritus), where individuals would groom the same individuals that they would spend time

532

with during co-feeding (Tiddi et al., 2011). White-faced capuchins, on the other hand, have a less

533

steep hierarchy, and patterns of association can be mixed across contexts and mostly depend on the

534

sexes within a dyad (Crofoot et al., 2011; S. Perry, 1996, 1997, 1998). Importantly, it supports the

535

idea of DeTroy and colleagues (2022) that measures of social tolerance within a population do not

536

necessarily correlate with each other across contexts. Thus, whether social networks are stable

537

across contexts seems to be influenced by species and maybe even populations. Comparable results

538

have been reported in Barbary macaques, where huddling, but not grooming or proximity networks,

539

predicted social learning in an extractive foraging paradigm (Garcia et al., 2022). It remains to be

540

tested whether other forms of tolerance (e.g., proximity networks) might show stronger correlations

541

with either the co-feeding network or the food puzzle network in the white-faced capuchin.

542

Furthermore, it is unclear whether similar patterns would emerge in brown capuchins.

543
544

Problem-solving

545

Both species approached the food puzzles an equal number of times, indicating that both species

546

were at least interested in the puzzles. Moreover, the average latency to approach the task was

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.09.05.674457; this version posted September 7, 2025. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
available under a CC-BY-NC 4.0 International license.

547

similar between the two species, suggesting that species had an equally low level of neophobia. This

548

result is supported by our independent neophobia test, where we also failed to find species

549

differences. However, visits of white-faced capuchins in the approaching area of the food puzzles

550

were much shorter than those of brown capuchins. One explanation may be that brown capuchins

551

are more used to forage on the ground than white-faced capuchins are, and are thus more relaxed.

552

However, in the enclosures animals are usually fed close to the ground. An alternative explanation is

553

that brown capuchins tolerate each other more around the food puzzles, making them stay longer

554

and allowing more individuals to stay simultaneously. In combination with a higher social tolerance,

555

simple mechanisms like social enhancement can facilitate individuals of a group to spend more time

556

around a novel object and ultimately facilitate perseverance (Fragaszy & Visalberghi, 1989; Sehner et

557

al., 2022).

558

We found a similar pattern when examining the number of exploration events per species.

559

Although there was a trend for brown capuchins to explore the food puzzles more often compared to

560

the white-faced capuchins, a larger and also significant difference was found when examining the

561

duration of the average exploration event. Brown capuchins explored much longer and started

562

exploring earlier than the white-faced capuchins. A likely explanation is that brown capuchins were

563

more motivated to interact with the devices, which could be triggered by simple social learning

564

mechanisms like social facilitation (sensu Zajonc, 1965). The proportion of animals around the food

565

puzzles was higher in brown capuchins, which may have triggered them to be more active and thus

566

show more explorative behaviours, and for longer. The idea of socially induced perseverance also

567

predicts that the success of others makes individuals more likely to approach and explore a food

568

puzzle again (Sehner et al., 2022). However, in our study, the highest number of explorative events

569

and the longest exploration duration per event were found during the second task, which showed

570

the lowest number of solvers and was proportionally the puzzle with the least extracted food items.

571

Again, simple social learning mechanisms may explain the motivation to explore the food puzzle, and

572

over time, successful individuals required fewer actions to solve a puzzle for the first and third tasks,

573

which decreased the overall number of explorative events and also the duration.

574

The largest difference between the two species was observed when it came to solving

575

success. Brown capuchins were much more likely to successfully extract a food item from a puzzle.

576

Due to their natural foraging behaviour and their propensity to use tools during extractive foraging

577

(Fragaszy et al., 2004), brown capuchins were expected to be more successful than white-faced

578

capuchins. However, we did not predict such a substantial difference between the two species. The

579

required physiological and social traits to successfully solve novel problems are present in both

580

species, since both are known to be rather tolerant during foraging and allow others to peer at their

581

behaviour (Oppenheimer, 1968; Perry & Ordonez Jiménez, 2006). In addition, both species are

bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.09.05.674457; this version posted September 7, 2025. The copyright holder for this preprint
(which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made
available under a CC-BY-NC 4.0 International license.

582

described as highly explorative (Fragaszy et al., 2004; Perry et al., 2017) and have the required motor

583

skills (Christel & Fragaszy, 2000; Torigoe, 1985). The latter is supported by the fact that at least some

584

white-faced individuals solved the problems, of which one had missing digits on both hands due to

585

injuries in the past. Moreover, although the differences in approaching and exploration duration may

586

contribute to the result, they alone cannot explain the observed pattern. The average total

587

exploration time of white-faced capuchins exceeded the average time brown capuchins spent

588

exploring before solving the task for the first time. A possible explanation is that due to an overall

589

higher tolerance in brown capuchins, the average individual could be more focused on the problem

590

at hand and spend less time being socially vigilant, which according to the collective problem-solving

591

continuum can enhance the average maximum achievable individual innovation complexity.

592

At the within-species level, however, we could only test brown capuchins on the influence of

593

social tolerance on problem-solving abilities. Eigenvector centrality as a measure of how well an

594

individual is connected socially to other individuals, also taking their connections into account, did

595

not predict individual solving success. Neither the connectivity within the co-feeding network nor

596

during the actual problem-solving sessions could explain individual success. This result is surprising

597

and contrasts with previous studies showing that more tolerant capuchins were more likely to solve a

598

novel problem (Morton et al., 2021). The values for Eigenvector centrality were based on the simple

599

ratio associations given through the time individuals spend in the area around the foraging devices or

600

within the co-feeding area. However, single individuals monopolized the foraging devices for a large

601

proportion of the sessions, meaning that individuals may be well connected within the network and

602

tolerated around the food sources, but still had little access to the actual device. In more natural

603

settings, where resources are usually more scattered, individuals may benefit from tolerant groups

604

and learn how to access certain resources. However, due to our setup, individuals may have learned

605

to solve the problem, but could not access it due to more dominant individuals monopolizing the

606

apparatuses. Overall, our results fit with the general assumption that social enhancement facilitates

607

innovation in capuchins, while restricted access inhibits innovation (Fragaszy & Visalberghi, 1989).

608

Additionally, we cannot rule out that our small sample size masks the effect of social tolerance on

609

individual innovativeness. To disentangle these effects, future studies should test single individuals of

610

different ranks as a control condition and also compare equal numbers of single individuals with

611

groups, in order to separate rank-related effects from the numerical advantages of larger groups

612

(Cantor et al., 2020; Sehner et al., 2022).

613

Ultimately, the best predictor for individual problem solving success was age, with older

614

individuals being more successful compared to younger ones. These results are in contrast to earlier

615

observations both in the wild and captivity, where younger individuals tended to invent more novel

616

behaviours regarding foraging than older ones (Morton et al., 2021; Perry et al., 2017). However,

617

captive individuals often behave differently from their wild counterparts and show differences

618

between sites due to several possible captivity effects (Clubb & Mason, 2003; de Oliveira Terceiro et

619

al., 2021; Fichtel et al., 2018; Splinter et al., 2025). For once, the cognitive load in captive animals

620

might be reduced due to a lack of predation pressure or the necessity to forage for food, and free

621

resources to explore novel items (Forss et al., 2015). However, this should be the case for both, older

622

and younger individuals. Another explanation is that animals in research facilities are tested

623

repeatedly and older individuals have more experience in these scenarios and expect a reward for

624

participating, whereas younger individuals have less experience and are also then blocked by their

625

older conspecifics. This notion is supported by the fact that older individuals also had longer

626

approaches, longer exploration durations, and shorter latencies to explore than younger individuals.

627

Ultimately, the successful older individuals in our experiment were also the most dominant

628

individuals across conditions, which explains why males were more innovative than females.

629

Lastly, we cannot rule out the possibility that innovations happened only once in each group

630

and were subsequently spread via social learning. Given their strong tendency to learn from one

631

another (Coelho et al., 2015; Custance et al., 1999; Kuroshima et al., 2008), at least some solvers

632

probably acquired the solution socially rather than independently. However, there were two stable

633

subgroups in the largest group of brown capuchins, which did not explore the food puzzles at the

634

same time, making social transmission for one of the puzzles improbable, and even and even less so

635

across all three tasks. Hence, at least some individuals invented the solution independently. A

636

network-based diffusion analysis could resolve this question and help explain to what extent social

637

learning is involved. Unfortunately, due to the small sample size, with only three groups of brown

638

capuchins available and two of them being small, it was not feasible to conduct a meaningful Order

639

of Acquisition Analysis (Franz & Nunn, 2010). Nevertheless, the pronounced difference between

640

white-faced and brown capuchins in both problem-solving success and social tolerance suggests that

641

social tolerance modulates not only the likelihood of social learning but also the propensity for

642

individual innovation.

643

In conclusion, our study adds to the body of research suggesting that social tolerance can

644

facilitate both, innovativeness and social learning and, hence, cultural evolution. Our study also

645

contributes to our understanding of why brown capuchins show a higher repertoire of foraging

646

traditions than white-faced capuchins since they show higher social tolerance in feeding and foraging

647

contexts. It raises the question of whether white-faced capuchins are more tolerant in other contexts

648

which facilitates the emergence of social traditions. Future work should adopt the approach of

649

DeTroy and colleagues (2022) to measure tolerance as a structural concept in white-faced capuchins

650

and to explore differences with other species. Furthermore, future work should also test whether

651

these capuchin species are more or less innovative within a group compared to when they are alone.

656

657
