# Ευρήματα από τις πηγές

Νέα πραγματική αναζήτηση πηγών για ορμητήρια, αγκυροβόλια και καταφύγια πειρατών και κουρσάρων στην Ανατολική Μεσόγειο, 1600–1700. Αναζήτησε σε όλες τις έντεκα οικογένειες πηγών, περιηγητές, portolans, σύγχρονα κείμενα, αρχεία και μεταγενέστερες μελέτες. Απόκτησε τα προσβάσιμα πραγματικά κείμενα. Για κάθε εύρημα δώσε ιστορικό τοπωνύμιο, σύγχρονη ταύτιση και γεωγραφική θέση μόνο όπου τεκμηριώνονται, χρονολογία, χρήστες, είδος χρήσης, ακριβή πηγή, σελίδα ή φύλλο ή αρχειακή αναφορά, ακριβές απόσπασμα, confidence και verification status. Ξεχώρισε αναφορά πηγής, ερμηνεία και υπόθεση, μεταγενέστερη μελέτη από πρωτογενές τεκμήριο και ορμητήριο από τόπο επιδρομής. Μην μετατρέπεις τη χρονολογία του ερωτήματος σε χρονολογία αχρονολόγητου γεγονότος. Δήλωσε απρόσιτα αρχεία και ανεξέταστο υλικό. Καμία αυτόματη ιστορική επαλήθευση. Κάνε νέα πραγματική απόκτηση πηγών. Μην χρησιμοποιήσεις παλιό κείμενο απάντησης. Μην εμφανίσεις εισαγωγικό placeholder ως εύρημα. Κάθε παράγραφος να περιγράφει συγκεκριμένο τόπο και χρήση που αναφέρει το παρατιθέμενο απόσπασμα.

Εντοπίστηκαν 79 αποσπάσματα εντός της ζητούμενης περιόδου. Αποδίδονται στις πηγές τους και εκκρεμεί έλεγχος.

> ABSTRACT
> The pirate phenomenon in the Aegean –and generally in the Mediterranean– is inscribed in the longue durée and formed

> historians and archaeologists. This paper attempts to respond to this challenge by focusing on the period of the extended

> collective memory of islanders and the microtoponymy of the Archipelago. These testimonies, though few in number, show
> that various monuments and architectural remains in the island complexes of the Aegean are or could be linked with the
> needs of the corsairs to defend themselves, guard their captives, heal their bodies and strive for the salvation of their souls.

> MAINTENANCE OF THE PIRATE AND CORSAIR SHIPS
> As already pointed out, pirate and corsair operations were interrupted from time to time to cater for the
> ­maintenance and supply needs of the ships. The success of the operations, after all, heavily depended on the
> speed of sailing, which could only be ensured by regular maintenance of the keel, scraping it clean from marine
> organisms and coating it with a mixture of grease and tar. These particular jobs, namely careening and graving,
> were carried out at least twice a year. Another job that took place at regular but less frequent intervals was
> caulking, namely the sealing of the hull with a tar-soaked oakum.
> In order to complete all these maintenance operations, the ship had to be disarmed in a suitable cove (Jal
> 1848, 388–89, 423, 655–56; Jurien de La Gravière 1885, 219–20; Fennis 1995, 454, 491–92, 849–50). In the case
> of pirates and corsairs, the cove in question had to fulfill a number of conditions in order to be considered
> suitable. First, it had to allow the ship to approach the shore as far as possible; then, it had to be secluded, and,
> finally, it had to provide opportunities for defense during the works, which we shall discuss below. The German
> scholar Johann Michael Wansleben, who traveled in the Ottoman territories in the 1670s, notes in one of his
> manuscripts that the corsairs graved their ships during the winter months in Astypalaia and Ios, while in June
> and July in Porto Chevalier (Fethiye or Şövalye Adası) and one other anchorage on the coast of Lycia (BNF, MS
> NAF 4193, f. 195v).1 Other sources, however, attest that the corsairs maintained their ships on Ios, Melos (­ Gazette
> 1668, 650, 660; Argenti 1954, 152) and evidently on other islands even during the summer months. I­ n­deed, a
> text containing comments on maps of the Archipelago informs us that sometimes the corsairs careened their
> ships at Arkioi (ANF, Marine, 3JJ 221, no. 4), in the wider area of which, as we have already ­mentioned, they lay

> LOOKOUTS
> Beyond the installation of cannons in the bays, where the corsairs found refuge for the maintenance of their
> ships, another practice, which may have left its traces in the landscape of the Archipelago, is the lookouts they
> placed on the highest points around the bays they used, either as ambush points or shipyards (Earle 2010, 158).
> In the 17th century, this practice is attested in Ios (Gazette 1668, 652–53; Argenti 1954, 152), in Pharmakonissi
> (de Contreras 1977, 67), and, of course, in Phournoi (Hacke 1699, 8), where even today the names of the island’s
> bays indicate that they were once the lairs of famous corsairs: Pano and Kato Maneta bays and, possibly, Pavlos
> bay (Fig. 9).
> The sentries alerted the corsairs to the appearance of ships in the distance by hoisting or waving small flags,
> thus giving orders to prepare for defensive or raiding operations. This practice should probably be associated
> with the name Bandieres (or Padieres) of the hill on the south side of the bay of Vathy, as the present-day
> ­inhabitants of Astypalaia do not have any explanation for this place name (Fig. 2). Thus, the name of the hill is
> probably not related to the relatively recent Italian occupation of the island, but to the practices of sailors of an
> earlier time (Koutzakiotis 2023, 231–32).
> Moreover, it is no coincidence that a similar place name is found in the narrow strait between Antiparos and
> Paros, where corsair ships frequently anchored. More specifically, the southernmost islet of the strait is called
> Pandieronissi and, as it is the largest of a cluster of islets and skerries, it lends its name to the entire cluster:
> Pandieronissia (Fig. 1). It must be added here that one of the Pandieronissia bears the name Preza (Skandalidis
> 2001, 185), which in the lingua franca of the time denoted the spoils of corsairs or pirates, their “prize”. “Preza”
> as a micro-toponym is also encountered today in Kimolos (Katsouros 1975, 326).

> Giorgos Koutzakiotis
> Between shortly before midnight of the 24th and early morning of the 25th of September 1679, the life of
> French corsair Hugues Creveliers, leader of the most important corsair company active in the Aegean sea
> in the 1670s, came to an inglorious end oﬀ the coast of Astypalaia, due to the blowing up of his ship by a

> portray the role that Astypalaia played in the raids of Catholic privateers against the Ottomans during the
> seventeenth century; raids which had become a regular occurrence because of the protracted VenetianOttoman wars. The privileged geographical location of the island made it an ideal base for the corsairs to
> launch their attacks against commercial ships following the routes between Alexandria and Constantinople.
> At the same time, Astypalaia was an excellent hideout for corsairs, not only because they could ﬁnd provisions there (such as bread, wine, etc.) and stock up with drinking water, but also because the island provided
> plenty of safe anchorages. The names of Astypalaia’s havens in French and Italian portolan texts, along with
> other information of the time (testimonies about corsair contracts written on the island and marriages
> between privateers and local Greek women), attest not only the strong relationship that the corsairs built
> with Astypalaia, but also the degree of appropriation of the place by them. In the last part of this study we
> examine the role of the bay of Vathy in the corsair raids, since this is most likely where the explosion
> of Creveliers’ ship took place. This particular bay has similar traits to other anchorages in the Cycladic
> islands where we know that corsairs repaired their ships, since they had built small forts or batteries at the
> narrow entrances to these lairs so that no enemy ship could approach. In-situ research conducted by the
> author on the south side of the narrow straits of Vathy bay led to the discovery of similar built structures

> THE ANNUAL CYCLE OF CATHOLIC CORSO
> Various sources of the last three decades of the 17th century inform us that the activity of Catholic corsairs in the
> Eastern Mediterranean followed an annual cycle, which was imposed by weather conditions and, ­consequently,
> by the schedule of the Ottoman fleet. The fleet of Kapudan Pasha sailed in the Aegean to collect taxes from the
> islands only when the weather was favorable, between the feast of Saint George and the feast of Saint Demetrius
> (Hidrellez and Kasim respectively in Islam). More specifically, the corsairs acted as follows:
> During the winter months, from mid-December until March or, according to another source (Deloffre and
> Rougeot 1976, 233), until the end of April, they retreated to islands of the southern Aegean, where no Ottomans resided, mainly Melos, Kimolos, Paros, Antiparos, Ios and Astypalaia (Fig. 1). In the spring, the corsairs boarded their ships and headed eastwards to Phournoi and their environs, between Ikaria, Agathonisi
> and Leipsoi, where, until the first summer months, they would ambush ships following the Asia Minor route,
> namely those that sailed through the passage between the Dodecanese and the Asia Minor coast that connected
> the ports of Syria-Palestine and Alexandria with Smyrna and Constantinople. Around the middle of July, they
> sailed along the coast of Cyprus and, as soon as they received the slightest news about the presence of Algerian
> or Ottoman vessels in Rhodes, they would head for the coasts of Alexandria and Damietta, where the shallow
> waters would prevent these vessels from approaching. Around the end of the summer, the corsairs would approach the Syrian coast, where they could disembark their ships and go ashore in feluccas, capturing people in
> and releasing them for ransom. In the autumn, they would return to the Aegean, where they would continue
> their operations until the advent of winter (ANF, A.E. BI 862, f. 75v; Magni 1679, 292–95; Hacke 1699, 8–9).

> CHURCHES
> Despite the atrocities they committed, pirates and corsairs, like their contemporaries, never forgot the divine
> ­element and manifested their religiosity in various ways, either because they had genuine metaphysical concerns
> or simply for reasons of social prestige and/or alliances with the religious authorities. The historical notes of
> the Capuchin monks of Naxos, which also narrate events from the period under consideration, record, among
> other things, donations from corsairs to the local Catholic Church, which were subsequently used by the clergy
> to build and equip temples; these included captives (to be exchanged for ransom), sums of money, precious
> vessels, textiles and quantities of metals (Zerlentis 1922).

> arms, which was mentioned earlier, bears at the bottom an inscription in Latin, which informs us that he was

> was spared from a plague epidemic, if we take into account the relevant traditions about this particular saint.

> Polyaigos, which is interrupted by two straits, and where corsair ships frequently anchored, as, much like in the
