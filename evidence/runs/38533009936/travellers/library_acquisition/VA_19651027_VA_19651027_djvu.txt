UAC Expansion 


The Edmonton-oriented board of governors of 
the University of Alberta apparently moves no faster 
than it has to when faced with expansion of facilities 
at the University of Alberta, Calgary. To the average 
observer, there appears to be a definite reluctance on 
the part of the Edmonton authorities to speed up the 
growth of UAC. 

The need for institution of health science facul¬ 
ties at the Calgary campus was emphasized recently 
when the University of Alberta at Edmonton had to 
turn away 74 potential doctors and 114 potential 
dentists because both faculties are taxed to the limit. 
Far more students than these were actually turned 
down in the two faculties, but the figures given are 
of qualified students. In addition, the faculty of 
pharmacy turned away 21 qualified applicants. 

The need for doctors and dentists in Canada is 
already great and is certainly going to increase in 
future years. Calgary has already been suggested as 
the site for a second medical school in the province 
and will now probably be approved for this faculty. 
But it is unfortunate that so many qualified students 
have to be turned down before the serjous shortage 
of medical training facilities in the province is 
brought into the open and plans for a second medical 
school considered. 

Expansion at UAC is going to be a slower-than- 
necessary process if the university officials at Ed¬ 
monton are able to call the shots. But full autonomy 
for the Calgary university is not too far away and 
should make a difference. 


Helping The Jobless 


(C. J. Harris) 

A quite encouraging report on the increasing en¬ 
rolment in special courses for the unemployed has 
just been released by the Dominion Bureau of Statis¬ 
tics. Under Program Five of the federal-provincial 
Technical and Vocational Training Agreement, train¬ 
ing is available to unemployed persons who wish to 
improve their basic education or occupational com¬ 
petence. 

In the program’s first year, 1961-1962, enrol¬ 
ments across Canada totalled 27,000. In its second 
year the total rose to 38,000. In its third year, 1963- 
1964, the total rose again to 49,000. Of last year’s 
total, the largest number of unemployed, some 16,000 
were enrolled in basic courses in such subjects as 
English, mathematics and science, designed to bring 
them to a level of development which would enable 
them to proceed with further training for specialized 
occupations. 

Of the unemployed who last year were taking 
courses designed to fit them for immediate employ¬ 
ment, nearly 10,000 were studying business practice. 
2,700 were studying welding, 1,500 were training to 
be nursing aides, 1,100 were studying merchandising 
and salesmanship, 1,100 were learning to operate 
power sewing machines, 1,000 were studying machine 
shop practice, and 1,000 were studying draughting. 

While no enrolment figures are available for the 
current academic year, it may be assumed that there 
has been another substantial increase in the number 
un e mp l oy e d taking advantage of—Program Five*. 
The federal government's budgetary estimate to cover 
its share of the cost of Program Five for this year is 
$12.6 million, considerably increased over last year’s 
spending of $9.8 million. In the previous year Ot¬ 
tawa’s share came to $5.6 million and in the first 
year of the program, 1961-62, it was $4 million. 

Also encouraging is the DBS report of an enrol¬ 
ment last year of 34,000 in full-time trade and other 
occupational courses in provincial trade schools, pri¬ 
vate trade schools and business colleges under Pro¬ 
gram Three of the federal-provincial agreement. Eli¬ 
gible under this program are persons who have left 
school and are seeking a first job, or employed per¬ 
sons wishing to up-grade their skill, or individuals 
wishing to retrain for a change of occupation. 

These programs are aimed at aiding those who 
are, or who fear they will be, unemployable. They 
can help only those who will help themselves. Fortu¬ 
nately, there are many such.
