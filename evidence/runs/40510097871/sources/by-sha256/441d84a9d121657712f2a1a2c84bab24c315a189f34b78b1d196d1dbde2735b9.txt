Abstract
This article examines Parion’s place within the epic and sacred geography of
the Troad through the relationship among civic identity, sacred memory and
public representation. It begins from the premise that the city’s identity
cannot be reduced to a single origin narrative, fixed etymology or
uninterrupted cultic continuity. Its central argument is that a distinctive
Parian repertoire of civic memory and representation can be reconstructed
from bodies of evidence belonging to different periods and carrying unequal
evidentiary weight. Colonisation and eponymic traditions, local sacred
genealogy, sacred topography, public commemoration and numismatic
imagery served different functions within this repertoire. Although Parion was
an Archaic foundation outside the Homeric narrative, its location between the
Propontis, Hellespont and Troad enabled later associations with the region’s
epic past. Strabo provides the principal textual basis for discussing Adrasteia,
Apollo Aktaios, Artemis, the reported transfer of sanctuary material and the
Altar of Hermokreon. Athenagoras’ contested Alexandros passage offers a
specific textual record of a taphos, bronze eikōn, sacrifice and festival in the
agora, while the Paris/Alexandros identification remains a defensible but nonexclusive modern reading. The Gorgoneion, Apollo Aktaios, Eros/Cupid and
Pario Conditori types document the circulation of selected images and sacred
or founding references without establishing collective belief, ritual continuity
or a single political programme. In the Roman period, local, sacred and epic
memories were rearticulated through colonial status and imperial visual
codes.
Keywords: Parion, Troad, sacred memory, Paris/Alexandros, numismatic
representation.

Genişletilmiş Özet
Parion, Propontis’in güney kıyısında, Troas ile Hellespontos arasındaki geçiş kuşağında yer alan bir kenttir. Bronz Çağı’na
bağlanan epik anlatının değil, sonraki kolonizasyon sürecinin ürünü olduğu için Homerosçu gelenekte bir odak olarak yer almaz;
ancak Troas’ın epik ve kutsal bellek alanlarına yakınlığı, bu mirasla daha geç dönemlerde ilişkilendirilmesine imkân vermiştir.
Çalışma, Parion kimliğinin tek bir köken anlatısına, sabit bir etimolojiye, kesintisiz kült aktarımına veya doğrudan bir Roma
siyasal programına indirgenemeyeceği yöntemsel öncülünden hareket eder. Merkezî savı, adlandırma, kutsal topografya,
kamusal anma, nümismatik temsil ve Roma koloni diline ilişkin farklı kanıtların Parion’a özgü bir kentsel bellek ve temsil
repertuarı içinde değerlendirilebileceğidir.
Makalenin yöntemi, farklı türdeki kanıtları aynı düzeye yerleştirmemeye dayanır. Antik tanıklıklar, geç mitografik anlatılar,
epigrafik ve nümismatik kayıtlar, modern topografik çalışmalar ve kuramsal literatür birbirini tamamlayan fakat aynı ağırlığa
sahip olmayan veri gruplarıdır. Strabon ve Athenagoras, makalenin iki temel antik tanıklık eksenini oluşturur; Frisch’in
testimonia derlemesi ise bu tanıklıkların izlenmesini sağlayan modern bir kontrol corpus’u olarak kullanılır. Bu ayrım sayesinde
antik kaynak, modern yorum, katalog verisi ve kuramsal çerçeve birbirinin yerine geçirilmez. Bu yöntem, bulgu ile yorumu
birbirinden ayırmayı da amaçlar.
Parion’un adlandırma ve yerel bellek gelenekleri, kolonizasyon bağlantıları, eponimik figürler, yerel kutsal soy ve Troas’ın
epik geçmişi arasında farklı ilişkiler kurar. Paros, Erythrai ve Miletos çevresindeki kolonizasyon gelenekleri, Parios/Iasion
eponimisi, Ophiogeneis’in yılan kökenli ve iyileştirici yerel soy belleği ve Paris/Priamos-Amandros anlatıları farklı kesinlik
düzeylerine sahiptir. Bu gelenekler tarihsel kuruluş belgeleri olarak değil, Parion’un koloni geçmişi, eponimik figür, yerel kutsal
soy ve Troas’ın epik belleğiyle ilişkilendirilmesini sağlayan farklı bellek unsurları olarak değerlendirilir.
Strabon’un Adrasteia, Apollon Aktaios, Artemis, kutsal alan malzemesinin Parion’a taşındığı yönündeki anlatı ve
Hermokreon Sunağı’na ilişkin aktarımı, Parion’un yakın kutsal topografyayla ilişkisini tartışmanın başlıca antik metinsel
dayanağıdır. Strabon daha eski ve günümüze ulaşmamış kaynaklardan yararlanmış olabilir; ancak bu pasajın kaynağı
belirlenememektedir. Pasaj, bildirilen malzeme transferi ile sunağın yeni anıtsal bağlamını tartışmaya imkân verir; kehanetin
sona erdiği bilgisi ise Adrasteia’daki ritüel işlevlerin Parion’da aynen sürdüğü yorumunu sınırlar. Ophiogeneis geleneği, Parion
çevresindeki yerel kutsal repertuarın yalnız Adrasteia-Apollon hattından ibaret olmadığını, yılan, iyileştirme, soy ve kutsal
topografya ilişkilerinin de kent belleğinde yer alabildiğini gösterir.
Athenagoras’ın Parion agorasına ilişkin tanıklığı, makalenin başlıca metinsel kayıtlarından biridir; ancak Alexandros’un
kimliği tartışmalıdır. Pasaj, agorada Alexandros’a ait bir taphos, bronz eikōn, kurban ve festival bilgisini doğrudan aktarır.
Schoedel’in Abonuteichuslu Alexandros yönündeki açıklaması ve Proteus/Peregrinus bağlamı dikkate alınırken, Corso ve KeleşYılmaz çizgisindeki Paris/Alexandros yorumu da modern literatürde savunulmuş bir okuma olarak değerlendirilir. Bu nedenle
pasaj, belirli bir yapının arkeolojik teşhisi veya tüm kurumsal yapısı belgelenmiş bir kahraman kültü olarak değil, agora içindeki
kamusal ve ritüel anmaya ilişkin özgül bir metinsel kayıt olarak kullanılır.
Gorgoneion ve boğa tipleri Parion’un erken kentsel tanınırlığını; Apollon Aktaios ve sunak betimleri Adrasteia ile
ilişkilendirilen kutsal referansların sikke dilindeki dolaşımını; Eros/Cupid tipleri sanat belleği ve yerel kült referanslarının Roma
dönemi görsel dili içinde yeniden ifade edilmesini; Pario Conditori tipi ise kurucu bellek, Parios/Parius-Paris muğlaklığı ve Roma
koloni dili arasındaki ilişkiyi belgeler. Parion’un yerel kentsel ve Roma dönemindeki koloni bağlamlarında üretilip dolaşıma
sokulan bu tipler, belirli imgeler ile kutsal veya kurucu referansların kamusal temsilini gösterir; ancak ortak inancı, ritüel
sürekliliğini veya tekil bir siyasal programı tek başlarına kanıtlamaz.
Roma dönemi, Parion kimliğini sıfırdan icat eden bir evre değil, yerel, kutsal ve epik bellek unsurlarının koloni dili ve
imparatorluk görsel kodları içinde yeniden dolaşıma girdiği tarihsel bağlamdır. Bu çerçevede Parion, epik anlatının dışında
kurulmuş bir kentte kutsal miras, yerel soy anlatıları, agora belleği, sanat ve kült referanslarının Roma dönemi temsil dili içinde
birlikte görünür olabildiği bir örnek sunar. Bu yönüyle makale, kentsel kimliğin geçmişe ilişkin unsurların mekân, anıt, ritüel ve
dolaşımdaki imgeler aracılığıyla yeniden düzenlenmesiyle şekillendiğini savunur.
Bu çerçevede makalenin katkısı, epik coğrafyanın sınırında kalan Parion’un farklı dönemlere ait bellek unsurlarının hangi
kanıt türleri aracılığıyla kamusal ve kentsel bir repertuar içinde anlam kazandığını açıklamaktır. Kolonizasyon gelenekleri, yerel
kutsal-soy anlatıları, Adrasteia ile ilişkilendirilen kutsal miras, agorada yoğunlaşan kamusal anma ve Roma dönemi sikke
imgeleri aynı tarihsel ağırlıkta değildir. Yazılı tanıklıklar, geç mitografik aktarımlar, topografik değerlendirmeler ve nümismatik
imgeler aynı tarihsel gerçekliğin doğrudan belgeleri olarak değil, Parion’un kentsel belleğini farklı düzeylerde izlemeyi sağlayan
kanıt grupları olarak kullanılır. Bu yaklaşım, kesinleştirilemeyen iddiaları sınırlandırırken kentin kimliğini mekânsal, ritüel ve
görsel pratikler üzerinden tartışılabilir kılar. Parion örneği böylece kentsel kimliğin farklı dönemlere ait bellek unsurlarının
mekân, anıt, ritüel ve dolaşımdaki imgeler içinde seçici biçimde yeniden bağlamlandırılmasıyla şekillendiğini ortaya koyar.

Introduction: Problem, Method and Sources
Parion is a harbour city on the southern Propontic coast, within the transitional belt between the Troad
and the Hellespont. Ancient and modern literature locate it variously within the Troad, Mysia, the

Hellespontine region and, in the modern inventory of poleis, on the Asian coast of the Propontis (Cook,
1973; Avram, 2004, pp. 991-992). This plurality requires Parion to be assessed at the margins between the
Troad’s epic geography and the Propontic-Hellespontine region, rather than within a single regional
category.
That Parion does not figure as a focus comparable to Ilion in the Homeric epic narrative is chronologically
to be expected: the city is not a component of the Bronze Age epic world of the Troad, but belongs instead
to the later Archaic colonisation process. The link between Parion and the epic memory of the Troad must
therefore be discussed not as a continuity of origin but through the selective relations the city established
with this heritage in later periods. From this the central question of the study arises: although Parion was not
a natural part of the epic narrative, through which naming traditions, transfers of sacred heritage, public
commemorative practices and instruments of numismatic representation can it be associated with the epic
and sacred geography of the Troad?
This study begins from the premise that Parion’s identity cannot be reduced to a single origin narrative, a
fixed Paris etymology or a linear cultic continuity. Its central argument is that a distinctive Parian repertoire
of civic memory and representation can be reconstructed from colonisation and eponymic traditions, local
sacred genealogies, sacred topography, public commemoration and circulating numismatic imagery.
Evidence for these components comes from sources of different dates and types, not all carrying equal
evidentiary weight. Miletos, too, is treated cautiously as one element within Parion’s multiple colonisation
traditions rather than as a definite founding centre (Keleş, 2011, pp. 238-239).
Strabo’s account of the cults of Adrasteia, Apollo Aktaios and Artemis, the reported transfer of sanctuary
material to Parion and the Altar of Hermokreon provides the principal ancient textual basis for discussing the
city’s relationship with its nearby sacred environment (Strab. 13.1.13 = Frisch, 1983, p. 90, no. T102a). This
passage allows discussion of the material and monumental recontextualisation of sacred heritage at Parion;
but, because it states that the oracle had ceased, it does not show that the ritual functions at Adrasteia
continued unchanged at Parion. Athenagoras’ reference to a taphos belonging to Alexandros, a bronze eikōn,
public sacrifices and a festival in the agora of Parion offers a specific textual record of public and ritual
commemoration in the city centre (Ath. Leg. 26.3-5; Athenagoras, 1972, pp. 65-66 = Frisch, 1983, p. 58, no.
T41). Yet, since the identity of Alexandros is contested, the Paris/Alexandros interpretation should be treated
not as a necessary consequence of the passage but as a defensible, though non-exclusive, reading in modern
scholarship. Frisch’s work, in turn, functions as a modern control corpus for tracing the ancient and late
antique testimonia; Frisch’s reference numbers are used only to make the attestations traceable and are not
treated as substitutes for the ancient texts themselves.
The same hierarchy applies to late mythographic narratives, numismatic evidence and theoretical
literature. Late antique and Byzantine narratives are evidence not for Parion’s Archaic foundation but for the
later transmission and reformulation of traditions linking Paris/Priamos with the city. Coins struck at Parion,
by contrast, belong to local civic or colonial contexts of production and circulation and show which images,
legends and sacred or founding references entered public representation, without directly documenting
collective belief, ritual practice or cultic continuity. Numismatic types, legends and iconographic details are
interpreted only when verified from identifiable specimens or reliable catalogues and corpora.
The theoretical framework does not substitute for the ancient sources and the numismatic data; it
provides only a limited interpretative language for explaining how different bodies of evidence may operate
together. Assmann is useful here chiefly because he treats monuments, tombs, temples and images as media
through which time and identity can be materially anchored (Assmann, 2011, pp. 7-8). Alcock brings this
question closer to archaeological space through her use of lieux de mémoire in relation to monuments,
sanctuaries, cemeteries and festivals (Alcock, 2002, p. 21). Nora is used more narrowly, as a comparative
caution that memory may concentrate in particular places and objects, not as a model of modern national
memory transferred to the ancient city (Nora, 1989, p. 7). Within this framework the expression “memory
repertoire” in the article designates the instruments of naming, sacred-topographical reference, public
commemoration and numismatic representation to which Parion could turn in different periods.

The article proceeds by this logic of evidence. The next section treats Parion’s naming, eponymic and
local sacred-genealogical traditions in the context of civic memory; the sections that follow discuss the
Adrasteia-Apollo Aktaios-Hermokreon axis, Athenagoras’ agora testimony, the numismatic data and the
Roman-period colonial language as different layers of evidence of the same model. This sequence avoids two
misleading alternatives: treating Parion as a rival epic centre to Ilion, or reducing it to a local colonial city.
Parion in Epic Geography: Naming Traditions and Civic Memory
Parion’s relationship with the epic geography of the Troad must be treated on a different plane from the
example of Ilion at the focus of the Iliad. Founded in the Archaic period, the city is not a natural part of the
epic narrative bound to the Bronze Age; its link with the memory of the Troad can therefore be discussed
not directly through a continuity of origin but through the layers of naming, descent, local sacred tradition
and epic association operating in later periods. The aim of this section is not to determine the ultimate
philological origin of the name Parion but to show what functions the relationships among the colonisation
traditions surrounding Paros/Erythrai/Miletos, the Parios/Iasion eponymy, the Ophiogeneis tradition and the
Paris/Priamos-Amandros narratives carried within civic memory.
The ancient traditions concerning the foundation of Parion do not converge around a single metropolis
narrative. Frisch records Erythrai, Thasos, Paros and a Miletos-Erythrai-Paros partnership in different forms,
while regarding the association with Paros as the more natural explanation (Frisch, 1983, pp. 59-60, no. T4347a). Keleş likewise notes Erythrai, Paros and Miletos, but stresses that the Miletos connection lacks
archaeological confirmation (Keleş, 2011, pp. 238-239). Özmen shows that modern scholars have interpreted
the roles of Paros, Erythrai, Miletos and Thasos differently (Özmen, 2024, p. 169). This plurality is better read
as a field of memory linking Parion’s past to different Aegean and Propontic connections. Malkin’s network
approach helps explain why such multiple origin narratives need not be treated as confused traditions, but
as relational ways in which poleis constructed the past (Malkin, 2011, pp. 8-9).
The second layer of naming memory is formed by the eponymous founder Parios or Parius. In the records
arranged by Frisch under T31-33, Parios/Parius explains the name Parion and is linked to the lineage of Iasion
(Frisch, 1983, p. 56, no. T31-33). These records are not documents of historical foundation, but eponymic
memory elements that bind the city’s name to a mythological genealogy. They do not identify Parios/Parius
with Paris/Alexandros; rather, they place him within the Iasion lineage. Curty’s work on legendary kinship
shows how mythological heroes or gods could provide cities with a language of relational past and legitimacy
rather than proof of historical origin (Curty, 1999, pp. 167, 184). In this sense the Parios/Iasion eponymy
functions as a memory mechanism linking Parion to a wider field of mythological descent and founder
figures.
Parion’s local sacred memory is not limited to the Parios/Iasion eponymy. Strabo’s immediately following
testimony reports that at Parion a genos called the Ophiogeneis claimed kinship with snakes and was
associated with a healing power against snakebites; the same record states that the arkhēgetēs of the genos
was recounted as a heros transformed from a snake (Strab. 13.1.14 = Frisch, 1983, p. 93, no. T107c). The
other attestations Frisch collects likewise show the Ophiogeneis as a local descent tradition around Parion to
which a snake origin and a healing power were attributed; the Aelianus tradition, for its part, transmits the
narrative of Halia’s union with a sacred snake in the grove of Artemis (Frisch, 1983, pp. 93-94, no. T107a-e).
This tradition is not directly connected with the Paris/Alexandros memory; but it points to a local memory
layer operating among the languages of descent, sacred power, healing and heros at Parion. The
Ophiogeneis thus shows that Parion’s repertoire of the past did not consist solely of colonial or epic
connections, and that local sacred-genealogical traditions too could hold a place within civic memory.
The Paris/Priamos-Amandros tradition, for its part, forms a later but striking layer in the association of
Parion with the epic geography of the Troad. In the late antique and Byzantine attestations Frisch collects
under T36-40, Priamos, on the prophecy that Paris would drive Troy to ruin, sends him to a distant territory
known by the name Amandra, Amandros or Mandro; in some variants he founds Parion there for Paris.
These narratives are not evidence of an early historical foundation; they are used here as late mythographic
transmissions preserved through Frisch’s arrangement of the Parion testimonia. The more secure
interpretation is to assess the Paris/Priamos-Amandros tradition as a mythographic transmission showing
that Parion could be associated with the Paris/Alexandros memory in the late antique and Byzantine periods

(Frisch, 1983, pp. 57-58, no. T36-40). The variants Amandra/Amandros/Mandro too are treated not as a
definite former name of Parion but as a toponymic tradition that locates the Paris narrative in the environs
of Parion.
At this point the interpretative value and the etymological evidential value of the phonetic proximity
among the names Parion, Parios, Paros and Paris are kept distinct from one another. The phonetic proximity
may have created a ground favourable for ancient and late antique authors to construct mythological
connections; but on its own it does not show that the name Parion derives from Paris. This proximity is, by
contrast, important for understanding why Parion’s naming memory could be associated with different
founder figures, genealogical narratives and epic connections. Instead of the proposition “the name Parion
comes from Paris”, it can therefore be said that the figure Paris/Alexandros is an epic element incorporated
into Parion’s later naming memory and civic-identity discourse.
The Roman-period Pario Conditori type shows that this memory of naming and founder figure continued
in the subsequent field of numismatic representation. Frisch’s record T34 transmits a youthful male head
and the legend Pario condit(ori) among the Parion attestations (Frisch, 1983, p. 56, no. T34). Since, however,
the catalogue, legend and iconographic variants of this type require numismatic verification, the detailed
assessment is taken up in the fifth section. What matters here is to note that the Parius/Paris ambiguity is
not entirely closed in the Roman-period visual idiom either, and that it adds a new visual dimension to the
discussion of foundational memory. In this section the Pario Conditori type functions not for iconographic
identification but as a bridge showing that the eponymic and epic naming memory could intersect with the
Roman colonial idiom; the consequences for interpretation that follow from defining the figure as Paris or as
Parius are taken up in the fifth section, within the framework of the problem of numismatic representation
and foundational memory.
Parion’s naming and local memory traditions therefore operate on four planes: colonisation narratives
connect the city to Aegean and Propontic networks; the Parios/Iasion eponymy frames the name through
founder-genealogy; the Ophiogeneis tradition adds a local sacred-genealogical layer; and the Paris/PriamosAmandros narrative attaches Parion to the epic memory of the Troad in later transmission. Athenagoras’
account offers a more specific textual record of public commemoration by referring to a taphos, bronze
eikōn, sacrifice and festival for Alexandros in the agora (Ath. Leg. 26.3-5 = Frisch, 1983, p. 58, no. T41).
Together, these strands outline Parion’s differentiated memory repertoire and prepare the discussion of the
Adrasteia-Apollo Aktaios-Hermokreon axis.
From Adrasteia to Parion: Apollo Aktaios, the Altar of Hermokreon and the Sacred Landscape
Parion’s relationship with the epic and sacred geography of the Troad cannot be explained solely through
naming traditions or the Paris/Alexandros memory. Adrasteia, in the city’s nearby sacred environment,
emerges through Strabo’s testimony as an important religious and spatial focus associated with Apollo
Aktaios, Artemis and an oracle within the wider Propontic cultic context surveyed by Erpehlivan (2019, pp.
39-40). In this section the term “sacred landscape” is used not in the sense of a broad landscape theory but
in the sense of a limited field of relations traceable among Adrasteia, Priapos and Parion through ancient
attestations and modern localisation proposals (Fig. 1). This usage is consistent with the polis literature that
stresses that sanctuaries could function not only as places of ritual practice but also as foci related to
communal belonging, the perception of space and territorial order. De Polignac’s approach to the function of
sanctuaries in ordering space and enabling the community to recognise itself within a particular territorial
framework provides a limited theoretical support for assessing the Adrasteia legacy to the extent that
Strabo’s testimony permits (de Polignac, 1995, pp. 19-20). This framework is not used as a substitute for the
ancient evidence; it only helps to explain why the Adrasteia material could become meaningful at Parion in
terms of civic memory.
The main source for this discussion is Strabo. He may have drawn on earlier sources now lost or
unidentified, but the source of this passage cannot be established. Strabo reports that Adrasteia lay between
Priapos and Parion and that below it there was a plain of the same name. In the same passage he speaks of
an oracle of Apollo Aktaios and Artemis in this plain; he then relates that, upon the destruction of the

sanctuary, its equipment and its stone and architectural material were transported to Parion, where an altar,
the work of Hermokreon and notable for its size and beauty, was built. The decisive interpretative limit of
the passage is that the oracle is explicitly said to have ceased, as at Zeleia (Strab. 13.1.13 = Frisch, 1983, p.
90, no. T102a). The passage therefore points not to the unchanged continuation of Adrasteia’s ritual
functions at Parion, but to the transport of sanctuary material into a new monumental context.
The movement from Adrasteia to Parion is better understood as the recontextualisation of sanctuary
material and its associated memory, rather than as a complete transfer of cult. Schreyer’s reading of Strabo
is important here: apart from Strabo there is no direct attestation of the transfer, and the continuation of
the oracular function at Parion remains uncertain (Schreyer, 2023, p. 88). Schreyer therefore shifts attention
from abstract cultic continuity to concrete objects, especially sanctuary equipment and marble or
architectural material (Schreyer, 2023, pp. 88-89). For Parion, the central point is the reinterpretation of
Adrasteia’s sacred heritage in the context of a new monumental altar whose oracular function cannot be
shown to have continued.
Strabo’s Ophiogeneis testimony reports that at Parion there was in circulation a tradition of a genos, a
local kin group, claiming kinship with snakes and associated with a healing power (Strab. 13.1.14 = Frisch,
1983, p. 93, no. T107c). Frisch’s Aelianus record, for its part, transmits the narrative of Halia’s union with a
sacred snake in the grove of Artemis as another variant of this tradition (Frisch, 1983, p. 94, no. T107e). This
evidence cannot be included in the same transfer process as the oracular precinct of Apollo Aktaios and
Artemis at Adrasteia; but it shows that around Parion the memory of Artemis, snake, healing power and local
descent too could join the city’s sacred past. The Ophiogeneis is therefore best assessed here not as the
carrier of the main discussion but as a local sacred-genealogical layer that complements the Adrasteia-Apollo
axis.
Although the precise localisation of Adrasteia is disputed, ancient attestations and modern topographical
assessments suggest that it was not a distant mythological reference for Parion. Frisch’s Strabo testimony
places Adrasteia between Priapos and Parion, near the plain of the same name; following Leaf, it has been
sought around Örtülüce, about 12 km south-east of Kemer (Leaf, 1923, pp. 77-78; Frisch, 1983, pp. 50-51,
no. T15). Körpe places the discussion between Kocagür and Örtülüce, while Özmen stresses the hot springs,
spolia and archaeological density around Örtülüce (Körpe, 2014, p. 8; Özmen, 2024, pp. 154-155). This
evidence provides a sufficient topographical basis for discussing Adrasteia’s relationship with Parion,
although the settlement’s precise position and limits remain uncertain.
In Strabo’s account, the Altar of Hermokreon is the most prominent monumental element of the
Adrasteia legacy at Parion. Keleş stresses that Strabo is the only ancient source for the altar: after the
destruction of the sanctuary at Adrasteia, its objects and architectural components were transported to
Parion, where a large altar was remembered as the work of Hermokreon (Keleş, 2018, p. 24). This suggests
that the Adrasteia heritage was not simply reused as material, but transformed into a memorable element of
Parion’s public and monumental memory. The problems Keleş identifies remain open: Hermokreon’s
identity, the altar’s form and plan, and the relation between the monument and altar depictions on Parian
coins cannot be determined securely (Keleş, 2018, pp. 24-25).
Hermokreon’s status must also remain open. Strabo names Hermokreon but gives no detail about his
identity. Keleş notes that modern sources have understood him as an architect and sculptor under Eumenes
II; this is modern interpretation, not direct Strabonian information (Keleş, 2018, p. 25). He is therefore not
defined here as architect, priest, dedicant or benefactor. What matters is that the altar entered ancient
memory through Hermokreon’s name, indicating a link between individual name, monumental production
and civic memory.
The scale and visual impact of the altar must also be treated cautiously. Strabo’s reference to its size and
beauty indicates a prestigious monumental focus rather than ordinary spolia or simple reuse. Keleş notes
modern comparisons with the Altar of Zeus at Pergamon and the Altar of Hieron II at Syracuse, but the
argument here rests not on restitution or scale, but on the transformation of the Adrasteia legacy into a
memorable civic object at Parion (Keleş, 2018, p. 24).

Strabo associates Apollo Aktaios primarily with the oracular precinct at Adrasteia; this evidence indicates
that the god formed an important point of reference in Parion’s nearby sacred environment (Strab. 13.1.13).
Ful, noting that the cult epithet “Aktaios” of Apollo has the semantic field of “situated on/by the coast”,
stresses that in the Parian context it may be the oracular aspect of Apollo rather than any clear maritime
function that comes to the fore (Ful, 2021, pp. 165-166). This interpretation is consistent with Strabo’s
association of Apollo Aktaios with the oracular hearth at Adrasteia; but the meaning of this cult epithet does
not on its own prove that the oracular function at Adrasteia continued at Parion. By contrast, Parian coins
with the Apollo Aktaios legend form a supporting body of data showing that this sacred reference could
enter the city’s visual and numismatic representational repertoire (Frisch, 1983, pp. 89-90, no. T101). This
coin evidence is noted here only with respect to the entry of the sacred reference into the public visual field;
the detailed iconographic and catalogue assessment is made in the fifth section.
The Adrasteia-Apollo Aktaios-Hermokreon axis is important less for Parion’s passive inheritance of the
sacred past in its environs than for its repositioning of that past within civic memory and monumental
representation. Strabo is the sole surviving ancient source to state that sanctuary material was transferred to
Parion and the Altar of Hermokreon constructed there; his notice that the oracle had ceased limits
interpreting this sequence as functional cultic continuity. The Ophiogeneis tradition, in the same regional
context but through a tradition distinct from the cult material at Adrasteia, is added to the city’s local sacredgenealogical memory. In this respect the section shows that Parion made the sacred and local memory
elements in its immediate environs legible again within its own civic context, and it prepares the spatialmnemonic ground for the transition to Athenagoras’ agora testimony.
Paris/Alexandros in the Agora of Parion: Taphos, Bronze Eikōn and Ritual Memory
While the Adrasteia-Apollo Aktaios-Hermokreon axis clarifies Parion’s relationship with its nearby sacred
environment, the next body of evidence directs attention to the agora. The aim here is not to prove Paris’
historical presence at Parion, but to discuss how a figure called Alexandros could be incorporated into civic
memory through public space, image and ritual. The explicit mention of the agora matters because, in the
Hellenistic and Roman periods, it remained a representational field where statues, monuments and public
events were embedded in civic life (Dickenson, 2017, pp. 42-43). In Parion, the agora provides the spatial
ground for discussing the figure’s public visibility and ritual accessibility.
Athenagoras reports that at Parion Alexandros had a taphos and a bronze eikōn in the agora, and that
public sacrifices and festivals were held for him (Ath. Leg. 26.3-5 = Frisch, 1983, p. 58, no. T41). The terms
taphos and eikōn are used here semantically, not archaeologically: the former may denote a tomb or
commemorative focus, while the latter denotes an image or representation, probably a bronze statue or
monumental image in this context. The evidence does not show whether the taphos was an actual burial, a
symbolic tomb, a cenotaph or a heroic commemorative structure, nor does it allow the eikōn to be identified
with a specific statue type. The first interpretative issue concerns Alexandros’ identity. Schoedel explains him
as Alexander of Abonouteichos, whereas the Homeric use of Alexandros for Paris, Frisch’s inclusion of T41,
Corso’s Alexander Paris reading and Keleş and Yılmaz’s discussion of the Paris monument/statue make the
Paris/Alexandros interpretation defensible, though non-exclusive (Athenagoras, 1972, pp. 65-66; Corso,
2020, pp. 356-357; Keleş & Yılmaz, 2021, pp. 382-384).
Athenagoras’ testimony offers a more specific textual account of public commemoration than the
Paris/Priamos-Amandros tradition treated in the second section. While the late antique and Byzantine
narratives associate Paris with the environs of Parion at a mythographic and toponymic level, Athenagoras
records a taphos in the agora, a bronze eikōn, and public sacrifices and festivals organised around
Alexandros.
The generic context of this testimony must be kept in view. The Legatio discusses pagan images, cult
practices and demonological explanations within a Christian apologetic framework, so it cannot be read as a
neutral archaeological report. Yet this does not remove the value of the Parion notice. Schoedel notes that
Athenagoras uses mythological and religious material for apologetic purposes, while his art-historical
information can generally be regarded as reliable (Athenagoras, 1972, pp. xix-xx). Marcovich places the

OANNES 2026 8(2)

Legatio around AD 177 (Athenagoras, 1990, p. 1). The passage therefore reflects a local public memory
recognisable enough to be used polemically in the late second century, although its route of transmission
remains uncertain.
The expression taphos lies at the centre of this local memory information. The term at first sight carries
the meaning “tomb”; but the present state of the evidence does not confirm that a physical burial belonging
to the figure of Alexandros was found at Parion. Even if the Paris/Alexandros interpretation is adopted, the
more secure reading is to assess the taphos as a symbolic tomb, cenotaph, heroic commemorative structure
or focus of public memory located in the agora. Ekroth’s study of hero cults provides no direct Parion
evidence here; but it offers a comparative ground showing that heroic commemorative practices in the
Greek world were not always limited to an actual burial. Ekroth stresses that in hero cults the tomb could be
important but that the cult focus could in some cases be constructed through a tomb, a cenotaph or other
remains and objects associated with the hero (Ekroth, 2002, pp. 337-338). Jones’s assessment of the forms
of commemoration shaped around local heroes, the arkhēgetēs and healing/protective qualities likewise
produces no direct evidence for Parion; but it helps to explain how the taphos could operate as a ritual and
monumental focus of local public memory without being reduced to an actual burial (Jones, 2010, pp. 2021).
Athenagoras’ information about sacrifice and festival shows that this memory did not remain only at the
monumental and visual level. Sacrifice and festival reveal that the figure of Alexandros was remembered
through particular public practices in the agora of Parion and made accessible to the city community. This
evidence is strong in respect of the ritual dimension; but, since no priestly institution, temenos boundary,
inscribed cult regulation, cult calendar or institutional cult personnel is transmitted, it is not sufficient to
define a fully institutional hero cult. More cautiously, if the Paris/Alexandros interpretation is adopted, the
figure of Alexandros may be understood as a public and ritual focus of heroic commemoration in the agora
of Parion. Ekroth’s assessment of sacrificial terminology likewise shows that hero-cult rituals cannot be
reduced to a uniform model; for this reason Athenagoras’ information about sacrifice is strong testimony of
ritual practice but does not determine the detailed form of the ritual (Ekroth, 2002, pp. 94-96, 337-338).
The bronze eikōn forms the visual and monumental dimension of this memory arrangement. Athenagoras
states that the image was of bronze; but he does not give its artist. The bronze eikōn at Parion and the
Alexander Paris statue Pliny attributes to Euphranor are therefore not at the same level of evidence. Pliny
speaks of Euphranor’s Alexander Paris statue and reports that this work was praised because it reflected at
once Paris’ three aspects, namely his being the judge of the goddesses, the lover of Helen and the slayer of
Achilles (Plin. HN 34.77). Palagia stresses that Pliny does not state the location of the Paris statue and that
the statue may be known only through an epigram (Palagia, 1980, p. 32). In the modern literature Corso
constructs a probabilistic connection between these two data, while Keleş and Yılmaz too discuss
Athenagoras’ information about the taphos and bronze eikōn attributed to Paris at Parion together with
Pliny’s Euphranor statue; but both assessments rest not on a definite identity constructed directly by the
ancient sources but on cautious art-historical interpretation (Corso, 2020, pp. 356-357, 360; Keleş & Yılmaz,
2021, pp. 382-384). The Euphranor connection should therefore remain a limited art-historical comparison,
not a secure identification of the bronze image at Parion. Its value lies in showing that Paris/Alexandros could
be conceived as a figure capable of complex monumental representation.
Since Athenagoras’ passage cannot be linked securely to a particular excavated structure, podium or
monumental arrangement, it should be assessed as textual evidence for commemoration and representation
in the agora rather than as the archaeological identification of a specific monument. Although the identity of
the figure remains contested, the passage shows that the elements of taphos, bronze eikōn, sacrifice and
festival were brought together in the same public context. Within the passage, this conjunction presents
Alexandros as a focus of civic memory articulated through space, image and ritual; the institutional scope of
the ritual and any archaeological correlate of the monumental arrangement nevertheless remain uncertain.
Civic Identity through the Coinage: Gorgon, Apollo Aktaios, Eros and Pario Conditori
Coins struck at Parion belong to local civic and, in the Roman period, colonial contexts of production and
public circulation, unlike the largely external literary testimonies used in this study. Their types and legends
therefore document the circulation of particular images, names and sacred or founding references in this

public medium, but cannot by themselves establish shared belief, ritual practice, definite cultic continuity or
a single political programme. The Testimonia section in Frisch’s corpus Die Inschriften von Parion is a modern
control compilation that brings together the ancient attestations concerning Parion and certain numismatic
references; for this reason it is used here not as an independent coin catalogue but as a limited reference
point that aids in tracing the relevant records. Accordingly, the analysis concentrates on the representational
functions of verified types, legends and iconographic details rather than attempting a comprehensive
catalogue of obverse and reverse variants.
In this section the hierarchy of evidence is preserved at three levels. At the first level are the types
coming from the Parion excavations or directly discussed in the Parion literature. At the second level Frisch’s
testimonia records aid in tracing the ancient and modern attestations. At the third level, standard
numismatic catalogues and databases, including BMC, SNG and RPC Online, are used only as reference tools
for typological details and variants, not as independent evidence for ritual or civic practice (Wroth, 1892;
Kraft, 1957; RPC Online, n.d.). This hierarchy makes it possible to discuss the function of the images within
Parion’s field of public representation, within the limits of the evidence, rather than deriving ritual practice
from coin imagery.
On the early Parian coins the Gorgoneion and bull iconography is a fundamental starting point for the
city’s visual recognisability. Keleş states that on Parion’s early coins, dated to around 500 BC, there is a
Gorgon head on the obverse and an incuse on the reverse; that this repertoire continued in the fifth-century
examples; and that in the fourth-century examples the Gorgoneion was paired with a bull turning its head
back (Keleş, 2011, pp. 238-240). These early issues gave Parion a recognisable civic image. Here the
Gorgoneion is assessed as a recognisable and protective image entering circulation together with the city’s
name; it is not seen as direct evidence of a particular mythological narrative.
The association of the Gorgoneion types with the Ophiogeneis tradition around Parion can be kept only at
a limited level of interpretation. Strabo’s testimony and the records Frisch compiles show the existence, near
Parion, of the Ophiogeneis tradition that claimed kinship with snakes and was associated with a healing
power (Strab. 13.1.14 = Frisch, 1983, p. 93, no. T107c). By contrast, there is no explicit ancient attestation
constructing a direct connection between this tradition and the early Gorgoneion coins. The Gorgoneion
should be assessed here not as a direct indicator of a local serpent-genealogical narrative but as the
adaptation, to Parion’s civic representational language, of a recognisable and apotropaic image in circulation
in the early numismatic repertoire. It is safer to read the Gorgoneion and bull types as establishing a strong
form of civic recognisability in Parion’s early coinage (Fig. 2a–b).
Apollo Aktaios and altar depictions make it possible to discuss Parion’s coin repertoire in the context of
the sacred heritage associated with Adrasteia. Frisch’s record T101 transmits a tetradrachm type with a head
of Demeter on the obverse and, on the reverse, Apollo depicted between a burning altar and an omphalos,
bearing the Apollo Aktaios legend (Frisch, 1983, pp. 89-90, no. T101; Fig. 2c–d). As noted above, Ful argues
that in the Parian context the epithet Aktaios may foreground the oracular rather than a clearly maritime
aspect of Apollo; the combination of omphalos and altar is among the iconographic elements supporting this
interpretation (Ful, 2021, pp. 165-166). Considered together with Strabo’s testimony concerning the oracular
precinct of Apollo Aktaios and Artemis at Adrasteia, this evidence shows that sacred references connected
with Adrasteia could be carried into Parion’s coinage repertoire. These coins, however, do not prove that the
oracular function at Adrasteia continued unchanged at Parion; they show only that the sacred reference in
question could enter the field of numismatic representation. The Apollo Aktaios types may therefore be
situated within a broader regional setting of sacred representation. This contextual reading, however, should
not be taken as evidence for a deliberate response to the iconography of Athena Ilias.
The altar depictions must be treated within the same limited framework. In the surviving ancient record,
the transport of sanctuary material from Adrasteia to Parion and the construction there of a large-scale altar
are reported in Strabo’s account alone (Strab. 13.1.13 = Frisch, 1983, p. 90, no. T102a). By contrast, it is not
secure to identify every altar depiction on Parian coins directly with the Altar of Hermokreon. Keleş notes
that in the context of the Altar of Hermokreon problems remain open such as the identity of Hermokreon,
the form and plan of the altar, and whether the altar depictions on Parian coins can be associated with this

In the Roman period the Eros/Cupid types represent a different field of memory in Parion’s coin

constructed among its artistic prestige, its local Eros memory and the Roman-period visual language. It can
be taken into account that the Cupid figure had wide associations in the Roman world with the genealogical
narrative of Venus and Aeneas; but the Deo Cupidini type at Parion should not be read as a local reflection of
the Julio-Claudian dynasty or of an Augustan-period propaganda programme. The firmer interpretation is
that Parion placed its own Eros tradition back into circulation within the Roman-period coinage repertoire.
The Pario Conditori type is the numismatic problem that requires the most careful treatment in the
section. Frisch’s record T34 transmits, on the basis of BMC Mysia no. 83, a youthful male head and the
legend Pario condit(ori) among Parion’s founder-figure attestations (Frisch, 1983, p. 56, no. T34). A Parion
excavation specimen provides a related obverse, with a left-facing youthful male head and the legend PARIO

102, Pl. No. 11/102; Fig. 3c–d). This record is important because it suggests that the Parios/Parius and
Paris/Alexandros associations were not entirely closed in the Roman-period public visual field. Nevertheless,
the present source verification is not sufficient to fix the figure definitively as Paris or as Parius. For this
reason the type can be assessed less as a claim of iconographic identification than in respect of the
relationship between foundational memory and the Roman colonial language. The Parios/Iasion tradition
opens onto local eponymic founder memory, the Paris/Alexandros association onto the epic memory of the
Troad, and the conditor language onto Roman-period founder and colonial discourse. This flexibility shows
that Parion could place its founder-figure memory into circulation among different fields of meaning without
reducing it to a single identity.
At this point the interpretative consequence of the iconographic identification is important: if the figure is
read as Paris, the type makes Parion’s late mythographic Paris/Alexandros memory visible within Romanperiod language of foundation figures; if it is read as Parius/Parios, it points instead to the re-expression of a
local eponymous foundation tradition through colonial coinage. Since the present catalogue and publication
evidence is not sufficient to identify the figure securely with either option, the type is better understood as a
representational field in which foundational memory remained open to more than one reading within
Roman colonial titulature.
Taken together, Parion’s numismatic repertoire is significant less as a series of isolated documents than
as a set of visual choices made in different periods. The Gorgoneion and bull types gave the city an early and
recognisable civic image; the Apollo Aktaios and altar types brought Adrasteia-related sacred references into
the coinage repertoire; the Eros/Cupid types rearticulated local artistic and cultic memory in a Roman visual
idiom; and the Pario Conditori type kept foundation memory open between the Parios/Parius-Paris
ambiguity and Roman colonial vocabulary. Through coinage, Parion gave durable and circulating form to

Parion between Troy and Rome: Local Memory, Empire and the Strategy of Identity
The Roman period did not erase the earlier layers of local memory traced above; it altered the idiom
through which they could be expressed. Colonial status, Latin legends, founder-figure images and imperial
visual codes provided a new field of public representation for Parion’s naming traditions, the Adrasteia
legacy, the agora memory and the numismatic repertoire. In this context the expression “colonial language”
designates not a centrally planned, integral propaganda programme but Parion’s ability, in the Roman
period, to express its status, local memory and elements of civic representation through Latin legends,
colonial abbreviations and selected images.
In the Roman world the Trojan past was associated in particular, around Aeneas, Venus, the Julii and Ilion,
with the production of founding descent, legitimacy and political image. Erskine stresses the founding role of
the Aeneas, Lavinium, Alba Longa and Romulus-Remus line in Rome’s Trojan-origin narrative, and the
symbolic weight the Trojan past acquired for Roman identity in the periods of Caesar and Augustus (Erskine,
2001, pp. 15-19, 225-245). Gruen’s assessment of Rome’s Trojan connection, before the late Republic, as a
way of positioning itself within the shared cultural world of the eastern Mediterranean on the basis of a
remote past and of expressing its cultural position, shows that the Trojan past functioned in the Roman
context not only as a genealogical but also as a cultural language of representation (Gruen, 1993, p. 49).
Zanker’s assessment of the association, in the period of Caesar and Octavian, of the images of Venus and
Aeneas with the divine and heroic ancestors of the Julian line explains the visual dimension of this ideological
ground (Zanker, 1988, pp. 33, 201-202). Spawforth’s assessment of the reorganisation of the Greek past
within imperial culture in the Augustan period likewise supports this wider context (Spawforth, 2012, pp.
107-136). Parion did not occupy the central place held by Ilion in Rome’s Troy-Aeneas imagination.
It would therefore not be correct to assess Parion and Ilion on the same plane. Ilion holds a special place
in Rome’s relationship with the Trojan past; the privileges Caesar granted Ilion clearly show this position
(Erskine, 2001, p. 19). Parion’s importance lies not in sharing this central function of Ilion but in the ability of
a city not located at the centre of the epic and sacred geography of the Troad to rearticulate its own local
memory repertoire within the Roman-period colonial idiom.
Roman imperial language cannot be explained merely as a one-directional propaganda system
transmitted from centre to periphery. Ando’s approach, according to which Roman political and visual forms
could be used by provincial societies within a shared public language expressing local status, allegiance and
belonging, provides a useful framework for understanding Parion’s representational repertoire that carried
its colonial status together with its local images (Ando, 2000, pp. 59-60, 84, 200). Price’s assessment, that
Roman power in Asia Minor could be reinterpreted by local communities within traditional religious and
public forms, strengthens this context (Price, 1984, pp. 99-100, 232-233). Within this framework the Roman
language on Parian coins should be assessed not as the disappearance of local memory but as the reexpression of local elements within Roman-period public forms of representation.
Parion’s Roman-period identity can be traced in particular through colonial status and the related
numismatic data. Keleş states that after Parion’s passage under Roman rule it gained strategic importance in
the context of the Mithridatic Wars; that the CGIP legend associated with the period of Caesar should be
understood as Colonia Gemella Iulia Pariana, and the CGIHP legend seen from the period of Hadrian onwards
as Colonia Gemella Iulia Hadriana Pariana (Keleş, 2011, p. 241). Ful too reports that in the period of Hadrian
the CGIHP legend is seen on coins and on inscribed architrave blocks recovered from the theatre; this
evidence shows that the colonial identity could be traced not only in the numismatic but also in the
epigraphic and architectural context (Ful, 2021, p. 170). More broadly, Parion’s urban development and
architectural transformation in the first and second centuries AD provide the material setting in which this
colonial language became publicly visible (Başaran & Yılmaz, 2021).
Kızılyalçın Oyarçin’s study of the Roman provincial coins found in the Parion excavations provides limited
but important data supporting the place of this colonial language among the excavation finds. According to

foundation memory in play among the local eponymous tradition, the epic association of the Troad and
Roman colonial discourse.
The relationship between the Paris/Alexandros memory and Rome’s Aeneas-centred Trojan ideology
must also be assessed within this measured framework. Aeneas holds a special place for Rome as founding
ancestor, model of pietas and legitimacy figure of the Julian line. Paris/Alexandros, by contrast, is in the
Parian context not a historical founder but an epic figure gaining meaning within the naming traditions, the
agora-centred order of commemoration and the representational repertoire. For this reason the
Paris/Alexandros memory at Parion should be thought of not as a model opposed to, or replacing, the
Aeneas ideology but as a different local reading of the Trojan past. The Roman period did not create this
reading but allowed it to enter a wider environment of representation within its colonial idiom and imperial
visual codes.
This synthesis also limits explaining Parion’s strategy of identity through a one-directional model of
“Romanisation”. The city’s public representation in the Roman period points not to the loss of local identity,
but to the re-expression of local elements through Roman status language. The sacred heritage from
Adrasteia, the Apollo Aktaios and altar images, the Eros/Cupid tradition, the public memory concerning
Paris/Alexandros and the Pario Conditori type are not evidence at the same level; but assessed together they
show that Parion could reorganise its past through different channels of representation.
In conclusion, Parion’s Roman-period identity was neither that of a central locus of Rome-Troy ideology,
as in the case of Ilion, nor that of a simple colonial identity detached from local memory. Parion’s distinctive
position lies in the ability of a city remaining outside the epic centre of the Troad to represent selectively,
within the Roman colonial language, its local sacred heritage, its epic associations, and its art and cult
references. Taken together, the Roman-period evidence indicates that Parion’s local memory was
rearticulated through colonial status and imperial visual codes.
Conclusion
This article has approached Parion’s place in the epic and sacred geography of the Troad through a
differentiated repertoire of memory and representation whose components were reused and
recontextualised in different periods. Its position outside the centre of the Homeric narrative provided a
context in which civic identity could operate through naming traditions, local sacred-genealogical narratives,
the Adrasteia legacy, agora-centred commemoration and numismatic images. The relevant bodies of
evidence do not carry equal evidentiary weight. Colonisation traditions and the Parios/Iasion eponymy
concern naming and origin memory; the Ophiogeneis tradition belongs to a local sacred-genealogical
repertoire; and the Paris/Priamos-Amandros narratives document later associations with the epic memory of
the Troad. Strabo’s account of Adrasteia, Apollo Aktaios and Artemis provides the principal textual basis for
discussing the reported transport of sanctuary material and its association with the Altar of Hermokreon at
Parion (Strab. 13.1.13 = Frisch, 1983, p. 90, no. T102a). Athenagoras’ reference to a taphos, bronze eikōn,
sacrifice and festival for Alexandros offers a specific textual record of public and ritual commemoration in
the agora, although the figure’s identity remains contested (Ath. Leg. 26.3-5 = Frisch, 1983, p. 58, no. T41).
The numismatic repertoire forms the material and visual dimension, in public circulation, of this
commemorative order. The series extending from the early Gorgoneion and bull types to the Apollo Aktaios,

political programme; but they are important in showing which figures, images and sacred-founding

The Roman period did not create this field of identity ex nihilo; rather, it provided a new representational
ground on which local, sacred and epic memory elements could be re-expressed through colonial status,
Latin legends and imperial visual codes. For this reason, Parion did not occupy the central position held by
Ilion in Rome’s relationship with the Trojan past; this, however, does not diminish the city’s importance.
Parion’s distinctiveness lies in the ability of a city remaining outside the epic centre of the Troad to relate,
within the same field of civic memory, its immediate sacred heritage, its local genealogical narratives, its
agora memory, its art and cult references, and the Roman-period colonial language.
The value of the Parion case lies in bringing uneven bodies of evidence into a single analytical frame
without flattening their differences. Ancient testimony, late mythographic transmission, local sacred
genealogy, monumental memory and coin imagery operate at different levels. Within that frame, selected
elements of Parion’s past became usable through names, genealogies, sacred heritage, public space,
monuments and circulating images. The case therefore supports a limited but methodologically important
conclusion: Parion’s civic identity took shape through the selective and recontextualised combination of
memory elements belonging to different periods and supported by evidence of unequal weight.
