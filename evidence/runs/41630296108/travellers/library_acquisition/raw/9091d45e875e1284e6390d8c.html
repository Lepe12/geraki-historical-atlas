The unspoilt Cycladic island where the only tourists are Greek mainlanders

A stone's throw from Milos, this beautiful unspoilt Greek island is determined to keep overtourism at bay
By Ali Pantony
August 17, 2026

It’s 5.30pm on the tiny Greek island of Kimolos and its cobblestone streets are deserted. Late afternoon sun filters through peach bougainvillaea, and blue-and-white Greek flag bunting – suspended between family-run delicatessens and ceramics shops – sways gently in the breeze. Here, in the sleepy hilltop village of Chorio, the pace of life is snail-slow. Owners shut up shop in the early afternoon and don’t reopen until the evening, when locals reappear from sugarcube houses for a strong cup of coffee or ice-cold drink at traditional kafeneia.

It’s all in stark contrast to other Greek islands I’ve visited. If I were in Mykonos, Santorini or the up-and-coming Paros, the town would be heaving with tourists queuing for a sunset snap or TikTok-famous beachfront bar. But despite its Cycladic charms, Kimolos is one of the few destinations in the popular island group that remains largely undiscovered. Even its neighbour Milos, just a few kilometres across the sea, has seen a sharp surge in tourism in the last couple of years. But during my three days on Kimolos, reached via a new four-hour ferry route from Athens, I haven't met anyone who isn’t staycationing from the mainland.

“Kimolos has never been aggressively marketed; its beauty is passed from friend to friend, from one Greek family to another, rather than through mass tourism,” says Panagiotis Paraskevopoulos, founder of Espera Suites, the only five-star boutique hotel on the island. “Unlike the better-known Cycladic islands, which began their transformation as international destinations in the 1960s, development on Kimolos has been deliberately restrained," he tells me.

Kimolos is protected by legislation that makes large-scale construction and overdevelopment difficult, Paraskevopoulos says, explaining why everything feels so wonderfully unhurried. "There are no traffic jams, no crowds spilling from cruise ships and no airport bringing a constant stream of arrivals. That absence is part of the magic.”

Opened in summer 2025, Espera Suites' sloping stone walls and huddled courtyards are inspired by the architecture of Kimolos’s medieval castle and its 18 suites are designed to have a traditional village feel. Mornings are spent sipping coffee on my suite’s sun-dappled terrace, followed by breakfast at Amoni restaurant, sampling omelette made with zucchini, basil and creamy anthotyro cheese, taking in the blue Aegean views towards Milos.

After breakfast, it's an easy stroll to the nearby Kalamitsi and Bonatsa beaches, where turquoise waters gently lap a golden shore backed by the twisting branches of tamarisk trees. These beaches are popular with Greek families in the summer, and children play in the sand while parents recline on loungers. But when I walk a kilometre further to the sandy stretch of Aliki, I have the beach entirely to myself.

Behind Aliki sits the terrace of Sardis, a family-run taverna founded by chef Beba and her husband Dimitris. The Sardis family have been serving traditional home-cooked food on this quiet stretch of coast since 1995, and their son Themis and his wife Anna now co-manage the business. I order a dakos salad with thick chunks of crunchy sourdough bread soaked in local olive oil and topped with juicy tomatoes, crumbled feta, capers, olives and sun-dried tomatoes, along with grilled sardines. At least on this island, life is blissfully quiet.

As the sun dips and the island slowly stirs from its afternoon slumber, it’s time to head back into Chorio, which lies 4.5 kilometres northeast of Espera. Taxis here are infrequent and sometimes costly, so the best way to get around the island is to rent a car, scooter or ATV when you arrive in Psathi, the island’s only port village. Park outside Chorio’s pedestrianised boundary and stroll the chalk-lined maze of cobbled paths and whitewashed houses, popping into boutiques for handmade jewellery, ceramics and homeware, marvelling at the 15th-century kastro (castle), or exploring the blue-domed Church of Panagia Odigitria, which dates back to the 19th century.

For dinner, venture north to Prasonisi, a traditional restaurant perched right on the water’s edge at Prassa Beach, one of the island’s loveliest. Fishermen descale their catch in the turquoise bay below, and plates of buttery sea-urchin pasta and zesty ceviche are ferried to our table as the sky turns a sunset candyfloss pink.

Like Prassa, this side of the island is also home to some of Kimolos’s best beaches. In the postcard-pretty fishing village of Goupa, fishermen have carved syrmata, or boat houses, into the soft volcanic rock. Today, their turquoise doors and saffron-yellow shutters perched precariously above the pale-grey rocks form one of Kimolos’s most distinctive stretches of coastline. Nearby is Rema, a small pebbled bay famous for its “elephantas rock”, a formation resembling an elephant dipping its trunk into the sea. Come summer, Rema also becomes an atmospheric open-air cinema, with Cine Kalisperitis screening films for free beneath the stars.

Also worth factoring into your trip is an excursion to Polyaigos, an uninhabited island just east of Kimolos whose only residents are families of wild goats from whom the island gets its name (polyaigos means “many goats” in Greek). Its untouched coastline is a succession of soaring mineral-rich cliffs, sea caves and turquoise coves, with no roads, hotels or tourist amenities. Spend the day swimming and snorkelling in the crystalline waters before tucking into lunch on board or at one of Polyagois’s deserted beaches. Tours leave from Psathi but boats can be uncomfortably full, so make sure to ask about capacity beforehand or consider hiring a private charter. You could also tie your trip into a visit to Milos, from which tours depart more frequently.

There are very few places I long to return to after leaving, but Kimolos is one of them. As Paraskevopoulos puts it: “The island’s greatest luxury is not simply beautiful beaches or crystal-clear waters, but the space to experience them without hurry.

"Come in May, June or September, and you may understand why those who visit Kimolos tend to return. The secret is getting out – but, thankfully, not too quickly.”
