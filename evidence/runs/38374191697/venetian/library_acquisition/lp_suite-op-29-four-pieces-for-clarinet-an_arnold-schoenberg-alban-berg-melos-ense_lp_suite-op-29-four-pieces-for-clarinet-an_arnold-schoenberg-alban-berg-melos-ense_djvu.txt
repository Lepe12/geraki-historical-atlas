SCHOENBERG: SUITE OP. 29 

Because of its most immediately striking 
feature, its dissonance, Schoenberg’s later 
music gives an illusion of radical innovation 
which repeated listening belies. Increased 
acquaintance with the Serenade Op. 24 (re- 
corded by the Melos Ensemble on OL/SOL 250) 
and the Suite Op. 29, both of which have in the 
past been counted among his most ‘difficult’ 
works, reveals an expansiveness and relaxed 
mood (a quality rare in Schoenberg’s music). 
combined with absolute artistic integrity, 
-which at once places them within the estab- 
lished Viennese tradition of high-quality 
light music, and demonstrates their affinity 
to the Serenades, Divertimenti, Marches and 
Dances of Haydn, Mozart and Schubert. 

In fact, freedom from tonality is the only 
radical element in Schoenberg’s style; there 
are still long, flowing melodic lines, rhythm- 
ically organised inentirely traditional manner 
(symmetrical patterns, answering phrases, 
sequence, climax, etc.), the relationship of 
melody to accompaniment survives intact 
(tunes + chords, or tunes + accompanying 
figures), the music has a regular pulse (often 
a dance measure) with occasional syncopa- 
tion, two melodies at times combine to form 
counterpoint, and the texture is continuously 
varying from extreme thinness to considerable 
complexity. Against all this, only the inter- 
vals (vertically in chords and horizontally in 


melodies) are at all different, and these are | 


a comparatively simple matter of mental 
adjustment to a higher level of dissonance. 
The instruments used in the Suite Op. 29 
comprise three distinct groups. A family of 
three clarinets — a high clarinet in E flat 
(the shrill military band instrument) or D, an 
ordinary clarinet (which can be in A, B flat 
or C, because in atonal music there is no 
predominance of either’ sharp or flat keys) 
and a bass clarinet in A or B flat (Schoenberg 
always wrote parts for transposing instru- 
ments at concert pitch) — matches the homo- 
geneity of sound of the string trio. On an 
equal footing with both groups is a piano. 
The work needs little detailed description, 
for it is far less concentrated than the Berg 
clarinet pieces and can be taken in by the 
ear. at a more familiar pace. 
OVERTURE: the melodic material passes 
freely through the clarinets and strings, the 
piano adopting a largely subservient role. 
The interval of the sixth, rising and falling, 
pervades the whole movement.

TANZSCHRITTE (dance-steps): chords of 


sixths and thirds (characteristically Viennese 


sounds!) and short scuttling semiquaver. 


phrases predominate. Half way through, the 
viola plays a tremolando passage, taken up 
immediately by the other instruments, faintly 
reminiscent of a mandoline—again a familiar 
Viennese sound, and much used by Schoen- 
berg elsewhere. 

THEME AND VARIATIONS: the theme is 
Aennchen von Tharau, by the German folk 
song collector Friedrich Silcher (1789-1860). 
The incongruously diatonic tune is first 
fragmented by wind and strings, secondly 
presented by solo piano, thirdly decorated 
by highclarinet and cello against a glittering 
background of piano figuration and string 
harmonics, and finally ‘picked out’ from 
within a highly complex texture, like an 
elaborate piece of embroidery. A Coda (the 


tune harmonised almost tonally by the piano. 


in several keys) after a quiet opening ends 
forcefully. 

GIGUE: The Gigue is in conventional tripletime 
and fugal form; cross-accents twice try to 
disrupt the basic metre. 

The work was completed in 1926. It was 
only his third work written entirely in the 
twelve-note system (after Piano Suite and 
Wind Quintet), but is in no sense an experi- 
mental work. The row, for those interested, 
is: E flat, G, F sharp, A sharp, D, B, C, A, 
G sharp, E, F, C sharp. 


IAN BENT 


ALBAN BERG: FOUR PIECES FOR CLARI- 
NET AND PIANO, OP.5 


It was during Alban Berg’s period of study 
with Schoenberg (1904-1910) that Schoenberg 
emancipated his own style from over-ripe 
romanticism and concentrated on aphoristic 
chamber works — the first Chamber Symphony, 
George Lieder, piano pieces Opp. 11 and 19. 
Characteristics of this extended tonal langu- 
age (including chords built on fourths), out- 
lined in Schoenberg’s Harmonielehre, are 
strongly present in Berg’s clarinet pieces. 
Dedicated to Schoenberg, these are dated 
Spring 1913, though the first performance 
was not until 1919. Like Schoenberg’s pieces 
of this period, they avoid melodic repetitions 
of notes and octave doublings: a striking 
anticipation of twelve-note practice. 
Although intensely compressed, they show 
traces of the four-movement sonata. 

The first piece (Massig), is unified by the 
rhythm quaver-crotchet-quaver, usually assoc- 
iated with semitones. The carefully graded 
densities of the piano writing are gradually 
increased towards the central climactic 
passage, with a low, loud clarinet trill and 
quasi -fluttertongue (an effect gained by 
rolling an ‘r’ instead of tonguing normally) 
punctuated by spread chords in the quoted 
rhythm. A rising clarinet cascade precipi- 
tates a sudden diminuendo before the static, 
ethereal three-bar coda (whose final chord 
contains ten different notes, so spaced in 
fourths as to avoid dissonant effect). 

The second piece (Sehr langsam) might 
almost be aconscious development of Schoen- 
berg’s piano piece Op.19, No.2. Each is 
nine bars long, each constructed round the 
interval of the major third in the piano left 
hand, with similar harmonic shifts at identi- 
cal stages, and in each case the final chord 
consists of superposed major thirds. In 
Berg’s case, this is a link with the opening 
augmented triad of No.III. The clarinet 
melody isclosely unified by melodic sequence 
and fourth-chords characterize the right-hand 
part. 


The third piece (Sehr rasch) is a fleeting, 


nervy scherzo, recapitulated in a compressed 
form after a five-bar-long ‘trio’ in slow % 
time with triplet movement. The dynamic 
markings range only from pppp to p. Repeated 
major thirds, fourth-chords, semitonal pro- 
gressions and fluttertonguing feature in both 
outer sections. As if to defy this symmetry, 
the clarinet descends a whole-tone scale at 


the end before echoing its previous descent 
by semitones. 
The fourth (Langsam) and longest is a 


_ ternary-form piece followed by a short fast 


section and slow coda. The descending clari- 
net semitones of the dirge-like opening, 
inverted to become major sevenths, are later 
softened to tones and minor sevenths against 
a predominantly major-seventh harmony. The 
pulse is even slower for the middle section, 


where there are progressively more notes to — 


the bar. No dynamic above pp is marked until 
the fast section, where the music is whipped 
up to a frenzy using -low trills and rising 
explosive sounds on the clarinet, and the 
bass register of the piano, which finally 
plunges to its lowest notes. The right hand 
depresses the notes C-E-G-B silently, and 
with the aid of the pedal, the harmonics of 
the low notes sing out three octaves higher — 
a device used by Schoenberg in his Op. 11, 
No.1. The. final clarinet phrase recalls the 
middle slow section.
