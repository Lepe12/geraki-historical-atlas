QUINTET FOR CLARINET AND STRINGS 


I wrote my clarinet quintet in 1932 and it had its first play- 
through at a musical evening in our house in Hampstead 
late that year with Frederick Thurston and the Kutcher 
String Quartet. Its first public performance took place 
with the same players in the Wigmore Hall in February, 
1933. 


Frederick Thurston was not only a remarkable soloist, 
but through his teaching, influenced the style of a whole 
generation of clarinettists. The personality of a great 
player has often been the incentive for me to compose a 
work. For Lionel Tertis I wrote my viola sonata, for 
Solomon my piano concerto, for Campoli my violin 
concerto, for Leon Goossens my oboe quintet, and for 
Frederick Thurston this clarinet quintet. 


The composition is in four movements, of which the 
two middle ones carry the most weight. The opening 
movement is a flow of ‘conversation’ between the five 
instruments. The clarinet starts the discussion, shortly 
afterward the viola joins in, then the ’cello, and last of all 
the two violins add their comments. The music throughout 
is euphonious and lyrical. 


The second movement is an extended scherzo, strenuous 
and dramatic. It follows a fairly traditional sonata form 
and calls for vital rhythmic playing from all the five 
instrumentalists. 


The third movement is a rhapsodical romance well 
suited to the expressive melodic powers of the clarinet, 
while the finale is all sprightly brilliance with cross rhythms 
to add to the zest. Towards the end there is a moment of 
tranquil lyricism, but the peroration soon follows with 
laughing gaiety. 


I dedicated this work to one of the most remarkable men 
I ever encountered and from whom I learnt much, Bernard 
van Dieren, composer, violinist, linguist, chemist, book- 
binder and writer. His enjoyment of the Quintet was 
praise indeed. 
QUINTET FOR OBOE AND STRINGS 
My Oboe Quintet is an earlier work than its companion. 
It owed its existence to an invitation from that great patron 
of chamber music, Mrs. Elizabeth Sprague Coolidge who,

wanted a new work for Leon Goossens to play at one of 
her International Festivals. 


The first performance took place in Venice in September, 
1927, with Leon Goossens and the Venetian String Quartet. 
I particularly recall the pleasure of the rehearsals which 
took place on the Lido and to which we went by gondola 
from our hotel in Venice. 


It is a slighter work than the clarinet quintet, consisting 
of three movements only. While the clarinettist introduces 
his quintet himself, the oboist modestly steals in after a 
string introduction. I admit to some pleasure in the exact 
timing of this entry. 


Both the first two movements are contemplative in 
character with more agitated central sections in contrast. 
In both the soloist is faced with long branching phrases 
demanding great breath control. The slow final page of 
each taxes a player to the full. 


The third movement forms a complete contrast, impelled 
forward as it is by an energetic rhythm that hardly lets up 
throughout. When I had worked half way through this 
finale I mentioned to the music critic, Edwin Evans, that 
what I was looking for at this point was a really good 
‘catchy tune’. He said, ‘Why not borrow one? I have a 
traditional one for you that I found in a book of old Irish 
dances. Have a look at Connelly’s Jig”. 


So in gratitude to him in went Connelly’s Jig, and you 
can hear each of the five instruments play a segment of the 
tune in rotation. The coda is something of a tour de force 
for the oboist. Agility, phrasing and expert ‘single 
tongueing’—all are required. 

I attended all the recording sessions for these two 
Quintets, and I should like to express in print my admiring 
gratitude to the two superlative soloists for so finely 
realising their difficult roles, and to the string players for 
their perfect ensemble playing. 


Both performances rejoice the heart of the composer. 
SIR ARTHUR BLISS 


GERVASE DE PEYER was a scholar at the Royal 
College of Music and completed his studying under 
Frederick Thurston in 1948. He has played for many of the 
London symphony orchestras and chamber orchestras and 
is at present principal clarinet in the London Symphony

Orchestra. He is well known as a soloist and has broadcast 
and performed with nearly all the major orchestras in the 
country under many international conductors. He has also 
appeared in chamber music at many Festivals including 
Edinburgh and Holland. 


PETER GRAEME—born in Hampshire in 1921. He won 
a scholarship to the Royal College of Music in 1938 but 
gave it up after a fortnight to join the London Philhar- 
monic Orchestra as second oboe to Leon Goossens under 
whom he studied. After four years in the Army he returned 
to the Royal College and later to the London Philharmonic 
Orchestra. He now freelances with many chamber or- 
chestras in London and is well known as a soloist. He has 
been a Professor at the Royal College of Music since 1949. 


EMANUEL HURWITZ was born and educated in 
England. At the age of 14 he won the Bronislaw Huberman 
scholarship for the Royal Academy of Music which was 
adjudicated by Huberman in person. In 1939 he became 
the youngest member of the London Philharmonic 
Orchestra. He is now the leader of the English Chamber 
Orchestra and is well known as a soloist. In 1954 he formed 
a String Ensemble which has gained considerable success 
playing music of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. 


IVOR McMAHON-—studied at the Royal College of 
Music with Arthur Catterall and Frederick Grinke. He 
won the first award of the Eda Kersey exhibition and also 
gained many other prizes while there. He was for many 
years a member of the Philharmonia Orchestra, and is 
now leader of the Jacques Orchestra. 


CECIL ARONOWITZ was born in South Africa and came 
to England on a Blue Riband scholarship to study with 
Rivard at the Royal College of Music. In the course of a 
few years he has become one of the leading viola players 
in this country. He is Professor of the Viola and Chamber 
Music at the Royal College of Music and is now well 
known as a soloist and member of chamber music groups. 
He is the principal viola of the English Chamber Orchestra 
and is also well known for his records and concerts with 
the Amadeus Quartet. 


TERENCE WEIL studied at the Royal Academy of 
Music, where he won numerous prizes for chamber music 
including the Sir Edward Cooper prize. He was a member 
of the Hurwitz String Quartet until it disbanded in 1951.
