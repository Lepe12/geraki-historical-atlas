1. Apams, JoHn. 1735-1826. 2nd President of the U.S. (1797-1801). Very Rare LETTER SIGNED as PRESIDENT, to the U.S. Senate, One 
page, Quarto, Washington, December 10, 1799. In full: 


“Gentlemen of the Senate 
I thank you for this address. I wish you all possible success and satisfaction in your deliberations on the means, which have a tendency to promote 
and extend our national interests and happiness - and I assure you that in all your measures, directed to those great objects, you may at all times rely with 
the highest confidence on my cordial cooperation. 
The praise of the Senate so judiciously conferred on the promptitude and zeal of the troops called to suppress the insurrection, as it falls from so high 
authority, must make a deep impression, both as a terror to the disobedient and an encouragement of such as do well. 
John Adams 
United States 
December 10th 1799.” 


In this 1799 letter, President John Adams compliments the United States Senate for its resolution praising the troops who put down the Fries 
Rebellion. The higher taxes made necessary by the defense preparations against France (the direct property taxes of 1798) angered eastern 
Pennsylvania residents. (Rumors circulated that President Adams had mortgaged the entire state of Pennsylvania and that he would flee to England 
to purchase a title.) On March 7, 1799, the rioters, led by John Fries, a country auctioneer, chased a few tax assessors out of Bucks County. 
President Adams' proclamation of March 12 declared that these acts amounted to treason. Troops swiftly routed the rioters and Fries and a number 
of his followers were captured, tried, and sentenced to death for treason. 


In reviewing the case against the rioters, Adams soon discovered that this insurrection — like the earlier Whisky Rebellion, which Fries, ironically, 
had helped suppress as a militia captain — was not a serious challenge to the survival of the government, but rather resistance by a few country 
farmers to oppressively heavy taxes. To Adams, then, the "rebellion," while a "riot, highhanded, aggravated, daring and dangerous indeed,” was 
certainly not "treason". When Fries and his followers appealed to the president, Adams wrestled with his final decision before he pardoned them 
in the spring of 1800. This action only served to cost Adams support from the conservative wing of his own Federalist Party, at a ttme when he 
was beleaguered by attacks from the opposition Jeffersonian Republicans and possible war with both England and France. In mint condition.

According to Moses King’s Hand-Book of Boston: Profusely Illustrated

1810. It was named for Ward Nicholas Boylston, John Adams’ second 
cousin and a benefactor of Harvard College. Over the market is the 
main building called Boylston Hall, where several churches were 
organized, as well as a variety of theatrical ventures. Boylston Hall was 
leased for several years to the Handel and Haydn Society, and had also 
been used by the public schools for drill purposes. The building and 
associated complex was owned by the Boylston Market Association, 
of which John Quincy Adams was the first president. The land was 
bought at 75 cents a foot, and the building cost $20,000.

15.  Bowpoin, James. 17206-1790. 
merchant and governor. Historic LETTER SIGNED as GOVERNOR OF 
MassacuusetTTs, One page, Folio, dated March 30, 1786 from Boston. 
Written to Richard Carwell, then serving as Governor of North 
Carolina. In full: 


Revolutionary statesman, 


“Pursuant to the request of the Legislature of this Commonwealth, I 
have the honour to inform your Excellency, that they have acceded to 
the proposal of the Assembly of Virginia, for holding at Annapolis in 
Maryland, a Convention of Delegates from the several States in the 
Union, for the purpose of considering the Trade of the United States, 
and their Resolve upon the Subject is enclosed.” Signed at the 


lower right, “James Bowdoin” in bold dark ink. 


Under the Articles of Confederation, the thirteen states operated more 
or less independently of one another—the "United States" were then 
considered a rather loose plural group of mini-nations instead of a 
single national entity. The Articles did not give enough power to the 
central government: there was no chief executive to enforce the laws; 
the Congress had no power to make the states pay taxes, nor could it 
control trade or prevent the erection of internal tariff barriers between 
the states; and no mechanism existed for settling disputes among the 
states. 


Page 9 


The Annapolis Convention was organized by James Madison, who 
had been a key figure in Virginia's call for a convention of states to 
discuss uniform regulation of interstate commerce; this issue provided 
the pretext for reviewing the entire Articles of Confederation. In 
Annapolis, Madison was joined by eleven other representatives from 


(The 


other eight states were not represented, despite Massachusetts having 


Virginia, Pennsylvania, Delaware, New Jersey, and New York. 


authorized a delegate per this letter.) The absence of so many states and 
the conflicting instructions given to the delegates prevented the meeting 
from accomplishing its stated purpose. But the strong nationalism of 
these dozen men compelled them to recommend an official convention 
to revise the Articles in a report, written by Hamilton, to the individual 
state legislatures and Congress. Congress endorsed the call for a 
convention to meet in Philadelphia on the second Monday in May of 
1787; meeting in secret, this convention would quickly vote to discard 


the Articles and draft a new Constitution in its stead. 


At the time of this letter, the United States was at a perilous crossroads, 
as it appeared that the individual states would go their separate ways. 
Before there could be a “Constitutional Convention” in which a 
stronger federal government was created, each of the thirteen state 
legislatures had to be convinced to send delegates—not an easy task in 
the post-colonial environment. Some of the southern agrarian states

The Western & Atlantic Railroad, owned by the state of Georgia, was

about 28 complete circles. In his annual report of Sept. 30, 1860, the

the sun", a colorful title that it still enjoys. 


During the Civil War, the W&A RR was well used, shuttling 
provisions and troops between Georgia and the western theater. It also 
has a rich history. The year prior to this letter, it was the railroad on 
which James Andrews and his raiders hijacked the locomotive “The 
General” to begin the famous “Great Locomotive Chase”; in 1864, 
the railroad was essentially the route of The Atlanta Campaign all the 
way from the mountains of Tennessee to the city center. [After the 
war, Union General William T. Sherman wrote: "The W&A RR of 
Georgia should be the pride of every true American because by reason 
of its existence the Union was saved. Every foot of it should be sacred 
ground, because it was once moistened with patriotic blood. Over 
a hundred miles of it was fought in a continuous battle of 120 days, 
during which, night and day, were heard the continuous boom of 
cannon and the sharp crack of the rifle."]

Although Washington officials wanted Lincoln buried in the Capitol, a Springfield delegation persuaded Mrs. Lincoln to choose their Illinois

Secretary of War Edwin M. Stanton, who had himself narrowly escaped death in the Lincoln conspirators’ complex plot, arranged the itinerary of

On Tuesday, April 18, 1865 Lincoln’s body, clad in his Second Inauguration suit, was laid in a $1500 open casket in a catafalque in the East Room

Lincoln’s request to be buried with his father’s coffin in Springfield. 


The funeral train arrived at Cleveland on April 29 at 7:00 am, where it remained until midnight, bound for the next stop at Columbus. Practically 
every town along the route from Washington to Springfield prepared a funeral arch, tolled bells, fired salutes, and turned out large delegations of 
mourners to watch the train pass (at less than 5 miles per hour). Altogether the train traveled 1662 miles and arrived only 1 hour late in Springfield 
on May 3, the day after the present letter was written.

placed in a temporary vault, and Methodist Bishop Matthew Simpson delivered the funeral oration. The dramatic, 16-day long funeral was a

Fantastic Political-Content Autograph Letter Signed, Pour pages, Quarto, dated July 12, 1837 from

of the abolition of imprisonment for debt (resulting in the Stilwell Act of 1831), and his connection with 
the reformation of the U.S. banking system. In fact, Stilwell broke with Jackson and the democrats after 
Jackson withdrew government deposits from the Bank of the U.S. in 1833. He reportedly refused an offer

Harrison pens (in part): 


“My dear Sir...I had noticed the movements made in favor of Mr. Webster in New York & had witnessed (indeed contributed to them as far as was in 
my power) the honors paid to him in the Western Country, without any feeling of jealousy, being willing as I ever have been to yield my claims to him 
or any other person on whom those in opposition to the principles of the administration could unite. I must confess however that I do not think that I 
have been very fairly treated. During the worst winter (up indeed to the adjournment of congress) information was received from various sources that it 
was agreed upon by the leading men of the nation assembled at Washington that I was to be the only candidate. But no sooner did the measures of the 
late administration begin to operate with baleful influence on the business of the country and the present incumbent of the presidency his determination 
to adhere to the measures, than the friends of Mr. Clay & Mr. Webster began to entertain the hope that the influence of Jacksonism would so rapidly 
decline & the Whigs do become decidedly dominant that the elevation of either of these gentlemen to the Presidential chair would be certain if either could 
receive the nomination of a respectable portion of the party & that under the influence of such a nomination the partialities of the people for another would 
be given up. To this effort in their behalf I have no objection. Let the attempt be made & if it should succeed there is no one who will more strenuously 
exert himself for the consumation of their wishes than I will. It is however, I think necessary that the ground upon which these are expected to rest should 
be well reconnoitered. Confidence in the weakness of our enemy has produced the most disastrous defeats on record. There are unquestionably seeds of 
disaffection & symptoms of mutiny in the ranks of our enemy. It must be recollected however that their discipline is perfect, & their resources immense & 
their experience in the use of them, is transparent...To give a victory with a leader who did not possess the talents or the disposition to make the proper 
use of it would be bad indeed, but I do not know that defeat would be any better. Some of the chivalrous friends of Mr. Webster think otherwise. Defeat 
with an able leader is with them glory enough. But in the kind of battle that we are to fight, glory should be out of our calculation. That kind of glory 
which these gentlemen speak of dying hard. Ours is a civil war. The glory to be gained is not for the victory we may achieve but from the use we make

The arguments for a Whig national convention in the upcoming election of 1840 were debated rather strenuously in the inter-electoral years. 
Being a young party, the Whigs they did not hold a national convention in 1836, opting instead for a contingent of four regional nominees in 
hopes of drawing enough support away from the Democrats to throw the election to the House. In contrast, the highly organized and motivated 
Democrats of Andrew Jackson brought a wave of delegates “fresh from the people” to their national convention, and spoke with one voice for 
VP Martin Van Buren as their nominee. It was a crucial distinction in strategy between the parties, and led to a Whig defeat by a popular vote of 
704,198 to 549,508, and by an electoral vote of 170 (15 states) to 73 (seven states). 


‘Within months of the election, William Henry Harrison renewed his candidacy - an action meant to prevent Daniel Webster, Henry Clay or even

months after the date of this letter — he officially reopened his campaign. However, it was clear after the fall gubernatorial elections that the Whigs 
had an uphill battle; the Whigs lost eight Congressmen due to the splitting up of Harrison and Clay strength - and there were dramatic gains by 
the Democrats in Maryland, Pennsylvania and New York City (despite Harrison’s contacts there). The Jackson-Van Buren organization was not 
to be easily overthrown, as Harrison so well understands in this letter. 


Having learned their lesson, and with three more years of political experience, the Whigs held their first national convention in “William Henry

56. JEFFERSON, THoMas. AUTOGRAPH LETTER SIGNED, One page, Quarto, dated October 7, 1823 from Monticello. Written to Capt. James 
Oldham, on the verso of the address leaf of his original letter to Jefferson, with Jefferson’s identifying docket, “Oldham James Oct.7.23.” Jefferson 
pens (in full): 


“Sir 


I received yesterday, during the session of the Visitors of the University, and laid before them your two memorials addressed to them, the one on your 
participation in the future work to be done on the Rotunda, & 2d. on the difference between yourself and the Proctor in the settlement of your accounts. 

As to the 1°., their answer is the fact that they do not propose that any further work on that building shall be engaged, until the part already 
engaged be finished, when their further proceedings will be determined by the state in which they shall find their funds then to be, but in any case, the 
letting the works to Undertakers is not within their functions. 

As to the 2d., they observe that the law has established the Proctor as the Executive agent of the institution; that within his duties lie those of 
making all necessary contracts, attending to the execution of those contracts and finally settling them in the way he thinks it his duty to do: & if he refuses 
to do justice to those who have contracted with him, the act establishing his office has laid the cou|rts] of law open to the person injured, (as to all other 
persons suffering injustice) by making him liable to their suit in ordinary courts. It is not within the duties prescribed by law to the board of Visitors to take 
on themselves the office of the courts of justice, nor would it be compatible with their plan of proceedings, to receive all appeals which might be made from the 
Proctor, & to go into the course of fending and proving between the parties, which that might require. This would more especially be a waste of their time, 
as their decision would nothing bind the complaining party, to whom the courts of justice would still be open, and thus give him two chances of a favorable 
decision instead of one, and would occasion all appeals to be first made to them. I return you your papers and assure you of my best wishes & respects. 


Th: Jefferson” 


‘When Jefferson was in Paris as U.S. foreign minister from 1784 to 1789, he used every spare moment to study contemporary French architecture. 
At that time all of Europe was going to France to study the arts, and this movement might be called the first modern school of Paris, when aesthetics 
was uppermost in the minds of the Parisians. There was a series of great architects who were making experiments in large social complexes, who 
were concerned with the richness and variety of the ancient world, and who, finally believed in the natural order of things. As geometry was, to 
them, one of the natural sciences, pure geometric shapes formed a large part of the theories of these men. Jefferson went out of his way to know 
the leading architects, some of whom he invited to dine at the beautiful American embassy on the Ronde Point on the Champs Elysees. When 
he came home, therefore, he was thoroughly trained as an architect through his vast study of European architectural and building styles. It was 
he who introduced French visionary architecture to North America, and today the Rotunda — embodying in its finished form all the ideas of that 
group — stands as the supreme example of that style in this country. 


Being Father of the University of Virginia was the occupation Jefferson chose for his final years. At the age of seventy-four, when most men today 
would be long retired, Jefferson embarked upon the great project of his old age. He not only laid out the curriculum, which was extremely advanced 
for his day, but he also selected all the books for the library, selected the professors, designed all the buildings, supervised their construction, and 
perhaps most important of all, coaxed a large sum of money, about a quarter of a million dollars, from a parsimonious and indifferent legislature. He 
new that, once the university had opened, the politicians probably would never grant the final $55,000 needed to build the Rotunda, so he would 
not permit the university to open, even though all the other buildings were completed, until he had received the money for that building. 


This splendid building was begun in 1823 and was completed the year Jefferson died, in 1826. It is said that in April of that year he spent several 
hours sitting in the Dome Room of the Rotunda, looking down the Lawn toward the vista of distant mountains, watching one of the final capitals 
being put in place and supervising some details of the bookcases being completed. When he left that afternoon for Monticello, he was never to 
return, but he had the satisfaction of knowing that his beloved university had opened in March of the preceding year and that with the completion 
of the Rotunda, he need have no more fears for its future. [Thomas Jefferson’s Rotunda Restored, (Charlottesville: UVA Press, 1981), by Joseph 
Lee Vaughan and Omer Allan Gianniny, Jr.] 


This letter precipitated the first lawsuit against the University of Virginia. Jefferson’s correspondent, James Oldham, had worked for Jefferson on 
the rebuilding of Monticello. Oldham was a fine craftsman, and in 1819, Jefferson hired him to build one of the pavilions at the new University of 
Virginia. The university agreed to pay its carpenters the prices printed in one of the standard price manuals of the day, the Philadelphia Price Book. 
The first two years of work went smoothly, but by the time Oldham was awarded a second contract, the school, finding itself in financial straits, 
had reduced the prices it would pay by ten percent. Oldham filed suit, but the outcome was anything but expedient. A decade after the quarrel 
began, and five years after Jefferson’s death on July 4, 1826, a judge finally awarded the contentious craftsman $2048 plus interest. Oldham later 
became a tavern keeper in Albemarle County, Virginia; he died in the 1840s. 


An exceptional letter mentioning the future work on the Rotunda, the crowning architectural glory of Jefferson’s beloved University of Virginia.

a war, the Cold War.”

Throughout adolescence and early adulthood, Abraham Lincoln worked at various jobs and even served briefly as a captain of militia in the Black 
Hawk War of 1832 (though he saw no battle). As a young man, he settled at New Salem, near Springfield, Illinois. In his spare time he studied 
law, and he was admitted to the bar in 1836. 


Lincoln practiced law for nearly twenty-five years in the Illinois courts. Other than part-time service in the Illinois legislature and the United 
States Congress, law was his full-time occupation. Lincoln handled cases in almost all court levels: justice of the peace, county, circuit, appellate, 
and federal. He conducted a successful law practice in partnership with John T. Stuart (1837-41), Stephen T. Logan (1841-44) and William H. 
Herndon (from December, 1844), as well as Clifton Moore (after April, 1849). Like many of his colleagues at the bar, Lincoln was a general 
practice attorney and represented clients in a variety of civil and criminal actions including debt, slander, divorce, dower and partition, mortgage 


foreclosure, and murder. 


Politics soon called him out of the legal profession, and he served for one term as [llinois' only Whig in the House of Representatives (1847-49). 
After Whig candidate Zachary Taylor was elected President in 1848, Lincoln, expecting to be given the post of Commissioner of the General Land 
Office, failed to get the position, and retired from politics for five years, returning to his law practice in Springfield, having declined appointments 
as secretary and governor of the Oregon Territory in 1849. In 1856, Lincoln joined the newly formed Republican Party, composed of a coalition 
of Whigs, Pree-Soilers, and anti-slavery Democrats. [Papers of Abraham Lincoln]

In a curt response to his political enemy Sen. Samuel Smith, James Madison rejects the charge that the

73. Mapison, JAMES. AUTOGRAPH LETTER SIGNED AS PRESIDENT, Two pages, Quarto, dated August 29, 1810 from Montpelier. Written to 
Senator Samuel Smith (1752-1839), a veteran of the Revolutionary War who rose to the rank of general in command of Maryland’s contingent 
of troops called out to suppress the Whiskey Rebellion. During the previous session of Congress, Smith had served as President pro tempore of the 
Senate. At the time of this letter, he was one of Madison’s most ardent political arch-enemies; his brother, Robert Smith, was then Secretary of 
State, and freely revealed confidential information to the Senator, which Smith used to undermine the Administration. Both of them had fought 
to compel the removal of Albert Gallatin as Secretary of the Treasury. Madison pens (in full): 


“Dear Sir 

Your letter of the 8th inst. came duly to hand, & I have since been favor'd with that of the 17th. 

It seems that in the decreasing amount of the Treasury deposits, any distributive transfer of them to the State Banks, would not be 
convenient to the public and must soon become unimportant to them. Nothing better therefore is practicable in that mode, than a temporary relief 
to particular Banks, particularly affected by the demands of the national Bank. Application has been made on this ground in behalf of the Bank of 
Columbia, and equal attention would be due to any other applications. 

The Spaniard who dined with me last winter, & was lately executed at the Havanna was not the one introduced by Dr. Thornton. 
His particular title to my civilities, was a letter of strong recommendation from Doctor Rush. The other Spaniard was never at my table. 

If the French Decree releasing the American property in the warehouses of Holland, be authentic may it not be the result of apprehended 
reprisals here? 

Be pleased to accept my respects & good wishes. 
James Madison” 


As chairman of the Senate Committee on Banking and Commerce, Samuel Smith had taken up the question of the re-charter of the Bank of the 
US., introduced into Congress during the current session by Treasury Secretary Albert Gallatin. In reviewing the bill, Smith had come up with 
some alterations to repair what he felt to be some serious faults. As a businessman he recognized the fact that the Bank had a stabilizing effect on 
the nation’s financial system. However, it was Gallatin’s monetary conservatism, reflected in the Bank’s policy, that disturbed Smith. The large 
number of foreign stockholders, the limited note circulation, and the tight credit policies did little to furnish the kind of financial energy to the 
business community which had been one of the most persuasive of Alexander Hamilton’s argument when he proposed the original Bank charter. 
Smith also believed that the Bank’s directors were not above a little partisan politics designed to promote Federalist interests. Most important, he 
felt that the Bank had not expanded its capital or its services in order to meet the expanding needs of the nation’s economy. Smith had therefore 
recommended in 1810 a re-charter which he felt would overcome some of these deficiencies. The Senate could not reach an agreement, and 
before the current session of Congress adjourned, it was decided to extend the charter until 1812. However, Smith’s suspicion of the Bank 
remained. 


During the summer of 1810, Smith thought he detected more of the kind of pressure which he had criticized. He accused the branch bank 
in Baltimore of withdrawing funds from state banks and tightening credit among merchants. He warned the President that any appearance of 
political meddling by the Bank would lead members of Congress to “believe that the Secretary of the Treasury was thoroughly favoring the 
institution.” In the August 8th letter (to which Madison is here answering), Smith bordered on language that could quite literally be construed 
as threatening: “...if [my] course should not be pursued...there will be members of Congress who will attribute it to improper motives, and 
who will believe that the Secretary of the Treasury was thereby favoring the institution. We certainly had better have no such institution, if the 
consequence is, that it can awe the Government at it’s pleasure...” 


Madison’s reply in the present letter is to answer the charges in the most even-tempered, yet cordial manner possible in defense of his embattled 
Treasury Secretary. The confrontation between Gallatin and Secretary of State Robert Smith culminated just seven months later, when Gallatin 
offered Madison his resignation stating that he could no longer be useful in the Cabinet “under existing circumstances”. Gallatin had essentially 
offered a “him or me” ultimatum. As Madison could not afford to lose the sole member of his Cabinet on whom he relied so heavily for counsel, 
the Treasury Secretary’s resignation was not accepted. However, the stage was set. Within two months, Secretary of State Robert Smith was out, 
and James Monroe was brought in as his replacement with much fanfare. 


A fantastic letter from Madison, ripe with veiled animosity, though thoroughly cordial as was Madison’s personal style. Encompassing virtually 
every important international and domestic issue of the day, it perfectly captures the intense political battle between the President and his chief 


political rival in the Senate, Samuel Smith.

Complicating matters further was the increasingly radical nature of the 
revolution occurring within France. While many leading Democratic 
Republicans, including Thomas Jefferson and James Monroe, embraced 
the French Revolution, the Federalist administration was horrified by 
the excesses committed against the traditional social and political order, 
which culminated in the executions of the benefactors of American 
independence, the king and queen. Thus existing domestic political 
tensions were played out in the arena of foreign policy, between 
Anglophile Federalists and Francophile Republicans. 


Essentially the last truly national figure available for the post, Monroe 
was not a good choice to replace Gouverneur Morris as Minister to 
France. Although favored by his Republican colleagues in the Senate, 
he was not President Washington's first choice, as his personal fondness 
for France was well known. This bias would surface disastrously 
upon his arrival in Paris in August, 1794, when he made overtures to 
his French counterparts for an official reception on the floor of the 
Republican Convention during which he gave a widely publicized 
speech that contradicted the official American position of neutrality. It 
could not have occurred at a worse time since John Jay had just been 
appointed Minister to England, with the secret object of securing a 
treaty of “amity and commerce” between the United States and its 
former overlord. Monroe’s intemperate statements lavishing praise on 
the French government endangered Jay’s mission, and won for Monroe 
an official reprimand from the Secretary of State. 


Writing soon after his arrival as the United States minister in Paris, 
Monroe offers a revealing statement of his — and the Jeffersonian 
Republicans’ — prolonged love affair with France. Apparently 
addressed to J. Hector St. John de Crevécoeur, the letter praises that 
author’s Letters From an American Farmer, a series of commentaries on the 
character of the American people and nation, written in epistolary form, 
that in many ways anticipates de Tocqueville’s Democracy in America. 


Sadly for Monroe, while he enjoyed his tenure in France his 
accomplishments there were few. Upon the ratification of Jay’s Treaty 
with the British two years later, Monroe’s failure to emphatically 
support the agreement from his post in Paris compelled Washington to 
order his recall. 


An excellent and timely letter from Monroe, echoing the recent tenor 
of his mission to France — a post that almost ended his political career.

The campaign of 1844 was conducted primarily in the press, with 
both parties leveling charges of mud-slinging against each other, while 
themselves orchestrating character assassinations of the opposition. 
Polk biographer Charles Sellers writes in James K. Polk, Continentalist

of North Carolina an elaborate Vindication in pamphlet form, 
10,000 copies of which were printed and distributed; and in the 
end Polk drove his Nashville editor Sam Laughlin almost to despair 
by his endless demands for further publications in vindication of 
his ancestor. 


In the end, Polk narrowly defeated Clay in the election, due primarily 
to the showing of third-party candidate James G. Birney in New York, 
who siphoned off enough votes to toss the election to Polk.

A victim of paralytic poliomyelitis, FDR lost the use of his legs in 1924 from 
the effects of this crippling disease. Around 1926, the future president 
heard of another polio victim who had taught himself to use his legs in 
water, and found that he could walk on land. Roosevelt, who had been 
an athletic young man, was ready for a miracle himself and journeyed 
to the Merriweather Inn in Warm Springs, Georgia, which had a warm 
water pool. He moved into a cottage and immediately began a rigorous 
routine of exercises in the pool; soon he was able to move his right leg, a 
feat he had not conquered in over three years. News of his visit attracted 
the press, and when Roosevelt returned to Warm Springs a few months 
later, he found another six polio victims there for the “cure”. 


Soon, Roosevelt became so involved with the Inn at Warm Springs that 
he decided to buy the facility outright and turn it into a rehabilitation 
center for other “parals” seeking treatment. The sale was completed 
in 1926, and a year later, with the help of his former law partner Basil 
O’Connor, he established the nonprofit Warm Springs Foundation 
— the country’s first organized effort to fight polio.

of funds, started to appeal to the general public. Initially, funds were 
raised through the annual “President's Birthday balls”, with participation 
of FDR and a variety of celebrities. In January 1938 the National

the nationwide significance and non-partisan character of the polio 
crusade. Comedian Eddie Cantor coined the phrase “March of Dimes”

Roosevelt, after a lengthy battle with Congress over the Meat Inspection 
and Pure Food and Drug Acts, arrived in Oyster Bay on June 31st for 
what he hoped would be a long holiday in the country. Each of these 
landmark laws was prompted by the publication of Upton Sinclair’s 
The Jungle, which exposed unsanitary conditions in the meatpacking 
industry. Sinclair’s lurid account of Chicago’s packing houses revealed 
in revolting detail the conditions in which beef and pork were prepared 
for America’s tables. Canned beef was made from old, diseased cattle, 
and sausage contained rats and rat dung. Potted chicken contained no 
chicken at all, only waste cattle products. Men fell into the processing 
vats and “‘all but the bones [went] out in to the world as Durham’s Pure 
Leaf Lard!” President Roosevelt was one of the most outraged readers 
of The Jungle, which paralleled with his own experience during the war 
with “embalmed beef” issued to the Rough Riders. 


Roosevelt teamed up with Secretary of Agriculture James Wilson 
in ordering an immediate inspection of the meat packing plants, 
and legislation was introduced in Congress which would bring 
the industry under tighter Federal control. However, the ultra 
Conservatives in Congress, led by House Speaker Joe Cannon, were 
vehemently opposed to the bills. After a vitriolic debate and much 
posturing between the White House and the Republican leadership, 
a compromise was reached between Roosevelt and Congress, and the 
bill was signed into law on June 30, 1906. One day later, TR arrived 
by train in Oyster Bay to enjoy a well-earned vacation.

Interestingly, Boone was forced to move from Kentucky just three years after the date of this survey, as his extensive land holdings in the area 
were coming under dispute. He lost nearly all of his land in Fayette County (even though the county seat, “Boonesborough”, had been named 
after him), and took up residence in 1788 near the Kanawha River in what is now West Virginia. In 1799, he moved even further west to the 


Spanish-held Missouri territory and was formally granted a parcel of land by Congress. 


Boone’s legend is somewhat larger than life; he became an internationally-known celebrity after being immortalized in one of Lord Byron’s poems 
(Don Juan, 1823), and is popularly remembered as one of the greatest American frontiersmen this country ever produced. Although certainly not 
the “First White Man in the West” (which he was alleged to have been), as one of the initial pioneers west of the Alleghenies he inspired the 


massive migration westward during the 19" century, and became a symbol of American spirit and resolve. 


A great association between Boone and the state with which he is most identified — Kentucky. Minor age toning and chipping to lower portion

Note is in reply to ours received, notifying us of the establishment of

The cordon of blockading ships which it is intended to maintain is,

infer that the commanders of His Majesty’s vessels of war engaged in 
the blockade will be instructed to be very careful that the blockade is

Of course, the United States has a long history of merchant shipping 
issues with the British; the War of 1812 arose directly out of 
impressments and extra-legal search-and-seizure methods employed by

history, Wilson’s anxiety is certainly understandable. 


Almost on-point, and just two months after the date of this letter, the first 
serious consequence of the blockade was realized: the German Navy, 
considering virtually every ship in the war-zone a carrier of contraband, 
proceeded to sink the passenger ship Lusitania. Of the 1,950 people on 
board, 1,198 drowned, including nearly 200 Americans. Wilson was 
slow to respond, but the egregiousness of this act compelled the United 
States to enter the war less than two years later.

121. [Woritp War I]. Rommer, Erwin. 1891-1944. German 
field marshal. Nicknamed "The Desert Fox" for his superior tactical 
genius. Historic WWII-patEe SicNep Postcarp, 6 in. x 4 in., 
written in German to his wife, Lucia Mollin, and dated September 9, 
1939 — eight days after the Nazi invasion of Poland to begin WWII!!: 
“Dearest Lu! We are now in the North. In the northern Germany 
edition of the ‘Voelkische Beobachter’ [a leading paper of the Nazi 
Party] you can see your ‘general’ next to the Fuehrer at the Vistula 
river in Graudenz. Things are going amazingly well for me. Almost 
interesting news. If we manage to maintain this pace, the war will be 
over in the West by the end of October, in my opinion. It was well 
managed, you will probably say. What is Manfred doing? How do 
they see things at the castle? [a reference to the Wiener Neustadt 
Military Academy, situated in an old castle thirty miles south 
of Vienna, where Rommel had been commandant prior to 
his assignment to Hitler’s headquarters.| Right hearty greetings, 
Your Erwin”. 


In November of 1938 Rommel was appointed commandant of the 
Vienna Neustadt Military Academy, housed in a large old castle thirty 
miles south of Vienna. He served in that capacity until August 23, 1939 
when he was chosen to be the commandant of Hitler’s headquarters 
and commander of his Guard Escort Battalion. 


Less than 10 days later, the German army invaded Poland using the 
new “blitzkrieg” or “lightning war” tactics that became the model of 
its military machine for the next four years. Caught by surprise, the 
Polish army was crushed within three weeks, after a futile defense. 
England and France, the nearest threatened neighbors, immediately 
declared war on Germany and World War II was begun (an almost 
unfathomable situation just a few short years before in light of the 
horrific losses of the Great War). 


Written just eight days after the start of WWII, this spectacular dispatch 
home to his wife, in which he mentions Hitler and marvels at his own 
new title of “general”, is absolutely amazing. His comments on the 
speed of their success and hopes for a quick end to the war only add to 
the historical importance of this manuscript treasure. In mint condition.
