Carlo D. Finni, 78, for many years 
one of the foremost Italian-Americans 
of New Bedford, died at 3 this morn- 
ing at his home, 328 Acushnet avenue. 
Mr. Finni had been ill three months, 
a complication of diseases causing his 
death. é 

Mr. Finni was born in Filattierra, 
Tuscany, Italy, and came to the Unit- 
ed States in 1872, He traveled about 


CARLO D. FINNI. 


the country at first, living all over New 
England, and even in Kentucky at one 
time. Later he came to New Bedford 
in 1880, and lived here since, 


Ran Retail Fruit Store, 


Mr. Finni was for many years in 
the fruit business, running a retail 
fruit store at the northwest corner of 
Purchase and Union streets. After 
many years in the fruit business, Mr. 
‘Finni ran a saloon, from which he re- 
tired in 1918. 

He was twice married, first to Cath- 
erine Cavaganaro, who died ‘in 1905, 
and later, in 1908, to Theresa Cap- 
rara. 

Mr, Finni is survived by his wife; 
three sons by his first marriage, 
Charles D., Ernest A., and Dr. Joseph 
N. Finni; and two daughters, the 
[Misses Mary and Theresa Finni, and 
jtwo sons, John and Lawrence Finni, 
all. by the second marriage. He also 
jleaves one grandson, Francis B. Fin- 
|ni, and a brother, Dominick Finni. 

He was a communicant of St. Law- 
jrence’s church until last June, when 
the Italian Catholic church of St. Fran- 
jcis of Assisi was founded, since attend- 
ing the latter church. Mr, Finni was 
a member of Holy Name society at 
the church, the Sippican tribe of Red 
Men, and was a charter member of the 
Italian Mutual Aid society. Mr. Finni 


Wank president of the latter society in 
1909.

The citizens of the City of New Bedford, Massachusetts, through thei#Alayor, re rov nhl and various > 
organized bodies, respectfully asks that the name of New Bedford be given to one the cruisers of the United States. 


Navy now authorized by Congress. 
No seaport in the United States has carried the flag to such far corners of the globe as has New Bedford, whose 


ships cruised every sea. 
The ship Dartmouth built in 1767 in New Bedford, gained fame as the leader of the ships carrying tea to the 4 


Boston Tea Party. 

On May 14, 1775, only twenty-four days after Concord and Lexington, a group of patriots from this port. fought’ 
the First Naval Battle of the Revolutionary War, capturing two British cruisers. 

! During the Revolution, over five hundred local men served in the army, besides a large number of seamen, and® 
the Port of New Bedford swarmed with privateers which preyed on British ‘shipping. In retaliation for the damage 

done by these privateers, a great British fleet. and foree invaded New Bedford on September 5, 1778, burning seventy 

| privateers and other ships, besides buildings and stores. 

John Paul Jones, after a battle, put into New Bedford, his ship’s seuppers running blood, to bury his dead seamen 

on New Bedford soil. 

The ship Bedford, owned in New Bedford, with a cargo of whale oil, arrived off London, February 6, 1783, being 
the first vessel to fly the American flag in any British port. ’ 

In the War of 1812, scores of New Bedford ships were captured by the British, while many of our seamen were 
impressed or were confined in British prisons. Again the Port swarmed with privateers, while local men defended 
‘the Port- from invasion. 

Whaling, an important business of New Bedford at the time of the Reyolution, grew in importance, reaching 
its peak in the Fifties, when over seven hundred vessels,manned by over sixteen thousand seamen, hailed from the 
Port of New Bedford. Over 320 of these were whaling vessels. which, on their three to five years voyages, sailed 
every ocean of the globe, touched at every port, charted unknown seas, and carried the name and fame of the United 
| States all over the world. 

New Bedford sent 3,200 men to the Civil War. During the Civil War, New Bedford suffered heavily. Confed- 
erate cruisers burnt. twenty-five of our whalers. New Bedford furnished twenty-four whalers for the Stone Fleet 
whieh was sunk to blockade the port of Charleston, 

After the Civil War, our intrepid: whalemen pushed farther and farther into the frozen north, losing between 
1871 and 1888 fifty ships crushed in the ice. 

With the decline of demand for whale oil, New Bedford turned to cotton manufacturing. Starting in 1846 to 
manufacture cotton goods, New Bedford is today the Fine Textile Center of thé. World. New Bedford is the fourth 
city in Massachusetts in size and in value of manufactured zoods. 

Many New Bedford men served in the Spanish War, while in’ the World War, over five thousand men went 
from New Bedford,*many hundreds to serve in the Navy. 

We believe that the record which New Bedford men and ships have made on thé sea, as well as its present po 
sition as a great manufacturing center, make the name New Bedford worthy to be given to a cruiser of our Navy.
