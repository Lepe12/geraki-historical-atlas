AFTERWORD 


Emanuel Kozachinsky,' a Ukrainian monk of Polish origin, made a profound impact when he 
arrived in Sremski Karlovci" with four Russian colleagues in 1733. His arrival marked the 
beginning of a new era in Serbian literature and theater. In 1734, he penned the first Serbian play," 
a significant milestone in the cultural history of Serbia. The play, named after the last Serbian Tsar 
Uro8, delves into historical events from the earliest times of Serbian history up to the days when 
the play was written. With his Serbian colleagues, Kozachinsky established the first Latin school,” 
a platform that would later stage his play with the help of his students during the school celebration 
in 1734. Kozachinsky and his Russian colleagues continued their work in Sremski Karlovci and 
Belgrade until 1736,” leaving a lasting legacy in Serbian literature and theater. 

Examining the context of Serbian drama and comedy within the larger context of the world 
scene reveals a significant time gap between the first dramatic works and the first Serbian drama. 
Some 2,200 years before the arrival of Kozachinsky in Sremski Karlovci, in the sixth and fifth 
centuries BC, great Greek playwrights, including the comedy playwright Aristophanes, wrote 
some of the greatest plays in history and inaugurated the long and important tradition of 
playwriting. Despite the long-held belief that comedy is inferior to tragedy, comedy has proven to 
be a vital force in literature and theater over the centuries. This perception has evolved, and the 
old opinion is now considered prejudiced. One scholar who supports a more favorable view toward 
comedy is Mathew Kieran (University of Leeds, UK), who wrote the essay “Tragedy versus 
Comedy: On Why Comedy is the Equal of Tragedy.” 

Regardless of the period we analyze, comedy plays a significant role. This more flexible notion 
toward comedy should not be affected because even Shakespeare’s comedy The Merry Wives of 
Windsor, according to many literary experts, is a work with less literary merit than his other works. 
In addition, Falstaff’s character is much stronger in the Henry V plays than in The Merry Wives of 
Windsor. The lesser quality of this play may be attributed to the limited time at Shakespeare’s 
disposal rather than to the shortages of the genre per se. Indeed, with its abundance of prose, this 
Shakespearean play is a testament to comedy's enduring relevance and impact in literature. 

In Serbia, comedy played a crucial role in theater and the resurrection of literature in its totality. 


Even now, Branislav NuSi¢é remains the greatest Serbian writer of comedies and, perhaps, the 


greatest dramatist of all. Joakim Vuji¢ (1772-1847) is considered the father of Serbian theater, and 
Jovan Sterija Popovic¢ is the father of Serbian drama. Although this conclusion may be confusing, 
the fact is that Vujic, working with the Prince of Serbia, MiloS Obrenovi¢, established the first 
Serbian theater in Kragujevac.”" In contrast, Sterija Popovié was the first Serbian to write plays of 
literary value. 

Popovic wrote poetry, historical novels, and plays but only achieved real literary acclaim with 
his comedies. He was the first Serbian comedic playwright, yet even in his comedies, he lacked— 
to some extent—one critical element: a sharp sense of humor. His strength lies rather in his clever 
use of irony. He was among the most educated people in nineteenth-century Serbia and established 
major cultural institutions, such as the National Museum in Belgrade, in 1844. He also established 
the Serbian Academy of Sciences and Arts and the National Library. He participated in the opening 
of the first theater in Belgrade (Djumruk, 1841), which opened with his tragedy The Death of 
Stephan of Decani. In the same year, Belgrade became the capital of Serbia, and the Lyceum 
(Licej), the first law school, was established in Belgrade; Popovic, a lawyer, became one of the 
first professors at this school. 

As a person of large intellectual capacity, Popovi¢ felt the need to influence society in many 
ways and was an educator, among other things. He was aware of the inherent hypocrisy and the 
low level of education of the people living in nineteenth-century Serbia. Comedy was a good 
medium for him to express his satirical points and to trigger change, if possible. One such play, 
The Patriots—the first play in this anthology—deals with such hypocrisy and implicitly fights 
against the flashy phenomenon in which real and sincere deeds do not follow empty words and 
declarations. 

The development of drama and its perception in any culture has faced some strange turns 
throughout the centuries. In today’s Serbia, it would be hard to find anybody who would not agree 
that Branislav Nu8ic is not only one of the greatest—if not the greatest—Serbian playwrights but 
also one of the greatest Serbian literary figures. One of Nu8i¢’s most famous plays is A Suspicious 
Character, presented in this book. In his preface to this play, knowing that he would be accused 
of imitating Gogol, NuSi¢ admitted that he “wrote the play under the direct influence of Gogol, so 
that critics cannot brag that they had discovered that.” 

Yet, in the period between the two world wars, with only a few exceptions, nobody believed 


that NuSi¢ was a great writer. Many critical texts focused on NuSi¢é’s plays, emphasizing his 


“shortcomings.” Today, almost all people familiar with theater and the history of Serbian theater 
and literature would agree that Nu8i¢ was, in fact, the only Serbian playwright of high caliber 
between the two world wars. More than three centuries before Nu8i¢, the greatest English dramatist 
and writer, William Shakespeare, died in 1616; only a few years after his death, he was almost 
forgotten, and his plays were not being performed.” If it were not for his friends, John Heminges 
and Henry Condell, half of his plays would have been lost, and, most likely, Shakespeare, as we 
know him today, would not exist. Thanks to the turns or whims of history and the more open- 
minded theater people, NuSi¢ became the most staged Serbian playwright and most popular after 
World War II. The new generations of theater directors realized that NuSi¢ had a streak of genius 
and was, perhaps, 50 years ahead of his time for the Serbian theater. 

The third playwright in this anthology, DuSan Kovaéevic¢, has been one of the most prolific and 
popular Serbian playwrights on the Serbian theatrical scene since the 1970s. The first term that 
comes to mind when thinking about some of Kovaéevic’s plays is grotesque, especially in The 
Marathon Family, one of Kovaéevi¢’s first plays. He more or less continued in this manner in his 
other plays. Victor Hugo thought the grotesque was “the richest source nature can offer art.” The 
simplest explanation for why the grotesque is so effective is that it makes the contrasts more 
obvious while juxtaposing the ugly and the beautiful, the divine and the unholy, the sublime and 
the ordinary, and the romantic and the dull. If we are directly confronted with beauty and ugliness, 
beauty starts shining brighter and becomes more obvious, forcing us to appreciate it more and not 
take it for granted. Kovaéevic is a master of the grotesque, so his plays may appear somewhat 
exotic, especially to foreign theatergoers. 

The Marathon Family play, as well as a movie made in 1982, based on a screenplay by 
Kovaéevié himself and directed by Slobodan Sijan, was so popular in the former Yugoslavia and 
Serbia that, in 2013, theater director Milica Kralj decided to stage The Marathon Family with the 
male roles played by female actors. In such a situation, for instance, Grandma Pantelija resembles 
Josip Broz Tito, and the main goal of all the women in the family is to become CEOs. This was 
not the first time that female actors played this play. In 1996, director JagoS Markovic staged the 
same play with female actors and achieved much success. Similarly, in 2013, the female roles in 
Mrs. Minister, directed by Tatjana Mandic¢ Rigonat, were played by male actors at the BoSko Buha 


theatre. This approach was influenced by and reminiscent of Elizabethan times when females were 


forbidden to act because acting and playwriting were not respected professions. Indeed, they were 
not even considered real jobs. In fact, until 1660, it was illegal for women to act in England. 

By presenting these three playwrights and their comedies, we can follow the most important 
developments in the last few centuries and develop direct and indirect feelings about the state of 
affairs in Serbian society on many levels, not only on the level of literature and theater. Popovi¢ 
was more of an intellectual and a didactic educator, desiring to enlighten the general populous and 
open their eyes through satire within the idea of the comedy of character. Meanwhile, Kovacéevié 
uses his imagination more freely, relies more on humor, and does not incorporate much satirical 
tone into his comedies. Somewhere between them, not only chronologically but also stylistically 
and in terms of the creative method, stands Nu8i¢ as the most remarkable figure of the Serbian 
theatre.
