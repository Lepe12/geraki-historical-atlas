Federal Loan 
To Blackie For 
Sewage Treatment 

Lawrence E. Kindt, M.P., for the 
Federal Constituency of Macleod 
has been informed by the Honour¬ 
able E. Devle Fulton, Minister re¬ 
sponsible for the operations of the 
Central Mortgage and Housing 
Corporation, the Federal housing 
agency, of the approval of a 
Federal Government loan of 
11,343, to the Village of Blackie, 
to assist in the construction of a 
sewage treatment project. 

Made under Part VI-B of the 
National Housing Act, the loan is 
for a term of 25 years with Inter¬ 
est at 5H per cent per annum. The 
loan is based on an estimated cost 
of $17,014. 

The Village of Blackie, has no 
piped sewer system and sewage 
is disposed of by septic tanks and 
outdoor facilities. The village will 
Install a sewage collection system 
with a stabilization pond for sew¬ 
age treatment The NHA loan will 
assist in the construction of a sew¬ 
age pumping station, six-inch 
forcemain 2,650 feet In length and 
a two-acre sewage stabilization 
pond. 

Under Part VI-B of the National 
Housing Act, Central Mortgage 
and Housing Corporation may 
make a loan, to a municipality or 
to a municipal sewerage corpora¬ 
tion for the purpose of assisting in 
the construction or expansion of a 
sewage treatment project. A pro¬ 
ject must be part of an overall 
plan for the elimination or pre¬ 
vention of water and soil pollution. 

For work completed on or be¬ 
fore March 31, 1963, CMHC may 
forgive repayment of 25 percent of 
the principal amount of the NHA 
loan and 25 percent of the interest 
that has accrued in respect to the 
loan as of the date of completion 
'of the project.

Reid Hill W.l. 
Holds Meeting 
October 11th 

The regular meeting of the Reid 
Hill- W.l. was held at the home of 
Mrs. H. Rutberg on Oct. 11 with a 
fine attendance of members. Assist¬ 
ing hostesses we:e Mrs. Jack Mar¬ 
shall and Mrs. Fred Schuler. 

Mrs. Ray Harold, the president, 
was in the chair and was assisted 
in conducting the meeting by sec¬ 
retary Mrs. Marshall. The treasur¬ 
er’s report was given by Mrs. Kuy¬ 
kendall. The punctuality draw was 
won by Mrs. J. C. MacMillan. 

Correspondence dealt largely 
with routine matters. A letter was 
read from the town requesting rep¬ 
resentatives to attend a meeting 
to be held in November for the 
purpose of making plans for the 
observance of Vulcan’s 50th anniv¬ 
ersary next year. Mrs. MacMillan 
and Mrs. Sales were named to rep¬ 
resent the Institute branch. 

At the conclusion of the business 
session the meeting was turned ov¬ 
er to Mrs. E. G. McPherson, who 
gave a most interesting demon¬ 
stration on Che making of artificial 
flowers from ribbon and foam rub¬ 
ber. She then presented the fin¬ 
ished corsages to Mrs. Harold and 
to Mrs. Frieda McRoberts, who has 
been convalescing from a fractured 
leg. 

A delicious hot lunch was served 
by the hostesses to bring the after¬ 
noon to a close. 


The 50th anniversary of Vulcan’s 
incorporation as a town will be 
celebrated the weekend of June 
30, 1963, with special events of 
various kinds being held on Satur¬ 
day and Sunday, June 29-30, and 
Monday, July 1, when the annual 
Elks’ Dominion Day celebration 
will be expanded and form part of 
the town’s 50th jubilee festivities. 

The date and general form of 
the anniversary celebration were 
decided at a meeting held in the 
Lodge Hall on Wednesday evening, 
Oct 10, when 25 persons, most of 
them representing various town 
and district organizations, were 
present. Chairman of the meeting 
was Mayor M. R. Morrison, with 
M. K. Sinclair acting as secretary. 

A special invitation will be ex¬ 
tended to old-timers and former 
residents of the town to attend the 
50th jubilee celebration. 

Although the meeting was pri¬ 
marily an organization meeting to 
appoint committee chairmen, 
several suggestions emanating 
from the group will likely be 
adopted. These included such Ideas 
as a giant barbecue Sunday after¬ 
noon following an open air com¬ 
munity anniversary service, "come- 
and-go” teas especially for the 
oldtimers to be held in the Lodge 
Hall Saturday and Monday after¬ 
noons, antique cars taxi service to 
the Little Bow Pioneer Museum, 
an expanded July 1 parade, a band 
to be secured to participate in the 
parade and provide music at other 
times during that day, special de¬ 
corations for the stores and down¬ 
town area and a special "Fly-in” at 
the airstrip. 

The following committee chair¬ 
men were appointed: General 
chairman— M. R. Morrison, pro¬ 
gram chairman— W. H. Richard¬ 
son, finance— Douglas Smith, pub¬ 
licity— Bob Munro and Peter 
Plckersglll, decoration— David 
Mitchell, band— Lundy Findlay, 
sports day— Ray Wellman (Elks), 
barbecue— S k e e z Christensen, 
school participation— H. H. 
Mumby and Fred Cartwright. 
Other committee heads will be ap¬ 
pointed later. 

The next meeting of the plan¬ 
ning group will be held on Wed¬ 
nesday evening, Nov. 14, at the 
Lodge Hall.

Talk On India 
By Missionaries 

The WSWS held their monthly 
meeting for October in the Grace 
Church. Rev. Heebner was in 
charge of the meeting. 

Mr. and Mrs. McKinstry, visiting 
missionaries from India, brought a 
real challenge to all present, to 
pray for the land of India, and to 
especially pray for the darkened 
and unfortunate state of the Hindu 
women. They also showed interest¬ 
ing slides of India and of their 
work there. After the showing of 
the pictures, the chorus, “On the 
Darkest Side of the Road," was 
sung. Rev. Heebner closed the ser¬ 
vice and prayer. 

A short business session followed 
and a delicious lunch was served 
in the basement of the church.

Soil Drifting Warning 

Alberta farmers are urged to take all possible 
precautions now to prevent soil drifting from becom¬ 
ing a serious problem this winter. If the snow cover¬ 
ing is light, strong winds could cause serious drifting 
because dry conditions in many parts of the province 
have left the land much more prone to erosion than 
usual. 

Soil drifting emergency control measures recom¬ 
mended by W. Lobay, Alberta’s Supervisor of Soils 
and Weed Control are based on three principles. The 
first is the re-establishment of the cloddy structure on 
the surface of the soil. A one-way disc with every four 
discs removed or a heavy duty cultivator with chisel 
points or shanks placed at two or three foot intervals 
is recommended for this jobj The next rule is to 
spread straw or manure on knolls and any other focal 
points which are particularly vulnerable to drifting as 
soon as any soil movement is noticed. This will prevent 
drifting from spreading over the entire field. Listing 
or ridging the field at right angles to the prevailing 
winds is the third principle in soil drifting control. A 
cultivator with shovels spaced about three feet apart 
will do a very good job of ridging. 

Since surface conditions are continually chang¬ 
ing, all fields should be checked regularly for signs of 
drifting and those with a past history of drifting 
should be ridged this fall. Summerfallowed fields 
should be very carefully checked this year because cul¬ 
tural operations which were done during the dry 
weather are likely to have the soil lose and powdery. 

Mr. Lobay advises any farmer with soil drifting 
problems to consult his district agriculturist who will 
be pleased to help him work out the best course of 
action for any particular set of circumstances.

Old-fashioned costumes, parades 
featuring carriages, democrats and 
buggies as well as sleek modern 
convertables, sports events, 
speeches and plenty of good food 
are the order of the day in many 
towns and villages throughout 
Alberta which this year are celeb¬ 
rating their Golden Jubilees. 

It was the establishment of 
Alberta as a province in 1905, 
followed by the completion of the 
railway, in 1912, Which sparked the 
influx of settlers. These pioneers 
journeyed to their new homes in 
Red River carts, buckboards. cov¬ 
ered wagons. A large number of 
them probably travelled in the 
"fast new railroad". 

In the intervening years the 
vast expanses of grassy prairie 
have become waving fields of 
golden grain, doited here and there 
by oil derricks. The log shacks 
have been replaced by comfortable, 
modern homes, and winding narrow 
paths have given way to broad, 
straight highways. Tihe trip from 
Calgary to Edmonton, which once 
took eight days, can now be accom¬ 
plished in four hours by road, or 
in forty-five minutes by air. The 
puffing steam engines which pulled 
the first trains into Alberta are 
now museum pieces. In their place, 
giant diesel units speed across the 
province in a matter of hours, and 
“dayliners” — combined engine and 
coach in one unit — run fast, 
regular service between major 
centres of the province. 

The population of the province 
of Alberta has increased from a 
little more than 375,000 in 1912 to 
its present 1,331,944.

New villages established in 1912 
were: Blackie, Carbon, Chauvin,

Irma, Mirror, St. Paul, Sylvan 
Lake, Three Hills, Vulcan, Veteran.

Five towns were incorporated in 
1912, but one of them, Diamond 
City, failed to survive, and was 
disorganized in 1937. The remain¬ 
ing towns are Bow Island, Corona¬ 
tion, Redcliff and Stavely. 

Three further steps went into the 
record for 1912. First, Fort Mac- 
leod, which had been a town since 
December 31, 1892, came “under 
the provisions of the Town Act” 
on March 20, 1912. Next, North 
Edmonton, an organized village 
since January 20, 1910, was 

annexed by Edmonton on July 22; 
1912, and finally, Strathcona, which

1899, and a city in 1907, was amal¬ 
gamated with Edmonton under an 
Act of the Legislature effective 
February 1, 1912.

years of negotiations between the 
three prairie provinces and the 
Government of Canada. In 1961 
the Natural Resources Transfer 
Agreement of 1930 was amended, 
granting authority to the province 
to eliminate school lands. 

Dominion Lands Legislation, 
prior to 1930, required two sections 
of land out of each surveyed town¬ 
ship in the prairie provinces to be 
set aside as an endowment for 
education and money realized from 
the sale of such lands has since 
been deposited in a school lands en¬ 
dowment trust fund. When resourc¬ 
es were transferred from Federal 
to Provincial authority, in 1930, the 
Transfer Agreemnt upheld the 
school • lands endowment Legisla¬ 
tion as a provincial responsibilty. 
In many cases, under provisions of 
the Dominion Lands Act, parcels 
originally designated as school 
lands have since been traded for 
other lands of similar size and 
value. In all cases the intent of the 
Federal Legislation was maintain¬ 
ed namely, the retention of a sub¬ 
stantial area of land in Alberta 
for educational endowment. 

During recent years provincial 
grants for education have far ex¬ 
ceeded income from the school 
lands endowment fund and the con¬ 
tribution that the fund makes to 
total education financing has be¬ 
come relatively small. 

Cost created by the need for sep¬ 
arated school lands administration 
by the province, within its whole 
public lands program, represented 
an additional and unwarranted 
public expense. 

Removing these parcels from the 
rigid endowment fund procedure 
will assist the province to administ¬ 
er them along with other public 
lands in an orderly manner. This 
change in policy will not hinder 
grants of land for school construc¬ 
tion and other education purposes.

— LAST WEEK’S MARKET REPORT — 

TUESDAY, FEEDER AND CALF SALE— 

All classes sold well, though we lacked numbers of those good 
ranch raised calves that the buyers like. Good Yearling Steers 
sold at $25 to $26.80; Common to Medium sold at $22 to $24.50. 
We didn’t have enough yearling heifers to establish a market. 
Good Steer Calves sold at $27 to $30 with the odd one going to 
$32. Good Heifer Calves sold at $23 to $25.70. 

FRIDAY BEEF AND FEEDER SALE— 

The market was slightly easier this week. Good to Choice 
Butcher Steers $25 to 27.90. We had one Choice Steer sell at 
$28.10; Good to low choice Butcher Heifers at $23 to $25.60; 
Good Cows sold at $14 to $16.50; Medium to Good Cows sold 
at $10 to $13.50; Canners and cutters $5 to $10 or subject. 
SATURDAY PIG AND MILK COW SALE— 

Prices were steady on all classes of hogs. Weaner Pigs $8 to 
$12; Feeder Pigs $13 to $35; Piggy Sows $45 to $85; Boars $20 
to $50; Milk Cows sold at $150 to $225. Some outstanding good 
Guernsey Heifers sold at $255 to $285. Baby Calves sold at $20 
to $40.
