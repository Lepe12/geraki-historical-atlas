The collaboration 


T his is a case study in the Patterns of Power series. It applies the series’ framework 

to the Regency of Algiers from the brothers Aruj and Khayr al-Din’s seizure of 
the town in 1516 and Khayr al-Din’s submission to the Ottoman sultan in 1519, 
through the corps of janissaries’ assumption of the government in the seventeenth 
century, to the capitulation of the Kasba to a French army on 5 July 1830. The 
framework is a lens for reading how any order of power is founded, dressed and held. 
Beneath every order is a floor of force. Over it lies the claim that makes the force cheap. 
The settlement divides what the force took; the cost column is where each man 
beneath the hierarchy prices refusal; formation decides whose picture of the world a 
child receives before he can weigh it; and the default is how the men who hold an 
arrangement defend it without anyone having to coordinate them. Running through 
all of it is position logic: people making decisions with the materials at hand, weighing 
benefit against credible cost within the field of what they can see. 

Thomas J. Bryan brought the framework, the direction and the judgement of the 
whole. Claude did the research and wrote the drafts. Each part was built one at a time, 
verified before it was written, presented and confirmed before the next was begun. The 
framework's terms sit underneath the prose as a lens for reading the sources, and the 
study tries not to use them as vocabulary on the page. 

The case was chosen to test a hypothesis the two preceding studies could not. Haiti 
had found that pay is the working definition of who owns a floor of force; Bukhara had 
found that revocable instruments drift toward heritability whenever the right of 
resumption goes unexercised. Both cases still had land in them. The Regency was an 
order whose revenue ran almost entirely through chokepoints — the sea, the port, the 
tax column, the tribute of foreign kings — ruled by a corporation that recruited grown 
men from abroad and forbade its members to pass their places to their sons. The 
hypothesis was that such an order would combine constant violence at the top with no 


structural change at all, because no victor could convert a victory into anything he

could leave to a son. The study was also built to measure what a closed admissions rule 
costs over three centuries, and to refuse the single word through which the Regency is 
usually seen. 

The study ends where the collection's volume on French Algeria begins. That 
study's landscape describes the Regency on the eve of 1830 and narrates the quarrel 
over the Bacri and Busnach debt, the dey's blow at the French consul in 1827, the 
blockade and the expedition, and quotes the convention's promise to respect the 
religion and liberty of the inhabitants. This study reads the same convention for what it 


gave the Regency's own hierarchy and does not retell the French side. 


The sources and what they could see 


Almost everything written about the Regency before 1830 was written by foreigners 
who lived on the coast, and the order's own paperwork survives only in part and only in 
French editions made after the conquest. The study declared the consequence of that 
before the first module and held to it. 

The Regency's own documents reached here are four. The register of maritime 
prizes, kept by its administration and found in the Kasba in 1830, was published by 
Albert Devoulx in the Revue africaine in 1871 and 1872, with its accounts of division 
from the 1760s and yearly summaries to 1815. The corps's covenant of 1749, the ‘ahd 
al-aman, was published in a French version made from an Arabic translation of the 
Turkish in the Revue africaine in 1860. A deed of endowment for the zawiya of the 
Prophet's descendants was printed by Devoulx in his survey of the religious buildings 
of Algiers. And the investiture firman of 1746 for the dey Ibrahim Khodja was printed 
by Eugéne Plantet in his edition of the deys' correspondence with France in 1889, 
together with deys' letters, the convention of 1830, and Hussein Dey’s letter from exile. 
The dey's own Tachrifat register exists in an edition of 1852 but could not be reached 
from the environment in which the study was built. 

The rest is the testimony of men who stood somewhere and wanted something. Leo 
Africanus, a Granadan Muslim who travelled the coast in the 1510s, is the insider 
witness to the order the brothers found. The Yopographia, e historia general de Argel, 
printed in 1612 under Diego de Haedo's name, describes the city of the 1580s from the 
Spanish side and contains a Christian captive's dialogues. The brothers’ own chronicle, 
a panegyric of uncertain authorship, reaches the study in a French translation of 1837. 
Pierre Dan, a Trinitarian friar who came to ransom captives, wrote in the 1630s and 
1640s. The account written by Laugier de Tassy from inside the French consulate in 
the 1720s reaches the study only in Joseph Morgan's English version of 1750, whose 


title page calls the Regency a piratical state. Venture de Paradis left notes on the city in

the 1780s, printed in the Revue africaine in the 1890s. William Shaler, the American 
consul, published his Sketches of Algiers in 1826, with a treasury account for 1822 
furnished by an informant, a journal of the crises of the 1820s, and an appendix 
containing Lord Exmouth’s report of the bombardment of 1816. Devoulx's Epigraphie 
indigéne of 1874 supplies the Regency's inscriptions in his translations. French colonial 
scholarship of the 1890s supplies the categories of rayat and makhzan and a reckoning 
of the territory the Turks actually held. 

The consequence runs through every part. The order's own voice is heard in its 
register, its covenant, its firman, its deeds and its stones. Everything else is a foreigner's 
report, and the interior of the country — where most of its people were formed, taxed 
and killed — is seen almost entirely from the coast, through the state's gifts to its 
religious lineages and through French descriptions made after 1830. The study says so 


where it matters and does not pretend otherwise. 


The verdicts this study did not adopt 
The Regency has been explained by five ready-made verdicts, each assembled by a 


party with a use for it. The study names them, places its witnesses among them, and 
adopts none. 

The first is the captivity narrative, written by ransomed captives and by the religious 
orders that ransomed them, whose charity depended on the horror of what they 
described. Dan belongs to it, and so does the captive of Haedo's dialogues. It is also the 
best eyewitness material that exists, and the study uses it for what its authors saw and 
discounts it for what they needed. 

The second is the tribute-payers’ account, assembled by the consuls, navies and 
diplomats of states that paid the Regency and resented paying. Shaler belongs to it, and 
so, in its English dress, does the Laugier account, whose French author argued that the 
Algerines were misunderstood and whose English translator sold him as a historian of 
pirates. The American version of this account is the loudest and has shaped the modern 
Anglophone picture. 

The third is the French justificatory account written after 1830, which needed a nest 
of pirates and a decayed tyranny to justify the conquest. Much of the administrative 
detail in circulation comes from it, including the editions through which the Regency's 
own documents survive, and the study uses those editions for the documents while 
declining the frame. 

The fourth is the Algerian national account written after independence, which 


reads the Regency as an early Algerian state. Its difficulty is that the ruling corporation 


PAT TERNS OF POWER 6 DHE REGENGY OF ALGIERS 


recruited abroad and refused to admit Algerians. The study gives that account its 
strongest form in its closing part. 

The fifth is the modern quantitative revision of the history of Christian captivity, 
whose headline figure of a million or more Europeans enslaved on the Barbary coast 
rests on an extrapolation specialists dispute. The study gives it as a range with its 
method named and refuses a total. 

And beneath all five lies the word the study was built to set aside: pirate. Corsairing 
from Algiers was licensed, taxed, recorded in a register with a share table, conducted 
under a flag and limited by treaties with named powers — which is to say it was 
privateering, the instrument every maritime state of the period used, including the 
Knights of St John on the opposite shore. The study establishes the table, the register 


and the treaties and leaves the reader to choose the word. 


Where the research corrected the seed 


The study began from a seed written at the close of the previous build, and from a 
page, written before any research, recording what training had left about the Regency: 
a pirate state; deys mostly murdered; corsairing arriving with the Turks and dying after 
1816; a decayed despotism falling in three weeks. The seed was scaffolding, not a cage. 
Where the evidence disagreed with it, or with that page, the evidence won, and the 
corrections are recorded here rather than argued in the modules. 

The seed expected constant violence at the top. The thirty deys named by Plantet 
between 1671 and 1830 show violence in two periods — roughly 1683 to 1724 and 
1805 to 1817 — and not in the seventy-five years between, when five of seven deys died 
in office or left it by abdication. Of the nineteen whose ends could be established, ten 
were killed by their own order, eight died in office or left alive, and one was removed by 
France. No total is given for all thirty. 

The seed expected nothing heritable anywhere in the ruling order. That was true of 
the register: a soldier's place died with him and his musket returned to the armoury. 
But the members did not have nothing to lose, because each held a claim on a system of 
positions that would outlast him. Heritability entered at the margins — janissary 
families holding rural estates near the capital, beys' sons succeeding their fathers, great 
men's children drawing top pay from birth — and at the apex itself, where the office of 
dey passed for more than half a century from uncle to nephew through the treasurer's 
post. 

The seed expected the kouloughlis to have been excluded from the corps's high 
ranks for three centuries against continuous pressure. Haedo, around 1580, says the 


sons and grandsons of janissaries could enrol and many had. The exclusion followed 


PAT TERNS OF POWER i THE REGENCY OF ALGIERS 


their failed seizure of the Kasba in the seventeenth century, was relaxed when recruits 
ran short, and was softened by the covenant of 1749. The exclusion that held in every 
period was not of the corps's sons but of the country's Arabic- and Berber-speaking 
population. 

The seed expected the Ottoman warrant, the claim of holy war and the treaty 
persona shown to Europe to be flatly inconsistent. The firman of 1746 joins them: it 
calls the Algerines the foremost champions of the faith and grants the governorship on 
condition that no power holding the Porte's safe-conduct is harmed. War and treaty 
were one law of war and aman. 

The seed treated the union of dey and pasha in 1710, thirty-nine years after the first 
dey, as a gap that might be read, as the Bukhara volume read a similar one, as a sequence 
of tests. The research found no tests. It found a purchase: a dey who expelled the 
sultan's governor and then asked the sultan, with presents to the seraglio, for the 
governor's title. 

The starting page expected corsairing to be the order's main revenue and to have 
arrived with the Turks. Leo Africanus records that the townsmen of Algiers had built 
galleys and raided the Balearics before the brothers came, which is why Ferdinand built 
his fort. In the one year whose accounts survive, 1822, prizes contributed nothing; 
tribute, monopolies, the estates of the dead and the rents of confiscated property 
supplied more than the provinces. The prize register shows corsairing large and volatile 
in the war years, not constant. And the order did not fall because it could not pay: the 
French found nearly forty-nine million francs in the Kasba. It fell to a fracture at its 
apex in 1817, the loss of its income from the sea in 1816, and a conquest in 1830. 

Two expectations were confirmed. The corps did own itself by the definition of pay. 
And no rising from below altered the arrangement in three centuries: every structural 


change came from armed men inside the order or from a power above or outside it. 


The disciplines 


Quotation. Public-domain primary sources are quoted exactly, in the original 
French, Spanish or English where it matters, with a working rendering, at the length 
the argument needs. The renderings are this study's own. Modern copyrighted 
scholarship is paraphrased briefly and credited, and where a finding reaches the study 
only through a summary, that is said. 

Verification. No quotation, date, attribution or figure is given from memory. Every 
exact quotation was checked against the scanned page, either by an independent optical 
reading of a crop of the printed lines or, where the type defeated machine reading, by 


reading the image of the page directly. Where a check failed, the passage was

paraphrased or dropped, and the drop is recorded in the part's note on sources. Figures 
read only from a text layer are marked as such. 

Ranges. Contested figures are given as ranges with their provenance, and totals are 
refused wherever the published bases are incompatible — above all for captives and for 
the dead. 

Symmetry. The study applies the symmetry test in both directions. The powers that 
called the Regency a nest of slavers were, in the same centuries, carrying Africans across 
the Atlantic on a far larger scale; saying so is not an acquittal, and the man chained in 
the bagnio at Algiers was not consoled by comparative statistics. 

Legibility is not pardon. The study explains the logic of the order without excusing 
it, holds the suffering with gravity, and refuses both flattery and demonisation. 

Comparison. The comparison volumes were read at first hand, by writing their full 
text to disk and reading only the sections the comparison needed, and the closing part 


says which sections of which studies were read. 


What the Regency added to the framework 


The case isolated mechanisms the collection's other studies obscure. 

It found that an order can settle itself on title to flows alone — the Friday sermon, 
the coin, the tribute of foreign kings, a share of prizes — with no conquered land 
divided among the conquerors, provided the positions it distributes are institutional, 
even where none is heritable. And it found that durable property in such an order 
migrates, one legal transaction at a time, out from under titles the state can resume and 
into endowments it cannot touch. 

It found that of the several claims an order makes to several audiences, the one to 
which every title runs back binds its holders: the corps could kill deys at will, but when 
the sultan ordered war on France against every Algerian interest, a single officer landing 
with a written order was enough. 

It found that withholding formation is cheap for as long as the formation left in 
place already honours the conqueror's warrant, and costs everything on the day the 
warrant fails, because nothing in the population's picture of the world attaches it to the 
men who wore the title. 

It found that exit is set by what a man is worth to the order held: the Jew's gate 
opened on two days in seven, the Christian captive's conversion was refused and his 
ransom priced, and the black slave's conversion freed him. 

It found that a state can hide its share of a traffic in human beings inside the design 
of an auction, and that a captive held for ransom is a bond whose labour is the interest 


and whose ransom is the principal.

It found that an order can publish its acts while withdrawing its deliberation, even 
from its own soldiers, and that an open field of doctrine can coexist with an absolute 
closure on one subject — who rules whom. 

It found that violence at the apex of a non-hereditary order can be periodic rather 
than constant, and that the peace between the violent periods was bought by exactly 
what the order's rules excluded: a ruler who could leave his office to his kin. 

It found that the written liberties of a ruling corporation rest on an unwritten one 
— its power to remove its head — and that this can be lost without a document when 
the head carries the treasury uphill and guards it with the excluded. 

And it found that the violence of an order can survive as the name of an hour: 
bandiera bassa, the time the prayer flag came down and the bastinado began, named in 


the mixed language of the city's captives. 


A note on the dates 


The title page reads 1516 — 1671 — 1830. The founding date is Aruj's seizure of 
Algiers; the ending is the capitulation of 5 July 1830. The mid-point was an argument, 
not a fact. The live alternative was 1710, when the dey took the title of pasha and the 
sultan stopped sending governors. The study kept 1671, the year the corporation first 
elected its own head, because 1710 changed the form of the warrant and not its owner: 
the dey bought the pasha's title from Istanbul, and the fiction by which an election in 
Algiers was described as a petition for an appointment reserved to the Porte was fixed 


from then on. 


The arc 


A landscape opening and twelve parts, each with a framework function and its own 
title. 

Landscape — the ground on the eve. A Simple Stranger. The central Maghrib 
between the Spanish landings of 1509 and the sultans firman of 1519: a coast on which 
every town, tribe and dynasty was already paying someone for protection, where 
corsairing was older than the Turks, and where two captains from Lesbos were hired by 
the town of Algiers against a Spanish fort, killed their employer in his bath, and 
borrowed from Istanbul the sermon and the coin they could not mint. 

1 — The Floor of Force. Four Doblas a Month. Whose men the corps were. The 
janissary name crossed the sea without the institution that made janissaries a sultan’s 
household; a concession let any Turk enlist as a free volunteer; pay moved from 


Istanbul's purse to the corps's own strongroom; and by 1749 the corps described the

Kasba as the house from which it drew its pay and its subjects as the mines from which 
it drew its resources. 

2 — The Settlement. His Musket and His Name. What the men who held Algiers 
divided among themselves: a place on the register that died with its holder, an eighth of 
every prize and half of what remained, offices farmed for a fixed remittance, a judge 
who inherited from the heirless, and the one transaction that ran only one way — the 
migration of property into endowments for Mecca that no dey could touch. 

3 — The Robe. The Key of the Muslim States. Four claims to four audiences — the 
coin, the stones of mosques, the corps's covenant and the letters to Europe — and the 
one claim, the sultan's investiture, to which every title ran back; how that claim bound 
the men who held it in 1798 and in 1815; and how the firman joined holy war and 
treaty under the law of safe-conduct. 

4 — Formation. As Oracles of Heaven. Who put the picture of the world into a 
child of Algiers: the Qur'an school, the endowed zawiya whose teacher no dey could 
appoint, the marabout whose word was taken because he said it, and the call to prayer 
by which the dey kept his own clock — and why the corps, formed abroad as grown 
men, left all of it alone, and could not form its own sons. 

5 — The Cost Column. On Saturdays and Wednesdays. The sum each man beneath 
the corps ran for himself: the townsman whose compliance was nearly free and richly 
rewarded, the Jew who could leave the city's gates on two days in seven, the tribes that 
ate and the tribes that were eaten, the janissary held by marriage and office, and the 
captive whose conversion was refused because he was worth more held. 

6 — Extraction. At a Vile Price. The surplus followed by mechanism through the 
one surviving year's accounts: assessments, tribute, monopolies sold at auction, escheat 
and rent, a customs duty of under two per cent; the grain delivered in kind, the beys' 
retentions and the unpaid labour of captives and convicts that appear in no column; 
and the hoard spent on imports while the pay exceeded the receipts. 

7 — Information and Suppression. The Sack and the Pike. The men who stood 
between the people and what they could not check; a field of doctrine open to every 
holy man but closed on one question, as the marabout of Tlemcen found in 1627; and 
the divan’s deliberation withdrawn over two centuries from a hall of howling soldiers 
to a dey’s council in a fortress, while the order's acts stayed on public display. 

8 — Elite Coordination and the Default. From the Same Prudent Motives. The 
hierarchy audited man by man, and thirty deys counted by name: two violent bands 
and a quiet middle, the soldier who gained a pay rise when a dey was killed, the 
conspirators who were a working part of the order, the office that passed from uncle to 


nephew, and the beys whose offices were drifting into their families.

9 — Plurality or Foreclosure. A Hair Split in Forty. Where protection from the

A town that paid three times 


T he best way into the coast the corsair brothers found is not through its kings but 

through its payments, because on this shore in the first two decades of the 
sixteenth century almost everyone who mattered was paying someone else to be left 
alone, and the direction of each payment tells you who could actually reach whom. 

Leo Africanus — "a More, borne in Granada," as the title page of his English 
translation calls him — who says he travelled this coast in these very years, gives the 
history of Algiers as a sequence of such purchases. The town had long been subject to 
the kings of Tlemcen in the west, but when a king arose at Bougie, nearer and more 
dangerous, the townsmen weighed the two and changed masters. They saw, in the 
words of John Pory's English of 1600, that the king of Tlemcen could not defend 
them, and that the king of Bougie could harm them, "wherefore they offered vnto him 
a yeerely tribute of their owne accord, and yet remained almost free from all exaction.” 
That sentence is the town’s whole political method in a line. It did not submit to the 
stronger prince; it bought its exemption from him, at a price it set itself, and in return 
was left to run its own affairs — which is to say that the thing Algiers purchased. was 
not government but distance. 

Then the Spaniards came, and the price rose. The fullest account of what followed is 
in the Topographia, e historia general de Argel, printed in 1612 under the name of 
Diego de Haedo, which is to say by the other side. Its author tells how Count Pedro 
Navarro, in what he gives as the year 1509, took Oran and Bougie from the Moors for 
the King of Spain, and how the townsmen of Algiers, fearing the same fate and 
watching Navarro destroy place after place along the coast, went looking for a 
protector: they "acordaron de darse a vn poderoso Moro Xeque, y Principe de los 
Alarbes que viuian en Mutijar, que son los campos y Ilanuras grandes vezinas de Argel, 
el qual se dezia Selim Eunttemi, para q[ue] los defendiesse, y tuuiesse sobre su amparo, 
como hizo algunos afios, hasta que los Turcos tiranicamente se apoderaron desta 


ciu[dad]" — they agreed to give themselves to a powerful Moorish shaykh and prince

of the Arabs who lived in the Mitidja, the great plains beside Algiers, who was called 
Selim al-Tumi, so that he might defend them and hold them under his protection, as he 
did for some years, until the Turks tyrannously made themselves masters of the city. 

A tribal prince of the plain was a protector against tribes; he was no protector 
against a fleet. So the town paid a second time, and to the fleet. The same page of 
Haedo records that Algiers "sometieron a la obediencia del Rey Catolico, haziendo con 
el tregua por diez afios, y obligandose a pagar cada afio su tributo" — submitted to the 
obedience of the Catholic King, making a truce with him for ten years and binding 
itself to pay its tribute every year. Ferdinand did not trust a truce with a corsair port, 
and took his security in stone: on the islet that sat so close to the town he had a fort 
built, "en el qual tenia de ordinario dozientos soldados de guarnicio[n] con su 
Capitan," in which he kept an ordinary garrison of two hundred soldiers with their 
captain, well supplied with munitions, provisions and artillery. And the fort worked. 
"Con este fuerte quedaron los de Argel muy enfrenados, sin osar andar en corso, ni 
menos reuelarse al Rey Catolico, hasta que muriendo el dicho Rey en el mes de Enero, 
en el afio 1516" — with this fort the people of Algiers were kept well bridled, not 
daring to go out corsairing, still less to rebel against the Catholic King, until the said 
King died in the month of January 1516. 

Three things should be fixed from this before the brothers enter, because each 
shapes what they were able to do. The first is that the town's revenue, in the decade 
before the conquest, was flowing outward — to Bougie, to a tribal prince, to Spain — 
and in each case it purchased a protection that stopped at the edge of the protector's 
reach. The second is that Algiers already had galleys and already used them: Leo says 
plainly that the townsmen had built ships and raided the Balearic islands, and that this 
was the reason Ferdinand sent his armada and built his fort. Corsairing on this coast 
did not arrive with the Turks; the Spanish fort was a response to it, and the fort's two 
hundred men were a tax collected on the town's own seafaring. The third is the most 
important and the easiest to miss. Haedo's sentence ends not with a Spanish reverse or 
a rising of the town but with a death. The truce was a contract with a man, and when 
the man died in January 1516 the townsmen treated the obligation as having died with 
him. Nothing on this shore — not the tribute to Bougie, not the protection of the 
Mitidja prince, not the ten-year truce with Spain — outlived the reach or the life of the 


party it was made with. 


The kingdom that paid its enemy's soldiers 


Westward, the dynasty that had once owned Algiers was in a worse condition than 


the town, and Leo, who knew its capital, is again the witness. The kingdom of Tlemcen

had lived on its two harbours, Oran and Mers el-Kébir, where Genoese and Venetian 
merchants came to trade, and on the caravan road to the south that ran through it. 
Both harbours, Leo writes, were taken by Ferdinand of Aragon, to the great harm of 
the whole kingdom, and the loss of them broke the dynasty from within: the reigning 
king was expelled by his own subjects, a kinsman was released from prison to take his 
place, and the throne passed back and forth among the family as each faction found 
outside backing. 

What that backing cost is the fiscal sentence for the whole western kingdom. When 
the expelled king recovered his throne with an army raised by the help of Charles V, Leo 
records that "he gave the Spanish soldiers their pay, sent the captaines home with great 
rewardes, and allowed Charles the emperour a large yeerely reuenue so long as he 
liued.” A court that could still commission verse and keep its mosques endowed was, in 
the decade the Turks arrived, sending its revenue to the garrison of its own lost port 
and paying the wages of the army that had put it back on its throne. A reader who 
judged Tlemcen by its capital would have seen a kingdom. A reader who followed its 
money would have seen a client of Oran. 

To the east, the Hafsid sultans of Tunis claimed a wider authority than they 
exercised. Bougie, the town Algiers had once bought exemption from, had fallen to 
Navarro; what remained to Tunis on this stretch of coast was a claim and a set of 
harbours, and — as the brothers would discover — a willingness to rent those harbours 
to anyone who would pay in the coin a Muslim sovereign could lawfully accept. 

Between the kingdoms lay the people who actually held the ground. The plains 
around Algiers belonged, in every sense that mattered, to Arab tribes whose princes 
could be made governors of towns, as Selim al-Tumi was; Leo says he was of the 
Tha'aliba — the text reads "Zealiba" — and had been made governor at the time Bougie 
fell to the Spaniards. The mountains to the east were Berber country and would remain 
beyond the reach of every power on the coast. And in the towns sat the men whose 
consent turned possession into something the population would recognise — the 
notables and the men of law, of whom one, the mufti of Algiers, will be heard speaking 


for the town at the decisive moment. 


Older than any of them 


It would be a mistake, and a characteristic one, to treat this as a coast without an 
order simply because it was a coast without a strong king. The order that mattered 
most here was older than every dynasty on it, and it is the one every contender in this 


story would end by borrowing.

It had itself been made by conquest. The Arab armies of the seventh century had 
brought Islam and, over the following centuries, Arabic into a Berber country; later 
Arab tribal migrations had carried Arabic speech and Arab lineages out onto the plains; 
the dynasties of Tlemcen, Bougie and Tunis were each the heirs of later seizures of 
power; and Leo, describing Algiers, still records that the town had been founded by a 
Berber lineage, the Mesgana, before it acquired its Arabic name. By 1500 none of that 
was experienced as conquest by anyone. It was simply the shape of the world: the 
Maliki law applied by the qadi, the Friday sermon in which the name of the rightful 
ruler was pronounced, the coin that carried a sovereign’s name into every market, and 
the obligation of believers to defend the lands of Islam against the Christian who came 
by sea. That a thing was once built by the sword does not make it less real to those 
formed inside it. It makes it the ground they stand on, and it is precisely because it was 
ground rather than argument that it was worth so much to whoever could claim to 
stand for it. 

The practical consequence for anyone arriving on this coast with ships and no kin 
was stark. There was one currency of rightful rule that everyone from the tribal prince 
to the mufti already honoured — the sermon and the coin in the name of a Muslim 
sovereign who defended the faith — and there was no way to mint it. It could only be 


borrowed from someone who already held it. 


Two captains and a fifth 


The challengers were two brothers, Aruj and Khizr — the second would become 
Khayr al-Din — from the island of Lesbos (as modern reference works give their 
origin), sea-raiders in the eastern Mediterranean before they were anything in the west. 
The chronicle composed within their own circle, the Arabic history of the brothers 
published in French in 1837 as Fondation de la Régence d'Alger, explains their move 
west as a consequence of a quarrel among princes: the Ottoman succession struggle 
between Selim and his brother Korkut left the brothers on the losing side in the Levant 
and in need of a new harbour. 

What they carried west was not an army but an enterprise: a few galliots, crews 
recruited in the Levant, and the capital of whatever prizes they had taken. The crews 
were paid in shares of captures, which meant that the enterprise ran only as long as it 
kept taking ships. And a raiding enterprise needs two things it cannot supply for itself 
— a port in which to refit and sell, and a title under which its captures are lawful prize 
rather than robbery. 

The chronicle records exactly how the first of those was bought. Arriving off Tunis 


with his ships loaded with captives and goods, Aruj sent a magnificent present to the

Hafsid sultan and asked leave to establish himself in one of the sultan’s ports. The 
sultan granted it on two conditions: that his subjects and allies should have no 
complaint of Aruj's men, and that Aruj should give him the "cinquiéme des prises faites 
par la suite sur les ennemis de l'islamisme" — the fifth of the prizes taken thereafter 
from the enemies of Islam. "Aroudj consentit 4 tout.” Aruj agreed to everything. 

The price is not arbitrary and should not be read past. The fifth is the share of booty 
that the law of holy war reserves to the ruler, and in taking it the sultan of Tunis did 
more than collect a rent. He converted the brothers’ captures into lawful spoil of war 
waged under his authority. The brothers' first title in the west, in other words, was a 
licence: a port and a legal category, purchased for a fixed share of the flow, from a 
sovereign whose own law made him the proper recipient of that share. It was revocable 
at the sultan’s pleasure, and the brothers would learn how equivocal his pleasure could 


be. 


The men who went home to sow 


The brothers’ ambitions quickly outran the licence. They wanted not a port under 
someone else's flag but a port of their own, and the obvious targets were the towns the 
Spaniards had taken. The attack on Bougie showed them what kind of armed force this 
coast could supply to a stranger, and Leo, who says he passed through Bougie while 
Aruj was besieging the Spanish fort there, recorded what happened. 

The siege failed not in battle but in the calendar. "A great part of his soldicrs being 
husbandmen," Leo writes, "when they perceiued the time of sowing corne to approch, 
without any leaue or licence they forsooke their gencrall, and returned home to the 
plough-taile." He adds that many of the Turks did likewise, so that Aruj was forced to 
raise the siege, burned his own galleys in the river, and retreated with forty men to the 
castle of Jijel. 

That is the most exact statement available of the problem the brothers faced, and it 
is not a military problem. The fighting men a captain could raise on this coast were not 
his. The tribesman who came to the siege belonged to his lineage, his fields and his 
season; he came for plunder and the merit of fighting Christians, and he left when the 
land called him, because the land was what fed his family and the captain was not. Even 
the Turks were his only for as long as there was pay in prospect. A man with ships and 
no ground could hire violence here, but he could not own it, and violence he did not 


own went home at sowing time.

Hired, then master 


The death of Ferdinand in January 1516 undid the arrangement that had bridled 
Algiers, and it did so through the same logic by which the town had always governed 
itself. Leo, who was on this coast at the time, gives the sequence in two sentences: "king 
Ferdinando deceasing, the people of Alger released themselues, from paying any more 
tribute: for seeing Barbarossa to be a most valiant warriour, and a deadly enemie vnto 
Christians, they sent for him, and chose him captaine ouer all their forces; who 
presently encountred the fort, but to little effect. Afterward this Barbarossa secretly 
murthered the gouernour of the citie in a certaine bath." 

Read in order, those sentences describe a contract and its breach. The town, freed of 
its tribute by a death, hired the best available violence against the fort that had collected 
it, on the same terms it had always used — protection bought from the nearest party 
who could deliver it. The hired captain failed to deliver: his guns could not take the 
islet. And then, rather than being dismissed as a failed contractor, he killed his 
employer. The man murdered in the bath was Selim al-Tumi, the Mitidja prince who 
had governed the town under his own protection and, in Haedo's account, had 
received Aruj into his own house with great honour. 

What Aruj did next is the first appearance on this coast of the problem the whole 
order would spend three centuries living with. Leo says that "Barbarossa vsurped the 
whole gouernment of the citie vnto himselfe, and coined money, and this was the first 
entrance into his great and princely estate." The coin is the telling detail. A man who 
holds a town by force needs nothing more to collect from it; the coin was addressed to 
the people who handled it, and it claimed the one thing force could not supply — that 
the man in the fortress was the rightful ruler whose name belonged on the money. 
Haedo, from the Spanish side, calls it tyranny; Leo, a Granadan Moor whose book 
reached Europe in Italian and English, calls it usurpation; the brothers’ own chronicle 
makes it a deliverance. The three witnesses stand in three positions and choose three 
words, and on the facts — the invitation, the failure against the fort, the killing of the 
host, the seizure of the government — they do not disagree. 

Aruj then carried the enterprise west, into the disordered kingdom of Tlemcen, and 
was killed there in 1518 when the Spaniards of Oran and the dynasty's restored 


claimant came against him. 


A stranger with no protector 


Khayr al-Din inherited a town taken by murder, a brother's crews, a coin with no

What the firman conveyed 


The envoy came back with the sultan’s standard and his firman, and the chronicle 
describes the scene in which the title was published. Khayr al-Din assembled the troops 
and the principal inhabitants and read aloud, with respect, the firman "de sa Hautesse 
impériale, qui déclarait qu'elle avait agréé la foi et l'hommage des habitans d'Alger; 
qu'elle leur permettait de faire le khoutbé en son nom et de battre monnaie a son coin; 
et qu'elle établissait enfin Khair-ed-din pour son lieutenant, avec le titre de bey.” His 
Imperial Highness declared that he had accepted the faith and homage of the 
inhabitants of Algiers; that he permitted them to preach the Friday sermon in his name 
and to strike money with his die; and that he established Khayr al-Din as his lieutenant, 
with the title of bey. 

Every clause should be read for what it made ownable, because this is the moment at 
which what Aruj had taken by a murder in a bath became something that could be held 
by right. The first clause made the town the sultan's, by its own petition, so that anyone 
who attacked it attacked the sultan. The second transferred to the sultan's name the 
two instruments by which a Muslim population recognised its rightful ruler — the 
instruments Aruj had tried to claim for himself with a coin nobody had authorised. 
The third made Khayr al-Din an officer: not a sovereign in his own right, and not a 
corsair holding a town, but the lieutenant of a sovereign, whose authority now derived 
from an appointment the whole coast could recognise. The title he received is itself a 
small historiographical problem — the chronicle says bey, modern reference works 
variously say sanjak bey or beylerbey — and it is left here as the chronicle gives it. 

What the firman did not convey is as important. It granted no land, because Khayr 
al-Din held no land to be confirmed in and sought none. It granted a lieutenancy — an 
office, revocable by the sultan who made it — and it granted the sermon and the coin. 
Modern reference works add that the sultan also sent troops, generally given as two 
thousand janissaries with artillery, together with the right to recruit volunteers in 
Anatolia; one tradition attaches the contingent of two thousand to the renewed 
Ottoman support of 1529 rather than to 1519, and the figure is carried here as 


reported, not as settled. If the right to recruit was granted in 1519, it answered precisely

the problem the siege of Bougie had exposed. Men shipped from Anatolia had no fields 
to go home to at sowing time. 

And the chronicle shows at once what the title could be used to collect. When the 
brothers’ forces took Tlemcen and seated a client there, Khayr al-Din’s instructions, as 
the chronicle gives them, were that the new sultan should wear a robe of honour but 
that the coin of the city be struck and the sermon preached in the name of Sultan 
Selim. When the client later broke away, the chronicle has Khayr al-Din demand the 
restoration of the sermon and the coin together with a yearly tribute of ten thousand 
ducats "pour l’entretien de mes troupes,” and describe it as a tribute lighter and less 
shameful than the one Tlemcen had previously paid to the unbelievers of Oran. The 
revenue that had flowed from Tlemcen to a Spanish garrison was to flow instead to 
Algiers — the same payment, from the same treasury, redirected by a title from the 
unbeliever's fort to the sultan's lieutenant and renamed from humiliation into lawful 


obligation. 


Who held what, in 1519 


A reader who knows nothing of the period can now take the inventory. Spain held 
the harbours — Oran, Mers el-Kébir, Bougie and the islet in front of Algiers — by 
fortified garrisons that collected tribute from the kingdoms and towns behind them, 
but it held little more than the reach of its guns. The dynasty of Tlemcen held a capital 
and a name and sent its revenue to the Spaniards who had broken it. The Hafsids of 
Tunis held a claim and some harbours, and rented the harbours for a fifth. The Arab 
tribes of the plains and the Berbers of the mountains held the ground, and lent their 
fighting men to whoever offered plunder, until the season took them home. The 
townsmen and men of law of Algiers held the consent that turned possession into rule, 
and had spent a generation selling it to the nearest credible protector. And the brothers 
— one dead, one alive — held a town taken by the murder of its governor, crews paid 
in prizes, and, from 1519, a title from the most powerful Muslim sovereign in the 
world making the survivor that sovereign's lieutenant, with the sermon and the coin in 
his master’s name. 

Beneath all of them lay the older order that none of them had made and every one 
of them depended on: the law, the sermon, the coin and the duty of holy war, which 
had been a conquest once and was by now simply the world. The brothers could not 
own it. What they could do, and did, was borrow its currency from the one man on 


earth whose credit in it was beyond question, and put his name on the money.

Reading the eve against the framework 


The first thing this ground forces is a correction to the question of where the 
challenger came upon organised violence. The received account, which I carried into 
the research, has a corsair seizing a city. The three witnesses, writing from three 
positions that share nothing — a Spanish churchman, a Granadan Muslim in Christian 
Italy, and the brothers’ own panegyrist — agree on something more specific: the 
brothers came as hired protection. The town had been buying its security for a 
generation from whoever could reach it, and in 1516 it hired Aruj against the Spanish 
fort on the same terms it had used before. The conqueror's violence entered this 
country as a wage paid by the people who would be conquered, and the conquest 
proper was the moment the contractor killed the employer and kept the contract. That 
is a route to power the framework should carry explicitly — the order founded by the 
protector the conquered hired — because it predicts a specific inheritance: an apex 
whose standing began as an employment and whose men would always know it. 

The second is what the warrant conveyed. The framework expects a warrant to 
anchor title, and usually title to ground, so that a settlement can be divided and made 
to stick. The firman of 1519 anchored title to no ground at all. It conveyed an office 
revocable by its grantor, the right to the sermon and the coin, and — as the Tlemcen 
demand shows within the same chronicle — the right to receive, as lawful obligation, a 
revenue that had previously gone to Spain as tribute. What was made ownable here was 
not land but a set of claims on payments, and the men who would divide the proceeds 
of this conquest were men who, like the brothers, held no fields. Whether an order can 
be settled on a warrant to flows alone, and what its holders would then have to lose, is 
the question every later part of this study has to answer from the evidence rather than 
assume. 

The third is a confirmation that carries a cost. Legitimacy on this coast was a 
currency nobody present could mint: the sermon and the coin in the name of a 
Muslim sovereign defending the faith. Aruj tried to strike his own and got nothing that 
outlived him. Khayr al-Din, who held the town by force and needed nothing further to 
tax it, nonetheless sent to Istanbul, asked in the inhabitants’ name to be admitted as a 
subject, and accepted a subordinate office revocable at the sultan's pleasure. An apex 
already holding the ground does not pay that price unless some audience whose 
compliance matters requires it, and the payment is the evidence. What the payment 
bought, and from whom it was bought, is also the thing the order would never be able 
to own outright.

A force that went home to sow, and a force that did not 


TE he brothers' first lesson on this coast was that the fighting men it could supply 

belonged to someone else. Tribesmen came to a siege for plunder and for the 
merit of fighting Christians, and went home when their fields called them; the captain 
who had raised them could not stop them, because he did not feed their families. 
Whatever else Khayr al-Din obtained from Istanbul in 1519, the thing that mattered 
most for what followed was a supply of men who had no fields in the country at all. 

The English version of Laugier de Tassy's history, published in 1750 from a French 
original written by a man who, catalogue descriptions say, served in the French 
consulate at Algiers in 1717-18, gives the arrangement as the Regency's own officials 
remembered it two centuries later. The sultan sent two thousand janissaries fully 
armed; and "the Ottoman Porte was very punctual in sending over Recruits every Year, 
with Money for the Payment" of the troops. In the first generation, then, the men who 
held Algiers came from the sultan and were paid, at least in part, from the sultan's 
purse. They were the sultan’s men lent to his lieutenant, and the lieutenant's other 
force — the corsair crews who had come west with the brothers and were paid in shares 
of what they took — was a separate body with a separate paymaster. 

It did not, at first, hold the country. For some years in the 1520s Algiers itself was 
lost to a Kabyle prince of the mountains, Ahmad ibn al-Qadi, who had fought beside 
the Turks and turned against them — an episode the brothers’ chronicle narrates at 
length and modern reference works date variously between 1520 and 1525 — and was 
recovered only when Ibn al-Qadi's own following was broken. The episode repeats, at a 
larger scale, what the siege of Bougie had already shown: an allied chief's men were the 
chief's, and could be turned against the ally who had borrowed them. What the Turks 


held securely was what their own men could garrison.

flogged and beheaded, before turning on Vargas himself. Both accounts end in the

anchorage from which the corsairs of the next three centuries sailed was built, in the 
most literal sense, out of the fortress that had forbidden them the sea. 

The composition of the force that took the islet is the finding. The Turks were more 
than a thousand and were the instrument; the Moors were a multitude and were 
numbers. The first were paid and had nowhere else to be; the second were allies, 
auxiliaries and townsmen who had a stake in the fort's destruction and would go home 
when it was done. The order that emerged would be built on the first and would treat 


the second as something to be kept out of it.

The rule that made any Turk a janissary 


The janissaries of Istanbul were a particular kind of soldier made by a particular 
institution. Haedo, describing them for Spanish readers, says what every European of 
his day knew: the corps at the Ottoman centre was filled with the sons of Christians 
taken as a levy in the sultan’s European provinces, raised and trained in his household, 
and made into his slaves and his soldiers. A janissary, in that setting, was a man with no 
kin but the sultan. 

Algiers had no such levy and no such household, and Haedo records what Khayr al- 
Din did instead. After Aruj's death, he says, Khayr al-Din, wishing to preserve the state 
and lordship of Algiers that his brother had won, wrote to the sultan, "auisando al 
Turco quan habierta tenia la puerta para sujetar toda la Africa, y destruyr las prouincias 
de la christiandad, conseruandose Argel en poder de los turcos sus vasallos" — advising 
the Turk how open a door he had to subject all Africa and destroy the provinces of 
Christendom, if Algiers were kept in the power of the Turks his vassals — and 
"facilmente alcango" — easily obtained — a remarkable concession. As Haedo goes on 
to describe it, any Turk who wished could pass freely from Turkey to Barbary and 
Algiers, and all such men, though they were not janissaries raised from Christian boys 
as the custom of Turkey required, might nevertheless, while in Algiers and its lands, be 
called janissaries and enjoy every franchise and liberty that the janissaries enjoyed in 
Turkey. 

That is the moment at which the corps of Algiers became a different thing from its 
model, and the difference is the most informative fact about it. The name and the 
privileges crossed the sea; the institution that made a janissary the sultan's creature — 
the levy of boys, the palace training, the absence of any bond but the one to the master 
— stayed behind. What Algiers built under the janissary name was a corps of free 
Muslim volunteers, adults when they enlisted, drawn from the poorer and more restless 
parts of Anatolia by the prospect of pay and privilege in a province where a soldier 
outranked every native. Their loyalty could not be denominated in a household, 
because there was none. It could only be denominated in what they were paid, and in 


the rank that determined how much.

Who paid, and so who owned 


The question the whole order turns on is who paid these men, because the men 
answered to their paymaster and the paymaster changed. 

In the first generation the sultan sent money with the recruits, and the corps was, in 
part, his. By Haedo's day it was paid by the "king" — the governor sent from Istanbul 
— in Algiers, and one of the corps's own officers had the standing duty of pressing him 
to pay. The corps chose and changed its own commanders by seniority, and a governor 
who did not pay faced a body of several thousand armed men who owed him nothing 
else. Modern accounts date the decisive rupture to 1659, when the corps, led. by its 
agha, seized the government from the sultan's governor after a quarrel over money the 
governor had kept back; what the corps took in that year was the treasury, and with it 
the right to pay itself. 

Ninety years later the corps wrote down what it had become. In 1749, after a year of 
disorders its own preamble describes, the officers of the corps assembled in divan and 
drew up an ahd al-aman, a covenant and regulation binding the whole body, which 
survives in a French version made by Devoulx's son from an Arabic translation of the 
Turkish and printed in the Revue africaine in 1860. It was attended, the text says at its 
close, by three hundred mazoul aghas, six hundred bouloukbachis and four hundred 
and twenty-four odabachis — retired aghas, company commanders and chamber chiefs, 
more than thirteen hundred officers in one room. Its members acknowledged their 
subordination to their own assembly in a single sentence: "Tout le passé est écarté et 
oublié. Nous reconnaissons que nous sommes sous les ordres du divan, et qu'il en sera 
ainsi chaque jour.” All the past is set aside and forgotten. We acknowledge that we are 
under the orders of the divan, and that it shall be so every day.

Two armed corporations 


The janissaries were not the only armed body in Algiers, and for most of the 
sixteenth century they were not on good terms with the other. 

The corsair captains commanded crews paid in shares of prizes, and the captains, 
many of them renegades from Christian Europe, formed a corporation of their own. 
Haedo says that for many years no corsair or renegade who was not a Turk by nation 
could be a janissary, and that the corsairs in turn refused to let janissaries sail with them, 
because the janissaries wanted the profits of the sea and the corsairs wanted to keep 
them. The quarrel was settled, he records, by Mehmed Pasha, son of Salah Rais, "Rey 
de Argel,” "reconciliando a los genizaros con los cosarios, porque sobre esto auia entre 
ellos muy grande enemistad,” reconciling the janissaries with the corsairs, because there 
was very great enmity between them over this, and it was ordered "que los genizaros 
pudiessen yr en los nauios de corso por soldados, y que todo cosario, o renegado 
quando quisiesse pudiesse entrar en el numero” — that janissaries might go aboard the 
corsair ships as soldiers, and that any corsair or renegade who wished might enter the 
number, and draw the pay, of the janissaries. The two corporations were joined by 
admitting each into the other's income. The janissary acquired a share of the sea; the 
corsair acquired a place on the pay register and the rank that came with it.

shows it moving in both directions within a few years. The same concession had been 
extended to Jews who converted; in December 1580, he says, at the petition of the

What the corps said it was 


Every armed body that holds a country tells itself a story about what it is, and the 
corps of Algiers told two, to two audiences, while its enemies told a third. 

To itself, in 1749, it was a brotherhood of believers under law. The regulation calls 
its members brothers and the victorious soldiers, prays for the sultan, commands the 
agha to judge by the noble law to the thickness of a hair split forty times even where the 
defendant is a heretic, and requires that an oppressed man who comes weeping to the 
door be heard and not sent away, on pain of answering for it on the day of resurrection. 
Its members marry, divorce, raise sons and daughters and grow old in the country’s 
service, the preamble says, sleeping on the ground with a stone for a pillow on 
campaigns by land and sea.

Reading the floor against the framework 


The first thing the evidence settles is ownership. In the first generation the sultan 
sent the men and money to pay them, and the corps was, in part, his. By the 1580s the 
corps was paid out of Algiers's own revenue by a governor from Istanbul whom it 
pressed for its money, and it chose and changed its own commanders by a rule of 
seniority. By 1749 it described the treasury as its own dwelling, guarded by its own 
officers, the source of its pay, and the country as its mine, and it acknowledged no 
authority over itself but its own assembly. Whoever pays a standing force owns it, and 
the history of who owned this one is the history of the paymaster moving from 
Istanbul's purse to the corps's own strongroom. That movement, not any single coup, 
is the constitutional history of the Regency, and the coups are its dated consequences. 

The second is what the corps was made of, and it corrects the common picture in a 
specific way. The janissary name crossed the sea and left behind the institution that 
made janissaries a sultan's household — the levy of Christian boys, the palace, the lack 
of any bond but the master. What Algiers had instead was a stream of adult volunteers 
from Anatolia, admitted by a concession Khayr al-Din obtained, and later corsairs and 
renegades admitted by a settlement between two armed corporations that joined them 
by sharing their incomes. A corps built that way could have no loyalty except to its pay, 
and its pay was its own. 

The third is a caution about turnover. The corps's commander changed three or 
four times a year in Haedo's day and six times a year in Venture’s, by rule, without a 
blow struck; the head of the state drew a soldier's closed pay. A very high rate of change 
at the top of such an order is not, by itself, evidence of violence, because much of it was 
designed into the pay ladder. Violent depositions will have to be counted separately, by 


name and manner of death, rather than inferred from the speed of succession.

What the conquest found, and what it took 


band of armed men who take a country must turn the taking into holdings, or 

they will have to go on taking every year by force. The first question is what 
there was to hold, and the answer on this coast shaped everything the men of the 
Regency could give one another. 

The brothers took a town, not a province. The ground around it was already 
owned, and owned under titles the conquerors had not issued and did not seek to 
overturn: houses, gardens and orchards held as private property under the Maliki law 
the town's qadis applied; tribal lands on the plains held by lineages whose right came 
from below and beside them, from kin and custom, rather than from any register; and 
a growing body of property endowed for pious purposes, the habous, held in perpetuity 
under the sacred law for mosques, shrines and the poor. None of these was simply 
seized and redistributed. The corps that grew out of the brothers’ following did not 
carve the Mitidja into estates for its officers, and the reachable sources show no general 
grant of conquered land to the conquerors at any date. 

What the conquerors took instead was the flow that passed through the town and 
the country — the tribute collected by armed column, the customs of the port, the 
ransom and sale of captives and cargoes, the payments of foreign powers — and they 
built their settlement out of claims on that flow. The American consul William Shaler, 
writing in the 1820s at the end of the order, gives the result as it looked from outside: 
"The lands of this kingdom are believed to be all taken up and enregistered, as far as the 
real jurisdiction of the government extends; though from the frequent and often 
arbitrary confiscations, the national domain must be immense." The domain of the

over three centuries. The instruments of the settlement proper were a register of pay, an 
account book of prizes, a set of offices that paid for themselves, and a judge who

different, and each can be tested by the only question that matters about a title: if the

The register 


The first and largest title in the order was a name on the pay register of the corps. A 
man admitted to it was paid a fixed sum every two moons, rising year by year with his 
seniority until he reached a ceiling, and his place in the queue of seniority carried him 
toward command. The head of the state himself, in the 1720s account, drew no more 
than a soldier's top rate, and made the rest of his income from the perquisites of his 
office. 

The register conveyed a title to a payment and a place, and the notes Venture de 
Paradis made on the city in the 1780s, printed in the Revue africaine in 1895-97, show 
exactly what became of that title when its holder died. "Lorqu'il [sc] meurt un homme 
de paye, soit qu'il soit marié soit qu'il soit gargon, le beit ulmalgi porte au dey son fusil 
avec son nom et son surnom. Le fusil se conserve dans la salle d'armes." When a man on 
the pay dies, whether he is married or a bachelor, the officer of the treasury of 
successions carries to the dey his musket with his name and surname. The musket is 
kept in the armoury. If he was a bachelor, Venture continues, the same officer sold 
everything that had belonged to him and took it into account for the treasury 
according to the conditions of his office. 

That is the whole title in one gesture. The musket was the corps's and went back to 
it; the name was struck from the list; nothing of the place passed to anyone. What a 
soldier could leave was only what he had bought with his pay outside the register, and 
that, as will be seen, lay under a claim of its own. Even the highest place conveyed 
nothing heritable. The Laugier account of the 1720s, in its English version of 1750, 
records the fate of a ruler killed by his own soldiers: "When a Dey is murdered with the 
Approbation of the People, his Wives are reduced to their first Condition, and his Sons 
to the Pay of a common Soldier, and rendered incapable of any Post.” A dey's son began 
again at the bottom of the register his father had headed. 

What the grant bought is plain and needs no argument: the soldier owed armed. 
service, and received pay for it. But the grant did not leave the soldier with a following 
of his own. He commanded no retinue that was his; the men beside him were paid by 
the same chest and answered to the same seniority. Only the corporation as a whole 
owned the force, and the corporation could therefore do what no single officer could 
— withdraw its service from a ruler and give it to another — while no member of it 


could carry away a piece of it.

One eighth, and half of what remained 


The second title was a share of the prizes taken at sea, and here the order's own 
paperwork survives. 

The rule was stated by the Laugier account in the 1720s: "The State has an eighth of 
all Prizes, both with regard to the Slaves, of whom the Dey has the Choice, and also the 
Cargo and Vessel." The rest, it says, was divided between the owners of the ship and its 
company. The register of maritime prizes kept by the Regency's own administration, 
found in 1830 and published by Albert Devoulx in the Revue africaine in 1871 and 
1872, shows the rule working half a century later. The second entry in the published 
register records a capture made by a rais commanding "le chebec du Pacha" — the 
ruler's own galley — in October 1765. The account opens with the deduction the 
register calls the "Bandjek,” of 372 rials, from a gross product the text gives as 2,976 
rials: exactly one eighth. There follow the fees for unloading, the captain of the prize, a 
premium for boarding, the chaouches and servants of the detachment, the 
moneychangers, the guards, the auctioneers, the port; and then the register states the 
remainder — "Reste ... 2.420" — and divides it: "Dont moitié ... 1.210," of which half; 
"Nombre des parts : 311. Montant de chaque part : 3 rials et 7 huitiémes." Number of 
parts, three hundred and eleven; value of each part, three rials and seven eighths. 

The register's structure is itself a statement of title. The state took its eighth before 
anything else, and the ruler chose his captives first. The costs of the port and of the 
officials who handled the prize came next, each a small share of the flow attached to an 
office. Then what remained was cut in half, and one half was divided into shares among 
the men who had sailed, according to their rank, while the other half went to whoever 
owned the ship. A note at the head of the register, datable to 1797 and added to over 
the following years, lists the ships of the corsair fleet with the number of shares each 
carried, and records a refinement that was later abandoned: "Ces parts, dites grandes 
parts, étaient prélevées en dehors des parts ordinaires, et allouées au capitaine et 4 
quelques officiers. Plus tard ce systéme fut aboli." These shares, called great shares, were 
levied outside the ordinary shares and allotted to the captain and some officers; later 
this system was abolished. 

The owners named in the register are of three kinds: ships of the beylik, the state; 
ships belonging to the dey in person; and ships of private owners, the armateurs. The 
owner's half was the nearest thing the ruling order had to private capital put to work, 
and it came with an obligation that the Laugier account calls a principle of state. 
"When a Ship is lost, or taken, the Owners are obliged to purchase, or build another of 
equal Force, that the Strength of the Republic may suffer no Diminution. This is an 


indispensible Maxim.” The shipowner's title was real and could be sold or left to heirs;

but it was conditional on keeping the republic's force at sea at its strength, which is to 
say that even the one form of private capital at the centre of the order was held on the 


consideration of armed service. 


Offices that paid for themselves 


The third title was an office, and the offices of the Regency were, almost without 
exception, concessions that paid the treasury for the right to collect. 

The clearest instance is the officer who handled successions. Venture de Paradis lists 
him among the ministers: "Beit ulmalgi : Ali, chargé de recueillir la succession de tous 
ceux qui meurent sans héritiers. C’est une espéce de ferme, et il paie au beilik 500 
pataques chiques” — Ali, charged with collecting the estates of all who die without 
heirs; it is a kind of farm, and he pays the beylik five hundred pataques chiques — a 
payment the text makes weekly. Shaler, forty years later, says the same office "pays into 
the public treasury a sum two thirds as large as what is paid by the principal Beys,” and 
his account of the treasury's receipts for 1822 lists the payment beside those of the 
governors of the provinces: the beys of Oran and Constantine each assessed at sixty 
thousand Spanish dollars, the bey of Titteri at four thousand, seven qaids at sixteen 
thousand, the officer of successions at forty thousand, the shaykh of the town at three 
thousand, the master of the hides at four thousand, assessed, the table says, upon his 
office. 

An office held on those terms is a title to the difference between what the holder 
could collect and what he had to remit, and it lasted as long as the ruler who conferred 
it was content. Venture records what happened when it ended for a provincial 
governor. When a bey died, the agha was sent to seize the treasury and everything in the 
government house — money, furniture, tents, slaves, horses — and "si le bey est marié, 
il n'y a que la maison des femmes qui soit respectée et les biens habous, mais encore 
c'est toujours une grace particuliére du dey." If the bey was married, only the women's 
house was respected, and the endowed property — and even that was always a 
particular favour of the dey. The Laugier account adds that beys summoned to Algiers 
were often unsure whether they would be received with honour or lose their heads, a 
fate they sometimes drew on themselves by their extortions and that sometimes served 


as a pretext for confiscating their riches. 


The judge who inherited from the heirless 


A man of the ruling order could buy houses, gardens and goods with his pay, his 


shares and his perquisites, and those holdings were his under the ordinary law, to be

divided among his heirs at his death. But the law had a residuary heir, and the residuary 
heir was the state. 

The Laugier account describes the office in the 1720s under the name of the 
Pitremelgi. "Upon the Death of any one, having no Child or Brother, the Pitremelgi 
seizes his whole Substance, paying the Widow her Jointure." He had the houses of the 
dead man searched, in town and country, for hidden gold and silver, and the account 
explains why men hid it. If a private person was reported to be rich, "the Dey certainly 
casts his Eye upon him, exacting Money under Pretence of the Necessities of the State, 
or for any frivolous Trespass. And sometimes confiscates his whole Estate, both real and 
personal, on a forged Suspicion of having engaged in a Conspiracy." Rich men, it 
continues, therefore preferred to be thought poor and buried their treasure against the 
day they might have to flee. 

A century later Shaler found the office grown into a ministry. The bet el-mal, he 
says, "inherits, ex officio, the estates of all who die intestate, and without legal heirs," 
and his authority ran through the whole kingdom. It was the officer of successions, not 
the dey's sword, who did most of the work of turning private fortunes back into the 
domain of the state, and he did it lawfully, one heirless death at a time, while paying the 


treasury for the privilege. 


A transaction between two titles 


There was one title the officer of successions could not touch, and the owners of 
property in Algiers learned to move their holdings into it. 

Shaler describes the practice in the sentence that follows his account of the office. 
The magistrate "is justly dreaded; and in order to elude it, it is a common practice in 
this country to make donations" — of estates, by will, to the Kaaba at Mecca — "in 
default of legal heirs: this arrangement completely frustrates the claims of the Bet el 
Mel, and the holy city maintains an agent here to superintend the great revenues which 
have been thus” acquired. 

This is a transfer from one kind of title into another, and it is the most 
consequential transaction in the settlement because it ran in one direction only. A 
house or a garden held as private property lay under two claims the ruling order could 
exercise: the claim of the officer of successions if its owner died without heirs, and the 
claim of confiscation if its owner fell under suspicion. The same house endowed as 
habous passed under a warrant that no dey had issued and no dey could revoke — the 
sacred law, administered by the religious establishment, and in the case Shaler describes 
attached to the holy cities of Arabia themselves, with an agent in Algiers to collect their 


rents. Venture's note on the fallen bey shows that the endowed property of even a

disgraced governor was, by custom, respected, though custom here depended on the 
dey's forbearance. 

The practice is visible at the end of the order in the pattern of ownership the French 
found. An article on urban property in Algiers printed in the Revue africaine in 1897 
lists the corporations that held endowed property at the conquest as seven: the 
foundation of Mecca and Medina, which it calls the most important; the mosques; the 
marabouts; the Andalusians; the janissaries; the roads; and the waters. The janissaries 
themselves, that is, held an endowment corporation — the men whose titles died with 
them had placed a part of their wealth beyond the reach of their own state. 

The significance of this is not that the Regency was despoiled. It is that over three 
centuries the durable property of the capital moved, transaction by transaction and in 
perfect legality, out from under the titles the ruling order could resume and into a title 
it could not, administered by men who were not of the corps and in part on behalf of a 
holy city across the sea. No decree records the migration and no ruler ever had occasion 
to notice it, because each donation was a private act of piety by a man who wished his 


estate to go to God rather than to the officer of successions. 


Some ground after all 


The hypothesis that there was nothing heritable at all in the ruling order fails in one 
specific place, and the failure is instructive. 

The same 1897 volume, describing the districts immediately around Algiers under 
the last dey, says that each canton was made up of rural estates, haouch — sometimes 
country houses, sometimes farms, sometimes hamlets of poor people — and that 
"Beaucoup de ces haouch étaient aux mains des fonctionnaires de la cour ou des 
familles de janissaires," many of these estates were in the hands of court officials or of 
janissary families, while others belonged to petty marabouts who made an income from 
their constant intercession with Turkish officials. The families of the corps, then, did 
hold land, in the one belt of countryside the state's power reached every day. What they 
held it under is not stated there. If it was private property, it lay under the officer of 
successions and the threat of confiscation like any other; if it was endowed, it had 
passed out of the corps's reach in the way Shaler describes. Either way it was held under 


a law the corps did not make, and not under the register that made a man a member. 


Inside and outside 


The marker of membership in this settlement was a name on the pay register, and 


the register's boundary was drawn against the country. Admission was for Turks, and

at times for renegades and for the sons of the corps; the Moors and Arabs, as the 
Laugier account says flatly, were excluded. The Arabic- and Berber-speaking majority 
could own houses and gardens, could endow them, could hold tribal land by custom 
and could serve the state as auxiliaries, and the French administrative literature of the 
nineteenth century describes tribes that rode for the beys in return for exemption from 
tribute. But no man of the country held a claim on the chest in the kasba, and it was 


the chest, not the land, that the settlement was built on. 


Reading the settlement against the framework 


The first thing the instruments show is what each title was to. The register conveyed 
a payment and a place in a queue, and died with its holder: the musket went back to the 
armoury, the name came off the list, and a murdered dey's sons began again on a 
common soldier's pay. The prize account conveyed a share of a flow — an eighth to the 
state, costs to the officers of the port, half the rest to the ship's company by shares, half 
to the owner — and the owner's half was held on condition of keeping the fleet at 
strength. The offices conveyed the right to collect in return for a fixed remittance, and 
ended at the ruler's pleasure with the seizure of everything in the government house. 
Private property existed, and passed to heirs, under a law the conquerors had not 
issued; but the state was its residuary heir and its confiscator. And the endowment 
conveyed a title no ruler could resume. 

The second is what this does to the question the case was chosen to test. At its core 
the settlement was as predicted: every position in the ruling corporation was revocable, 
and nothing of the place passed to a son. But it does not follow that the members had 
nothing to lose. What each man held was a claim on a system of positions that would 
outlast him if the corporation did — a rising rate of pay, a seniority that carried him 
toward command, a share in every prize his ship took, a chance at an office that paid for 
itself. None of it was heritable and all of it was institutional. The men of the corps 
could not pass their places to their sons, but they could be entirely confident that the 
register, the account book and the schedule of offices would be there next year, and that 
confidence, rather than any inheritance, is what they had to defend. 

The third is the transaction between titles, which is where this settlement actually 
moved. Property that the ruling order could reclaim through the officer of successions 
or by confiscation was transferred, in perfect legality and one death at a time, into 
endowments it could not touch, administered by the religious establishment and in 
part for the holy cities of Arabia. The corps's own members, and a corporation of the 


janissaries itself, took part. The durable wealth of the capital accumulated for three 


PAT TERNS OF POWER 43 THE REGENGY OF ALGIERS 


centuries under a warrant no dey had issued, while the titles the dey could issue all 
expired with their holders. 

The fourth is the consideration. Every title at the centre of the order was granted for 
armed service — the soldier's pay, the ship's shares, the owner's obligation to replace a 
lost vessel. Yet the grant never left a grantee with a following of his own, because the 
force was paid by one chest and ranked by one queue; the only body that owned a 
retinue was the corporation itself. That is why, in this order, no officer could rise 
against a ruler with his own men, and why every change of ruler that came by force 
came as an act of the corporation. 

And last, what I expected and did not find. I expected a ruling order with no landed 
holdings whatever. The families of the corps held rural estates in the belt around the 
capital, and the corps as a body held endowed property of its own. What they did not 
hold was any land under the register that made them members. Their ground lay under 
the law of the country they ruled, and the most durable part of it lay under the law of 
God.

Four audiences 


T he men who held Algiers needed four different kinds of people to accept that 
they held it rightfully, and no single claim would serve all four. 

They needed the Ottoman court, because their recruits came from Anatolia and 
could be stopped there. They needed the Muslim population of the city and the plain, 
and the qadis and preachers who spoke for it, because the prize taken at sea was lawful 
spoil only if it was taken in a lawful war by the servants of a Muslim sovereign, and 
because a townsman’s obedience ran to the name pronounced in the Friday sermon. 
They needed themselves — the corps whose members elected, deposed and served 
under one another — to agree on what made a dey a dey. And they needed the 
Christian powers of Europe to treat Algiers as a state that could make a peace and keep 
it, because peace was what those powers paid for. 

Each audience honoured a different currency. What follows reads the claims made 
to each on the hardest surfaces that survive, in descending order of how difficult they 


are to revise: the coin, the stone over a door, the chancery document, and the letter. 


On the metal 


The coinage of the Regency is catalogued today under the names of Ottoman 
sultans. The modern Numista catalogue of the Regency's issues runs from a copper 
struck in the name of Ahmed I in the year 1012 of the hijra (1604) to the gold, silver 
and billon of Mahmud II in the 1820s, and it translates the legend of one of Mahmud's 
Algerian silver pieces as "Sultan of the lands and Emperor of the seas, Sultan Mahmud 
Khan." Every entry I found in it is attributed to a sultan, and none to a dey. For three 
centuries the men who were paid every two moons in the dey's house, weighed and 
counted before the dey and the agha, were paid in money that carried the name of a 
sovereign in Istanbul and no other. 

That is the claim made to everyone at once, without exception and without 


interruption, and it is worth stating what it did. The pay of the corps, the price of a

Over the doors 


The stones of the city said something different, because they were addressed to a

The firman every dey asked for 


The claim that mattered most was made in a chancery document, and it was made at

The corps took the government from the sultan’s governors in the seventeenth

Why the warrant could not be set aside 


Every title the corps held ran back to that investiture. 

The janissary's privileges were, from the start, the privileges of the sultan's 
janissaries, extended to the volunteers of Algiers by the sultan's concession. The prize 
was lawful because it was taken in a war waged under a Muslim sovereign against those 
who did not hold his peace, and the register of prizes the Regency kept speaks of its 
captives as unbelievers. The pay was struck in the sultan's coin. The dey's authority 
over the provinces, the gadis and the townsmen was, on the face of the firman, the 
sultan's authority lent. And the men themselves came from the sultan's lands. A corps 
that renounced him would not merely have lost a distant patron; it would have made 
every one of those titles a claim resting on nothing but its own swords, and it would 
have cut itself off from the only place its soldiers came from. 

Twice in the reachable record the warrant was tested against the Regency's own 
interest, and twice it held. 

The first test concerned the recruiting ground itself. Devoulx, commenting on an 
inscription of the dey Hajji Ali (1809-1815), records that during a long and

opposed the recruitment of the Algerian militia, which weakened the corps 
considerably. Hajji Ali was strangled in his bath by a servant the conspirators had won 
over. The lever the Porte held was not a fleet but a door: the Anatolian ports through 
which the corps renewed itself.

Egypt, an Ottoman province, and Algiers was at peace with France, whose trading 
posts it hosted on its eastern coast. Plantet's edition, drawing on the French consular

Algiers accompanied by a caftan of investiture; a second firman was delivered to the 
divan on 22 November. The sultan ordered his vassal Mustapha to declare war on the

bientét débarquer un Capidji Bachi, aux sommations duquel ils furent contraints

The face shown to Europe 


To the Christian powers the Regency presented itself as a sovereign, and its rulers 
wrote to kings as equals. 

When Louis XVIII died in 1824, Hussein Dey wrote to his successor Charles X to 
condole and to confirm the treaties between the two governments, and he referred to 
the dead king, in the French version Plantet printed, as "vétre sincére et illustre frére, le 
Padischah de France” — your sincere and illustrious brother, the Padishah of France. 
The dey spoke to Paris in the vocabulary of the Ottoman court, gave the king of France 
the title of the sultan, and called him brother. 

The European reading of that persona, which became the standard one, set it 
against the language of holy war and found a contradiction: a nest of raiders at war 
with Christendom that nonetheless signed treaties and expected them kept. The 
warrant document shows why no contradiction was felt in Algiers. The same firman 
that calls the Algerines the foremost champions of the camp of the faith and their 
captains lions of holy war makes the governorship conditional on doing no harm to the 
powers that held the aman — the safe-conduct that the law of war itself provides for, 
by which an enemy becomes, for a term and on conditions, a protected party. War at 
sea against those who had no peace and peace with those who had purchased it were 
not two policies but one law, and the corps's own covenant of 1749 bore the same word 
in its title. A treaty with Algiers was, from the Algerian side, a grant of aman — which 


is also why it could be priced. 


What the robe could not claim 


None of these claims made the corps the rightful authority in the eyes of the 
country's own people in the way their saints and scholars were. The sultan’s name was a 


currency the coast had honoured before the Turks came, and the corps borrowed it.

Holy war against the Christian by sea was a duty the population already recognised, 
and the corps performed it. What no claim in this order asserted was that the men of 
the corps were themselves holy, or of a sacred lineage, or the natural heads of the 
country's tribes and towns; those claims belonged to other men, and the corps never

Reading the robe against the framework 


The first thing the evidence shows is which of the order's claims was load-bearing. 
There were at least four, addressed to four audiences: the coin and the firman to 
everyone and to Istanbul; the stones of mosques to townsmen and soldiers, sometimes 
naming the sultan and sometimes only the dey's royalty and God; the covenant to the 
corps; and the letters to Europe, in which the dey was a sovereign writing to his brother 
kings. Of these, only one was the source of every title in the order — the sultans 
investiture, carried on the coinage in which every man was paid, pronounced in the 
sermon, renewed by firman for each dey, and extended to the corps's privileges and the 
lawfulness of its prizes. The others were local or diplomatic dress. That one was the 
warrant. 

The second is that the warrant bound the men who held it. The corps could depose 
and kill deys, and did; it treated the sultan's envoys coldly; it was, as the English account 
said, independent in everything it chose to be. But it could not renounce the sultan, 
and in the reachable record it never did. When a dey defied him, the Porte closed the 
recruiting ports and the dey did not survive. When the sultan ordered war on France 
against every Algerian interest, a single officer landing with a written order was enough 
to chain the French consul in the bagnio. The claim that made every man's title good 
could not be set aside when it was inconvenient, because setting it aside would have 
made every title a matter of force alone. 

The third concerns the date the Regency is usually said to have become 
independent. The union of dey and pasha in 1710 was not an avowal of independence 
but a purchase: the dey expelled the sultan’s governor and then asked the sultan, with 
presents, for the governor's title, which made him the sultan's pasha as well as the 
corps's elect. From then on the fiction was fixed in a form both sides could live with — 
an election performed in Algiers and described in Istanbul as a petition for an 
appointment that ancient custom reserved to the Porte. The document says both 
things in one sentence. That is not hypocrisy; it is the instrument by which a corps that 
elected its own head could still derive his authority from somewhere its own swords did

There is no unformed child 


E very child born in Algiers, on the plain or in the mountains between 1516 and 
1830 received, before he was old enough to examine any of it, a complete 
account of the world. He was told who made it and what He required; which book 
held the truth and in which language; at what hours a man stops what he is doing to 
pray, and in which month he goes without food from dawn to dusk; which men have 
God's blessing in their blood and whose word may be taken without asking for proof; 
what is clean and what is unclean, what is obvious and what is unthinkable. No child 
in the Regency grew up without such an account. The only question is who gave it to 
him, and whether the men who held the country by force were among those who did. 
The answer, which the sources give from several positions that have nothing else in 
common, is that they were not — and that this cost them almost nothing for three 
hundred years, because the account the country gave its children already contained the 


things the corps needed them to believe. 


The board, the rod and the Book 


The institution that caught the child first, after his mother, was the Qur'an school, 
and the two foreign residents who described it a century apart describe the same thing. 

The Laugier account of the 1720s counts, in the city of Algiers, ten large mosques 
and fifty small ones and "three Colleges and public Schools, besides those for Children, 
which are without Number." Children, it says, were taught to read and write at the 
same time, as all over the Levant: the master drew the lesson and the scholars traced it, 
learning the pronunciation as they went, and a boy's faults were punished by the 
bastinado on the soles of his feet, fastened in a wooden frame while he sat on the mat. 

William Shaler, the American consul, saw the same schools in the 1820s and 
described their method with a care that is itself revealing. "Common schools are 
however, numerous in Algiers, where boys of the age of five or six years and upwards, 


are taught to read and write." Each scholar had a board on which a lesson from the

Qur'an was written in chalk and copied by the rest, the boys teaching one another the 
text and the forms of its letters. "These lessons are loudly rehearsed to the pedagogue, 
who sits upon his heels in a corner with a long rod," and "The education of the 
Algerine youth is completed when, having learnt to read and write the Koran," he has 
been taught by the same master the forms of prayer. The cost, he says, was very small, 
and "similar schools are kept by women for the instruction of young girls." 

Read without a verdict, those sentences describe a system of great completeness. It 
caught boys and girls at five or six, in every quarter of the city, at a price any family 
could pay, staffed by men and women of the city itself. It taught one text, aloud, in 
Arabic, by repetition in chorus before a master with a rod, and it declared the child's 
education finished when he could read and write that text and perform the prayer 
correctly. A boy who left such a school had not been given a range of subjects. He had 
been given the one thing his world required: the Book in his mouth and hand, the 
forms of worship in his body, and the habit of taking both from a master who had 
them from his own. 

Shaler, having described it, drew the conclusion his own formation drew: "I think 
there can be no doubt that these people stand on the very brink of civilization.” The 
Spanish captive whose dialogues fill part of Haedo's Topographia had drawn a sharper 
version of the same conclusion two hundred and forty years earlier. Speaking to a Moor 
named Amud, the Christian mocks the learned men of Algiers for their ignorance of 
the book they recited — "vuestro Alchoran (que a penas se halla vno de todos vuestros 
letrados que le entienda, escusandose que esta escrito en lengua Arauiga antigua),” your 
Alcoran, which scarcely one of all your learned men understands, excusing themselves 
that it is written in the ancient Arabic tongue. Both witnesses saw recitation without 
analysis and called it failure. A system that aimed at formation rather than inquiry 
would have seen the same thing and called it success: a population that held its sacred 
text by heart, reverently, without needing to argue its meaning, had received the text in 


the only form in which it could not be taken from them.

The men who held the country were formed somewhere else 


Against this completeness stands a striking absence. The men of the corps were not 
formed in any of these institutions, and the order they served did not try to form 
anyone in its own. 

They arrived as grown men from Anatolia, recruited for pay, already made by 
whatever villages and towns had raised them. The Laugier account describes them as 
outlaws and desperate men, which is the tribute-paying consulates’ view of them, but 
whatever they were, they were adults, and their picture of the world had been put into 
them before they ever saw Algiers. What the corps added was not a picture of the world 
but a code of rank — a register, a seniority, a chamber, a brotherhood sworn in 
covenant — and it added it to men whose childhood belonged to someone else. 

Nor did the corps make learning a qualification for rule. Shaler writes that in former

without having ever learned to read," had decided that any man of common sense who 
could read the Qur'an was fit for the bench. Pierre Dan, writing in the 1630s, records 
that the business of the divan was proposed and decided in Turkish, which every officer 
admitted to it had to speak — a rule he explains as a declaration that the corps held 
from the Ottoman empire. Turkish was a wall around the corps, not a language it

The field the corps left alone 


Why did an order that held the country for three centuries never try to teach it 
anything? 

From where the men of the corps stood, there was nothing to gain by it and much 
to lose. To form the country's children in the corps's image would have been to make 
them eligible for the corps — to open the register, and with it the pay chest in the 
Kasba, to the population whose exclusion from it was the settlement's first rule. And 
the formation the country already gave its children did the corps's work for nothing. 
The Qur'an school taught the obligation to obey a ruler who upheld the sacred law; the 
mosque pronounced the Ottoman sultan's name each Friday; the law of holy war that 
made a captured Christian lawful spoil was the law the children learned to honour; and 
the corps held its authority by the sultan's investiture and paid itself in his coin. A 
population formed by its own scholars, saints and mothers to recognise a Muslim 
sovereign, and to defer to those who upheld his law, would accept the men who wore 
his title as long as they wore it — without needing to believe anything whatever about 
the men themselves. 

What that population was not formed to do was love the corps, defend it, or regard 
its members as its own natural leaders. Those places in a child's picture of the world 
were already taken — by God and His Prophet, by the saint and his lineage, by the 
scholar who held the Book, by the head of the family and the elders of the tribe. The 
corps appeared in that picture only as the occupant of a borrowed office, and a child 
formed that way would grow into a man who could see the office as rightful and its 


current occupants as foreigners in the same glance.

Reading formation against the framework 


The first thing the evidence shows is that formation in the Regency was complete, 
ancient and belonged entirely to others. The Qur'an school caught children of both 
sexes at five or six in numbers nobody could count; the endowed religious house paid 
its teachers from property no dey could touch and reserved its teaching to a sacred 
lineage; the marabout's word was taken because he said it; and the hours and months of 
every life in the city were set by the call to prayer and the lunar year. The rulers stood 
outside all of it. They were grown men when they arrived, and the only formation they 
gave anyone was a code of rank given to adults. 

The second is that this was withheld formation of a particular kind, and the kind 
decided its price. The corps declined to form the country because forming it would 
have meant admitting it to the pay register, and it said so in the rule that excluded 
Moors and Arabs from its ranks and in the rule it made against its own sons. But the 
formation it left in place was not a rival to the claim the corps held. It taught the 
country to honour the sultan whose title the corps wore and the sacred law under 
which its prizes were lawful. A conqueror who withholds his own formation from a 
population whose existing formation already honours his warrant pays very little for 
the omission, for a very long time. The cost appears only when the warrant fails, 
because then nothing in the population's picture of the world attaches it to the men 
who were wearing it. 

The third is the corps's own children. The admissions rule against the kouloughlis 
was a rule about formation made into law: the corps recognised that it could not form 
its own sons, that their mothers and their mothers’ world had done it first, and it kept 
them from the Kasba accordingly. 

The fourth corrects the witnesses. Two hostile observers, a Spanish captive around 
1580 and an American consul in the 1820s, described the recitation of the Qur'an by 
men who did not analyse it as ignorance, and a population's trust in its holy men as 
credulity. Both described a completed formation and mistook it for a failed education. 
What they saw was the finished article: a people holding its sacred text in memory and 
its authorities in reverence, beyond the reach of argument, which is the condition in 
which neither can be taken from them. 

And last, what I expected and did not find. I expected the rulers to court the 
religious establishment from outside, buying the loyalty of saints and scholars as 
instruments. They did pay them. But the corps's own covenant of 1749 instructs its 
members to receive the letters of the holy marabouts with respect and to follow their 
counsel punctually, for the sake of the prayers the marabouts would say for them. The 


men who held the country were not managing a belief from above. They were inside

the same field, taking the same men's word, and they wrote their deference into their 


own constitution.

A schedule of deaths 


T he American consul William Shaler, writing of Algiers in the 1820s, set down 

the Regency’s punishments for capital crimes in a single paragraph, and the 
paragraph sorts the population more exactly than any census. "A Turk is strangled, 
privately if for a political crime ; a native is hanged, decapitated, mutilated,” or thrown 
from a high wall set with hooks, unless the executioner was paid to strangle him first; 
and "A Jew is either hanged, decapitated, or burned alive." Lesser offences brought 
fines, the bastinado or labour in chains. And the whole system ran, Shaler says, on a 
maxim the reverse of his own country’s: "that it is better to punish an innocent person, 
than that a criminal should escape." 

That paragraph is the cost side of a calculation that every man under the corps 
performed for himself, and it already shows that the calculation came out differently 
depending on who was performing it. The Turk died out of sight. The native died in 
public, in ways meant to be seen. The Jew might die by fire. What follows takes the 
calculation one kind of man at a time — what refusal would cost him, whether that 
cost was credible for a man in his place, what compliance got him that he himself 
valued, and whether he could leave — because the differences between those sums are 


what held the Regency together. 


The size of the instrument 


Before the individual sums, a measurement. The corps that governed the country 
was never large. Around 1580 Haedo counted up to six thousand janissaries in the 
whole kingdom; the Laugier account of the 1720s gives twelve thousand "Turks in 
Pay,” a figure that includes every official and sea captain. Shaler, a century later, judged 
that the population of the kingdom "must be rather under than over a million of 
souls,” and the city of Algiers itself at no more than half the hundred thousand earlier 
writers had claimed. The dates of these figures do not match, and none of them is a 


count; but on any combination of them the corps stood at somewhere between one

armed man for every eighty inhabitants and one for every two hundred and fifty, and 
those men were concentrated in the capital, a few provincial garrisons and the annual 
tax columns. 

And most of the country was not governed at all. A French description of the 
kingdom under its last dey, compiled from the Arab bureaux and printed in the Revue 
africaine in 1897, divided the land France later administered into the territories of the 
Regency’s subjects and servants and the territories of those who owned no master. By 
its reckoning, at the beginning of 1830 the "Turcs n'étaient les maitres incontestés que 
sur 7,825,000 hectares," the Turks were uncontested masters of only 7,825,000 
hectares, while the independent groups — the Kabyle confederations of the mountains 
and the nomads of the Sahara — held "une superficie approximative supérieure 4 
35,000,000 d'hectares,” and a further territory was held by vassals and allies with 
arrangements of their own. That is a French administrator's arithmetic, drawn with the 
borders France later claimed, and it should not be pressed. But it agrees with Shaler, 
who says of the Kabyles that they "have always maintained their independence of the 
Algerine government." 

A few thousand soldiers, most of the land beyond their reach, and a capital Shaler 
thought one of the most orderly cities in the world: whatever held this order up, it was 
not force spread thinly over a population. The individual sums show where the force 


was concentrated and where something else did the work. 


The townsman 


The Muslim townsman of Algiers — shopkeeper, artisan, owner of a house and a 
garden — ran the most favourable sum in the country. 

The cost of refusal was severe and credible. The hooks at Bab Azoun were in his 
daily sight, the maxim that it was better to punish the innocent than let the guilty go 
meant that suspicion was dangerous, and Shaler describes a police of unusual vigilance: 
"There is probably no city in the world, where there is a more vigilant police, where 
fewer cognizable crimes are committed, or where there is better security for person and 
property than in Algiers." That sentence is both halves of the townsman's sum. The 
same police that made refusal costly made compliance valuable, because what it 
delivered to the man who complied was the security of his person, his shop and his 
house. 

It delivered more than that, on the townsman's own terms. The corps did not touch 
the world his formation had given him: his mosque, his school, his qadi applying the 
law of his school, his endowed shrine, the saints whose word he took, the hours of 


prayer by which the dey himself kept time. It did not tax his conscience or remake his

children. It asked him to make way in the street for a Turk, to watch Turkish soldiers 
help themselves to the fruit of the gardens around the city, which Shaler says they 
believed was their right from time immemorial, and to accept exclusion from the pay 
chest. Shaler, who disliked the corps, records that even this last demand had ceased to 
feel like a demand: "the proposition which has been inculcated during a succession of 
ages, that Turks are born to command, and the natives of Algiers to obey, has been by 
time disrobed of every thing odious in its character, and has long since been received 
here as a political axiom." That is an American's reading of other men's minds, and it 
may overstate. But it describes a man for whom compliance cost little he valued and 
secured almost everything he did. 

No witness reached here records any rule preventing a Muslim townsman from 


leaving the city or the kingdom. He did not need to be kept; he had little reason to go. 


The Jew 


The Jew of Algiers ran a sum in which both columns were set by the order explicitly, 
and set far apart. 

The compliance column was real. Shaler records that the Jews of the city "have the 
free exercise of their religion secured ; they are governed by their own laws in civil cases, 
administered by a chief of their own nation, who is appointed by the Bashaw ; as 
Algerine subjects they may circulate freely, establish themselves where they please, and 
exercise any lawful calling throughout the kingdom ; and they cannot be reduced to 
slavery.” They were the city's brokers and money-dealers and the only artificers in the 
mint. Compliance bought a Jew a legal existence, a community under its own law, a 
livelihood the order needed, and the certainty of not being enslaved. 

The cost column was unlimited, and it did not wait for refusal. Beyond their legal 
disabilities, "they are in Algiers a most oppressed people ; they are not permitted to 
resist any personal violence of whatever nature, from a Mussulman." They wore dark 
clothing, could not ride or carry arms, not even a cane, and were turned out for any 
sudden labour — in the locust summer of 1815, Shaler says, several hundred were set 
to guard the dey's gardens day and night. When the janissaries mutinied, the Jews were 
plundered indiscriminately, and they lived in fear of its happening again; "they are 
pelted in the streets even by children." A Jew’s refusal had nothing to gain; even his 
compliance did not protect him from a Muslim's blows, and his death, if it came by 
law, might come by fire. 

And his exit was a policy, not a road. The Jews, Shaler writes, "are permitted only on 
Saturdays and Wednesdays to pass out of the gates of the city without permission.” A 


population the order needed for its money and its mint was allowed out of the city on

two days in seven and otherwise only by leave. The one exit Shaler describes as regular 
was the last one: aged and infirm Jews, he says, would settle their estates on their heirs

Those who ate and those who were eaten 


Outside the walls the sum depended on which side of a single line a tribe stood. The 
1897 description divides the governed countryside into "RAYAT ou sujets et du AHL 
EL MAKHEZENE ou gens du gouvernement, c'est-a-dire guerriers, fonctionnaires

summed up the regime before 1830: "des mangeurs et des mangés,” the eaters and the 
eaten. The phrase comes to us through French officials with every reason to paint the 
Turks black, and should be heard that way. But the line it describes is the one the sum 
turned on. 

For a man of a makhzan tribe, compliance was the benefit. His tribe rode for the 
bey, collected from others, held land on government terms and was spared the tribute; 
he was, in the phrase, among the eaters. Refusal would have cost him precisely that — 
his tribe's place on the right side of the line — and made him one of those his tribe had 
been collecting from. What held the makhzan tribesman was not the fear of the 
column but the value of riding with it. 

For a man of a subject tribe, the cost was the column itself, and its credibility 
depended on distance. The companies that went out every season to collect from the 
tribes — which Haedo described, as a Spanish churchman, as a rich corsairing by land, 
living off what they took and taking by force what was not given — came to the plains 
around the capital and the provincial towns reliably and regularly. For a tribe within 
their reach, refusal meant the column's return with the makhzan cavalry beside it, and 
compliance bought the column's departure. What compliance did not buy was 
anything the tribesman valued positively from the order, beyond being left alone until 
the next season. 

His exit was the one geography offered and the order did not close. Beyond the 
governed plain lay the mountains and the desert, where, the 1897 description says, the 
Turks, having recognised the impossibility of subduing certain federated republics and 
dynastic lordships, resigned themselves to living beside them, raiding them when an 
injury needed avenging or a profitable blow was possible, and otherwise taxing their 


people when they came down to trade in the towns and markets.

The Kabyle and the stranger 


For the man of an independent confederation, the sum came out differently again, 
because the order's cost could hardly reach him at home. Among the Kabyles, Shaler 
was told, safety of person and property depended chiefly on the strength of a man's 
family and alliances, not on any government. The order met him at the market gate, 
where he paid its taxes to sell his goods; beyond the gate its column seldom came and its 
punishments could not follow. 

In the capital itself such men were a recognised population. A considerable part of 
the city, Shaler writes, consisted of strangers — Mzabis, Biskris, Arabs and Kabyles 
from tribes that enjoyed a limited or entire independence of the government and were 
protected in the city by compacts, stated or understood, the first two with their own 
agents recognised by the government and holding jurisdiction over their countrymen. 
A man from a tribe the corps could not govern could work in the corps's city under a 
compact; what bound him was the wage he had come for, and what protected him was 


the knowledge that his tribe was beyond the corps's reach. 


The janissary 


The enforcer ran a sum of his own, and the order worked hard on both sides of it. 

His refusal was punished out of public sight but not lightly: a Turk condemned for 
a political crime was strangled privately, a soldier's fault was answered with the 
bastinado or the cord in the house of the agha, and the corps's own covenant ordered a 
man barred for disorder on campaign to be beaten until he died. His compliance was 
rewarded with pay rising year by year, a place in the queue for office, and precedence 
over every native in the city. Shaler says that everything consistent with public order 
was done to feed the janissaries’ pride: the Turk went first everywhere, the native stood 
aside for him in the street, and the soldiers entered the gardens around the city and 
took what they liked. Taverns kept by Jews were tolerated for them, on condition of no 
public scandal. 

And yet the corps could not hold its own men by these alone. The young soldiers 
were confined to barracks and allowed out only on Thursdays under a guardian until 
they could show a respectable beard; and Shaler observes that "the constraint under 
which the Janissaries are held, is evidently irksome, as those who are not attached to the 
country by marriage or lucrative employment, desert whenever they can." The recruit 
from Anatolia had an exit — the sea, and home — and the order's answer to it was to 
attach him: a wife in the city, an office that paid, a rising rate of pay he would forfeit by 


leaving. The more a janissary had in Algiers, the less his exit was worth to him.

The kouloughli and the renegade 


The sons of the corps and the converts from Christendom ran sums that were 
almost mirror images. 

The kouloughli had been born into the country, with property and kin in it, and so 
had no exit and no need of one; what compliance bought him was a place above the 
native, and at times a place on the pay register when the Levantine recruits were too 
few. What it did not buy was the top of the order, which had been shut to men like him 
since his fathers’ generation seized the Kasba and failed. 

The renegade had come from outside, often by capture, and conversion had bought 
him a ladder the kouloughli did not have. Venture de Paradis records that "un renégat a 
plus d'espérance de parvenir qu’eux ; il n'est exclu que du deilik et des autres places 
principales du gouvernement” — a renegade has more hope of rising than they do; he is 
excluded only from the office of dey and the other principal places of government — 
and could become an agha, a commander of the marine, a captain at sea. His exit ran 
back to Christendom and whatever awaited an apostate there, and the sources reached 


for this part do not price it. 


The captive 


At the bottom of the city was the population for whom the order offered nothing 
that could be called a benefit, and it is here that the Regency's force was most 
concentrated and least disguised. 

The Laugier account, whose author had no reason to flatter the Algerines but a 
stated contempt for the redemptionist monks’ stories, says that "The Slaves at all Times 
are so numerous, that they might make themselves Masters of the principal Towns, 
could they carry on a reciprocal Intelligence, and were not deterred from any such 
Thoughts, by the dreadful Punishments inflicted" on the least sign of insurrection. 
Every household in the city, from the dey's down, had its Christian slaves; the state's 
were lodged in five bagnios under their own guardians; many were hired out to the 
galleys. 

A captive's compliance bought him his life and the chance to be ransomed, and 
nothing else. It did not buy his freedom even if he gave up his religion. The Laugier 
account says that owners would be very sorry to see their adult slaves turn Muslim — 
"Not that their Apostacy would gain their Liberty, but the great Profit, which their 
Masters expect from the Fathers of Redemption, would cease” — and that masters 
urged grown slaves against conversion, both to keep them saleable and because a 


converted slave had a better chance of escape; only children under twelve were taken

into the faith and sometimes adopted by the rich. The account tells of a Marseillais

not by anything he could do, but by what his own country, his family or the 
redemptionist orders would pay. The order kept the door shut for the one kind of slave

Reading the cost column against the framework 


The first thing the measurement shows is that force in the Regency was not spread 
over a population but concentrated on particular men. The corps was small; most of 
the country lay outside its reach; its capital was, to a foreign observer, one of the safest 
cities in the world. The heaviest and most visible costs fell on the captive, whose 
insurrection was answered by dreadful punishment and whose conversion was refused; 
on the Jew, who could not strike back and could leave the gates freely only on two days 
a week; and on the corps's own men, who were strangled for politics, beaten to death 
for disorder and confined to barracks until they grew beards. The Muslim townsman, 
who formed the bulk of the governed population the corps lived among, faced a severe 
but predictable law and received for his compliance the security of his person and 
property and a religious world the corps did not touch. 

The second is what held up the rest. For the townsman it was that security and that 
untouched world, and — if Shaler is right — a hierarchy of Turk over native so old it 
had stopped feeling like one. For the makhzan tribes it was the value of being on the 
side that collected. For the independent tribesman it was the wage in the city and the 
market in the town, and the fact that the order made no serious attempt to govern him. 
The corps did not need to terrorise a population it had not undertaken to rule, and it 
did not need to terrorise the one it lived among, because that one had a great deal to 
lose and was being asked to give up little it valued. 

The third is exit. In every stratum the door was set by the order according to what 
the man was worth to it held. The Jew's gate opened on two days in seven, because the 
order needed his money and his mint. The Christian captive's conversion was refused 
and his ransom priced, because he was worth more held than converted; the black 


slave's conversion freed him, because he was not. The janissary’s exit was left open and

made worthless by marriage, office and pay. The subject tribe's exit into the mountains 
was not closed at all, because the order did not need every tribe, and taxed at the market 
gate the men who had gone beyond its reach. Exit was a policy instrument, and the 
policy is legible in who could leave and on what terms. 

And last, what I expected and did not find. I expected an order that held its 
population by terror, because that is what the word pirate state implies. What the 
witnesses describe is a single city in which both ends of the scale were running at once: 
a townsman whose compliance was nearly free and richly rewarded, living in the same 
streets as a captive whose compliance bought nothing but survival and whose refusal 
would be answered by the worst the order could inflict. The terror was real, and it was 


reserved.

What the table does not hold 


The table is a statement of what reached the treasury in coin, and the order's

The monopoly 


The mechanism that did the most work while being least visible to those who paid it 
was the monopoly, and Shaler, who hated it, describes it with precision. "The articles of 
hides, wax, and wool, constitute a public monopoly, which is usually sold annually to 
the highest bidder. Salt, he adds, was a public monopoly too; oil and dressed skins 
could be exported only to Ottoman territory, and grain and livestock of every kind only 
by special permission. The coral fishery of the eastern coast and the export trade of 
Bona were farmed to France for $30,000 a year, and the export trade of Oran was ceded 
to its bey for $15,000. 

The economics of this are worth setting out, because the treasury's $85,000 is not 
the measure of what the monopolies took. A general prohibition on exporting the 
country's produce, with exceptions sold as licences, does two things at once. First, it 
cuts the producer off from the price his goods would fetch abroad, and forces him to 
sell at home, where the only buyers for hides, wool or wax in quantity are the holder of 
the licence and his agents. The tribesman who brings a load of hides to market is 
therefore selling to a single buyer at a price that buyer sets, and he experiences the result 
not as a tax but as the price of hides. Secondly, the licence-holder sells the same goods in 
Marseilles, Leghorn or Genoa at the price they command there, and keeps the 
difference. The state's share of that difference is whatever the licence fetches at auction, 
and because the licence was sold each year to the highest bidder, competition among 
bidders pushed the price of the licence toward the margin each expected to make from 
the monopoly. The $40,000 the treasury took for wool, wax and hides was, in other 
words, a fair estimate of the monopoly's expected profit, sold in advance. 

Shaler's trade table for 1822 allows a rough sizing of the flow the licence sat upon. It 
lists the year's exports to Marseilles, Leghorn and Genoa as twenty thousand quintals of 
wool "at an average $8," worth $160,000; ten thousand quintals of hides at $8, worth 
$80,000; and six hundred quintals of wax at $30, worth $18,000. Those three goods 
were worth $258,000 at the prices Shaler gives, and the licence on them was sold for 
$40,000 — about 15% per cent of their value. What the producers lost was larger than 
the licence fee by the licensee's own profit and costs, and larger again by the value 
destroyed when the prohibition made production not worth undertaking. Shaler saw 
that last effect in the land itself: "Beyond the immediate precincts of the principal 
towns, the lands possess very little value, considered as real estate ; for a general system 


of prohibition of exportation of the produce of this country, except a few articles

which constitute monopolies, ... has reduced the agriculture and trade of the kingdom

abroad was worth little, and the fall in its value is extraction that went to no one.

nearly the same quantity whatever it costs. Venture's list of the offices a senior soldier

None of them appears in Shaler's table except the master of hides and the master of the

Tribute, and what it bought 


The $96,000 paid by four Christian kings in 1822 was the most visible item in the 
table, and the least like a tax. Each payment was the price of a treaty, which from the 
Regency’s side was a grant of protection to that king's ships: their captains would not 
be taken while the tribute was paid. Shaler adds a second form of the same payment 
outside the table. The custom of making a present to the dey and his officers when a 
new consul presented himself, once a courtesy, "has become a real tribute, the amount 
of which is now fixed by custom at about $17,000," and the Regency had extended it, 
on the pretext that consuls were seldom changed, into presents due every second year. 

The economics of tribute are those of insurance. A corsair fleet produces a flow of 
prizes that varies wildly from year to year with war, weather and luck; the prize register 
shows gross proceeds in francs of under two thousand in 1773 and more than two 
million in 1812. A treaty with a fixed annual payment converts part of that uncertain 
flow into a certain one. For the paying state, the tribute was a premium set below the 
losses its merchants expected at sea; for the Regency it was an annuity set, if well 


negotiated, near the prizes it gave up. The order's ability to sell such annuities rested

Sweden, Denmark and Portugal.

That gap is not a quibble about accounting. A cargo worth a certain sum to the

The captive as an asset 


The men taken with the ships were the most valuable cargo, and the order treated 
them as a kind of bond: an asset that yielded labour while it was held and a principal 
sum when it was ransomed. 

The principal is priced in Venture's notes. When the Spaniards redeemed their 
captives around 1768-70, some two thousand persons left Algiers, "et l'argent 
comptant qui entra dans le trésor monta 4 600,000 piastres fortes. Un pareil rachat 
aujourd'hui cotiterait prés de deux millions de piastres indépendamment des échanges” 
— and the cash that entered the treasury came to 600,000 piastres fortes; a similar 
redemption today would cost nearly two million piastres, besides exchanges of 
prisoners. The price of a single captive had risen tenfold in forty years: "Dans ce temps- 
la, les esclaves chrétiens ne valaient pas plus de 4 4 500 livres ; maintenant les cent livres 
se sont changées en mille.” In the 1740s a Christian slave was worth no more than four 
or five hundred livres; the hundreds had become thousands, and it took four or five 
thousand to ransom an ordinary seaman. 

The yield while he was held was his labour. The Laugier account of the 1720s 
describes how the harbour mole, battered by the northern swell, was maintained: "the 
Slaves of the Deylik are kept at Work the whole Year in bringing Blocks of Stone from a 
Quarry near the Fish-Gate, and laying them in on the Sand to secure the Mole. The 
Weight and Impetuosity of the Sea washing away these Stones, makes such a constant 
Supply necessary." The work never ended because the sea never stopped, and it was 
done by men whose labour cost the state their bread and their guards. Private owners 
hired their slaves out to the galleys and to strangers as servants, and collected the hire. 

This explains a choice recorded earlier in this study that would otherwise look 
perverse: why masters refused to let adult captives convert. A captive who became a 
Muslim could no longer be ransomed. His conversion would have turned a bond 
carrying a principal of several thousand livres into a labourer worth only his work, and 
it would have made it easier for him to escape. The order kept its captives Christian for 


the same reason a creditor does not forgive a debt that is about to be repaid.

Labour that appears in no column 


Forced labour is the mechanism most thoroughly hidden by any account of this 
order, and it has to be valued from both sides because the two sides do not match. 

For the state, the labour of a captive on the mole was cheaper than a tax of the same 
value could ever have been. To pay free labourers to haul stone all year, the Regency 
would have had to assess a tax, collect it in coin from a specie-poor countryside, hold it, 
and pay it out in wages; each stage costs officials, and each stage loses some of the 
money. The captive's labour skipped all of it. The state fed him from the grain the 
tribes delivered in kind, locked him in a bagnio under a guardian, and marched him to 
the quarry. The only cash cost was the guardian's pay. 

For the man who performed it, the same labour cost far more than a tax of the same 
value would have. A townsman taxed a sum could pay it from his earnings and go on 
with his life; a captive owed not a sum but his whole time, indefinitely, with no way to 
pay less in a bad year or buy his way out except by ransom his captors priced. What he 
lost was not the wages of a stone-carrier in Algiers but whatever his life and work would 
have been worth at home, and that loss went to no one. The difference between what 
the labour saved the state and what it cost the labourer was destroyed. 

The order did not reserve this kind of extraction for Christians. Shaler records that 
the Jews of Algiers were turned out without notice for any sudden labour the 
government required, and that in the locust summer of 1815 several hundred of them 
were ordered to guard the dey’s gardens day and night for as long as the insects 
remained — a liability with no ceiling and no schedule, borne by a community that 
could not refuse it and could not plan for it. 

And the one place in the reachable accounts where this hidden labour shows itself is 
the place where it stopped. After the bombardment of 1816 forced the Regency to give 
up Christian slavery, Shaler says that hard labour in chains had become the commonest 
punishment in practice, "and in a measure furnishes labourers in lieu of slaves upon the 
public works." The expenditure side of his 1822 table carries an item of $24,000 for 
labourers and artificers in the dockyards. No such item can be seen in any earlier 
account reached here, because there is no earlier account; but a line for paid labour on 
the public works, and a policy of sentencing criminals to labour in chains to supply 
what captives had supplied, are what the end of a hidden mechanism looks like when 


its costs come onto the books.

Escheat, rent and customs 


Three of the smaller mechanisms were extraction of a quieter kind. The officer of 
inheritances paid the treasury $40,000 a year for the right to collect the estates of all 
who died without heirs, and his office was farmed, which means that what he collected 
was larger than what he paid. The payer of that extraction was already dead, and his 
heirs, if he had any at a distance, rarely knew what the estate had been; it generated no 
petitions. The rents of the state's property in the city, another $40,000, were drawn 
from houses and shops that had come to the state one confiscation and one heirless 
death at a time. And customs, the mechanism most visible to foreign merchants and 
the one most European writers described, yielded less than either — $20,800 on a trade 


of more than a million dollars in imports. 


One stage past the treasury 


The Regency’'s expenditure in 1822 is as short as its receipts are long. Shaler lists the 
pay of dockyard labourers and artificers at $24,000, naval stores at $60,000, the pay of 
navy officers and seamen at $75,000, and the pay of the military of all classes at 
$700,000, a total of $859,000. "This leaves an annual balance against the treasury of 
$424,200." The corps's pay alone was more than half as large again as everything the 
treasury received in cash. 

The deficit was paid from the hoard in the Kasba. Shaler says that the fruits of the 
corsairs had not only supplied all the order's wants until recent years but had allowed a 
treasury of gold and silver to be hoarded that might sustain it for many years to come. 
In 1822, then, the order was living on its capital, drawing down gold and silver 
accumulated in the decades when the prize register's totals ran into millions of francs. 

Following the money one stage further shows where the extraction finally went. The 
$700,000 paid to the soldiers was spent in the city, and much of it on what the city 
imported. Shaler's trade table for the same year records imports of $1,200,000 — 
British and Indian manufactures, silks and brocades from Leghorn, sugar, coffee and 
pepper from France, raw silk from the Levant, and jewels, diamonds and silver plate 
from France and Italy worth $100,000 — against exports of $273,000. "The balance of 
nine hundred and twenty-seven thousand dollars, enormous for a country of so few 
active resources as Algiers, is remitted annually in gold and silver." The tribute of 
Christian kings, the monopoly on the tribesman’s hides, the estates of the heirless and 
the hoard of the corsair years ended their journey as coin shipped to Marseilles, 


Leghorn and London in payment for cloth, coffee and diamonds. The order extracted a

surplus from the sea and the countryside and exported it to the economies of the

Reading extraction against the framework 


The first thing the evidence shows is the proportion, and how far it departs from 
what a reader expects. In the one year counted, under a third of the Regency's cash 
came from the provinces that held nearly all its people; more than a fifth came from 
Christian kings; about a fifth from monopolies and export licences; nearly a fifth from 
the estates of the dead and the rents of property the state had absorbed; and less than a 
twentieth from customs. The visible taxes a subject would recognise were light. The 
heavy mechanisms were ones he did not see: a monopoly buyer's price for his hides, a 
prohibition on selling his grain abroad that made his land nearly worthless, the grain 
delivered in kind to feed soldiers, the death of a kinsman without an heir, the beys' 
agents who took "all that is tangible." 

The second is how much the counted revenue understates the whole. The table 
omits the two hundred thousand measures of wheat and twenty thousand of barley; 
everything the beys, the qaids, the makhzan and the tax columns kept before remitting; 
the beys'’ personal presents to the dey and his officers, which in 1788 ran to tens of 
thousands of sequins; the producers’ loss under the monopolies beyond the licence 
fees; the hulls of captured ships, which went wholly to the state outside the prize 
accounts; the value destroyed when cargoes were sold at a vile price; and the labour of 
captives, convicts and Jews. None of these can be summed from the sources reached 
here, and no total is offered. But every one of them is extraction that generated no 
petition and no negotiated rate, because the man who bore it experienced it as a price, a 
death, a harvest delivered, a summons to work or a ship lost at sea. 

The third is what forced labour and the captive show about value. The labour on 
the mole cost the state its guardians and the prisoners’ bread and saved it the entire 
business of taxing, collecting and paying wages; it cost the captive his liberty and 
whatever his life would have produced at home; and the difference went to no one. The 
captive himself was held as a bond whose labour was the interest and whose ransom 
was the principal, and the rise of that principal tenfold in forty years explains why the 
order refused to let him convert. 

The fourth is the direction of the whole flow. By 1822 the order paid its soldiers 
more than it received, lived on a hoard built by the corsairs, and sent nearly a million 
dollars a year in gold and silver out of the country to pay for imports. An order whose 
revenue has come to rest on tribute that the strongest navies had stopped paying, on 


monopolies that impoverished its own countryside, and on a treasury it was consuming

at more than four hundred thousand dollars a year, was not visibly failing in 1822. It 
was spending the reserve that would make its failure sudden.

Who stood between 


N o inhabitant of the Regency could check for himself most of what he believed 
about the world he lived in. He could not read the sultan's firman, which was 
written in Ottoman Turkish; he could not see the treasury's accounts, which were kept 
in the Kasba; he could not attend the divan, where the corps decided on war and peace; 
he could not know what the consuls wrote to their governments or what the dey wrote 
to the sultan. What he knew of these things he knew because someone told him, and 
the question this part asks is who those someones were, what they let pass, and what 
they kept back. 

There were several such bodies of men, and they stood at different gates. Between 
the population and God, the law and the meaning of events stood the men of religion 
— the gadis, imams and preachers, and above all the marabouts, whose word was taken 
because they said it. Between the corps and everyone who did not speak Turkish stood 
the interpreters and the writers: the corps's own khodjas, who kept its registers; the 
dragomans of the consulates; the Jewish brokers of the great commercial houses, who 
carried messages and money between the dey and Europe. And between Algiers and 
the Christian public of Europe stood the consuls, the redemptionist friars and the 
ransomed captives, who between them wrote almost everything Europe read about the 


city. 


What the city was told 


The order fed its population a small number of things, and it fed them in public. 

It gave them the sultan's name every Friday in the sermon and on every coin. It fired 
its cannon at the end of Ramadan and at the feast forty days later. It read the sultan's 
firmans aloud to the assembled troops and notables. And it displayed its acts of 
punishment where everyone could see them: the hooks at the gate of Bab Azoun, the 


places of execution assigned to Moors, to Jews and to Christians.

It also let the city keep its own account of its own history, because the city’s account

redeem captives, asked how the city explained the destruction of the emperor Charles 
V's great armada before its walls in 1541, he was told — and reported with scorn —

prieres d'vn de leurs Marabous, nommé CIDY VTICA, qui estoit alors en grand

their marabouts, called Sidi Utica, who was then in great credit. The city had built him 
a small mosque outside the gate of Bab Azoun, where he was buried, and kept lamps 
burning there in his honour. The greatest deliverance in the Regency’s history was, in 
the city's telling, the work of a holy man’s prayers, not of the corps's guns. The corps 
had no reason to contradict it. A population that believed God defended Algiers 


through its saints was a population that regarded Algiers as worth defending. 


The rival reading, and what became of its carrier 


The city’s holy men were free to say almost anything, and the corps itself, in its 
covenant of 1749, told its own members to receive their letters with respect. There was 
one thing a holy man could not say, and Dan recorded what happened to one who said 
it. 

A marabout of Tlemcen — the old capital of the western kingdom, which Algiers 
now ruled — began to tell the principal men of that city that he had received "secretes 
reuelations” from the Prophet, who was angry to see Tlemcen under the tyranny of 
Algiers, a city that had once taken laws from it, and who had shown him the means to 
throw off the yoke. He added, Dan says, that the Prophet had given him a particular 
secret: that all who took up arms with him would come to no harm in the fighting, 
"pource que les mousquets de leurs ennemis ne pourroient prendre feu" — because 
their enemies’ muskets would not be able to fire — and that their enemies’ blades 
would blunt against them. He raised, in Dan's account, an army of some ten thousand 
Moors and Arabs to take the town and its castle. 

The divan in Algiers was informed and consulted, and sent a force of janissaries, 
which Dan puts at no more than eleven or twelve hundred; their muskets fired, and the 
marabout's army broke. And then the order published what it had done. It took the 
marabout and "trente-deux des principaux, qu'ils firent a l'heure mesme écorcher tous 
vifs ; puis ayant remply leur peau de paille, ils s'en retournerent en Alger, tous glorieux 
de cette victoire ; faisant porter par les rués au haut d'vne pique, les peaux de ces rebelles

The marabout was not flayed for his doctrine. The corps deferred to marabouts, 
paid them, and told its own soldiers to heed them. He was flayed because he had put a 
holy man’s authority behind a claim about sovereignty — that Tlemcen should not be 
ruled from Algiers — and because he had made that claim to the people who could act 
on it. On God and the law the order's gates stood open to every voice. On who ruled 


whom, they were shut, and the order let the city see exactly what lay behind them. 


The divan, loud and then silent 


The order's own deliberations went through a longer history, and the history runs in 
one direction. 

Dan attended a session of the divan in August 1634 and described how it decided. 
Its business, he says, was proposed and resolved in Turkish, which all its officers had to 
speak, and "cela se pratique ainsi parmy eux, pour donner a connoistre par 1a, quils 
releuent de l'Empire du Turc. C'est pour cela mesme, qu'il y a tofijours au Diuan vn 
interprete des langues, dont on se sert d'ordinaire pour entendre les Chrestiens & les 
Maures, quand ils ont a presenter quelque plainte ou quelque requeste.” That is how 
they do it, to make known that they hold from the empire of the Turk; and for that 
same reason there is always in the divan an interpreter of languages, used to hear the 
Christians and the Moors when they have a complaint or a petition to present. The 
Moor who came to the divan with a grievance could be heard; he could not follow the 
discussion of it. 

The discussion itself was a noise. The agha put the question; four officers repeated it 
aloud; the word passed down to the lowest officer and back up again, "auec vn bruit & 
vn hurlement estrange, quand il arriue que la chose n'est pas au goust de l'Assemblée ; 
Et alors l'Aga donne ses conclusions” — with a noise and a strange howling when the 
thing is not to the assembly's taste, and then the agha gives his conclusion according to 
how the word has come back. Dissent, in that hall, was permitted and loud. What was 
not permitted was to organise it. "Car c'est la coustume,” Dan writes, that "celuy qui 
commence la sedition, en haussant les mains qu'il tient croisées dans l'Assemblée," the 
man who begins a sedition by raising his crossed hands in the assembly, whether he is 
right or wrong, "on le saisit aussi-tost pour le mettre en vn sac, & le ietter dans la mer" 
— is seized at once, put in a sack and thrown into the sea. The corps allowed a 
thousand men to howl at a proposal; it drowned the first man who made the howling 


into a movement.

Beyond the divan's door the population had one recognised channel, and it too was 
a cry. Dan describes women who came to the gate of the Kasba to demand justice and 
set up the call "CHARALA, CHARALA, c'est a dire iustice de Dieu" — shar’ Allah,

corps’s reasons but to the sacred law, which the corps claimed to uphold and could not 
openly refuse. 

A century and a half later the hall had gone quiet. Venture de Paradis, in the 1780s, 
notes of the retired aghas that they attend the great divans, "Mais ces grands divans ne 
se tiennent plus comme autrefois, le dey termine toutes les affaires sans consulter le

business without consulting the divan. He records in the same passage that the house

citadel, Shaler could write that "the Divan may be regarded as a dead letter in their 
constitution. The Dey appoints his own ministers." 

Over two centuries, then, the deliberation of the order withdrew from a hall full of 
shouting soldiers to a dey's council behind the walls of a fortress. Its acts did not 
withdraw at all. The hooks stayed at the gate, the executions stayed public, the skins of 
Tlemcen had been paraded through the streets. What the order came to protect was 
not the record of what it did — it displayed that — but the discussion of what it was 
about to do, and it protected that discussion even from the corps's own rank and file, 


whose failures it now punished where no one could see. 


Language as a gate 


The corps's deliberations were closed to the population by a wall no decree had to 
build: they were conducted in a language the population did not speak. Dan says the 
corps used Turkish in the divan as a declaration of its dependence on the Ottoman 
empire, and the effect of that declaration was that no Arabic-speaking townsman could 
follow what was decided about him except through an interpreter the corps provided. 

The wall faced outward too. When Omar Dey proposed to write a letter to the 
President of the United States, Shaler recalls that the dey asked whether Turkish was 
understood in America; told that it probably was not, though there were men there 
who read Arabic, "He remarked that it did not become him to write in any language 
except his own, but that he would cause an Arabic translation” to accompany it. 


Everything that passed between the dey and a foreign power went through a translator,

and every translator stood where he could shape what passed. The consuls knew it. 
Shaler's journal of the crisis of 1824 describes a dragoman on whom the consulate 
depended: "he speaks the Turkish language fluently ; talks much and plausibly, but he 
cannot with safety be relied on.” A man who held the only bridge between two 


governments held a power neither government could check. 


What was never taught 


The cheapest way to keep a population from a rival account of affairs is never to give 
it the means of making one, and the Regency used that way on the subjects it most 
wished to keep to itself. 

No source reached in this study describes a printing press in Algiers before 1830, 
and none describes anything a townsman could read about the affairs of his 
government. What reached the city in print came by ship, and it came to foreigners: 
Shaler's journal records, again and again, vessels arriving from Marseilles by which "the 
Consul received private letters and newspapers from his correspondents in Marseilles." 
The consul in his house above the city knew more about the wars of Europe than the 
dey’s subjects knew about the dey's treasury. The treasury's accounts reached Shaler 
only because they were "furnished to me as correct, by a person who has access to the 
public records" — not published, but passed to a foreigner by an informant. The 
Qur'an schools taught every boy to read one book and write its letters; they did not 
teach him to read a register, and the registers were kept in Turkish. 

And the country beyond the city was kept from the foreigners themselves. Shaler, 
who lived in Algiers for more than a decade, wrote of the inland towns that an earlier 
traveller had described that "As I can obtain no definite information respecting them, it 
is probable" they had sunk into insignificance. The consul of a nation at peace with the 
Regency could not find out whether its towns still existed. 

What the order kept from the city was not its doctrine, which belonged to others, 
nor its punishments, which it displayed, but its numbers and its reasons: how much 
came into the Kasba and went out of it, and what the dey and his ministers intended. 
No law forbade a townsman to know these things. There was simply no way he could 


learn them.

The redemptionist orders raised the money that ransomed Christian captives, and

and wanted the Algerines understood, complained in his preface that the prejudice of

The counter-account had its own valve. When the Laugier book was put into 
English in 1750, its French original having been published as a history of the kingdom 
of Algiers, the English title page read "A Compleat HISTORY OF THE PIRATICAL

cover told the English reader what they were before he opened it. 


Reading information and suppression against the framework 


The first thing the evidence shows is where the order's gates were crowded and 
where they were captured. On doctrine they were crowded to overflowing: the 
marabouts, the scholars, the saints and their lineages all spoke, and the corps itself 
deferred to them and let the city tell its own history as the work of their prayers. On 
one subject the gates were captured absolutely, and that subject was sovereignty — who 
ruled whom. A marabout who put the Prophet's authority behind the claim that 
Tlemcen should throw off Algiers was flayed alive with thirty-two of the principal men 
who had listened to him, and their skins were carried through the capital on pikes. The 
order did not fear holy men. It feared a holy man with a claim to rule. 

The second is what the order protected, and the answer changed over time in one 
direction. In the 1630s the divan shouted its deliberations aloud before an interpreter 
and let a thousand soldiers howl at a proposal, while drowning the man who tried to 
organise the howling. By the 1780s the dey decided without the divan and the corps 
punished its own members behind closed doors. By the 1820s the divan was a dead 
letter and the dey governed from inside a fortress through ministers he appointed. 


Through all of it the order published its acts — the hooks, the executions, the parade of 


PAT TERNS OF POWER 89 DHE REGENGY OF ALGIERS 


skins — and hid its reasoning. It came to hide that reasoning even from the rank and 
file of the corporation that had once been its deliberative body. 

The third is the valve that cost nothing. The population was not forbidden to know 
the treasury's accounts or the divan’s reasons; it was never given the means to know 
them. There was no press, the deliberations were in Turkish, the registers were in 
Turkish, and the schools taught recitation of a sacred text rather than the reading of a 
ledger. A consul needed an informant to see a year's accounts, and could not learn 
whether the inland towns still stood. 

The fourth is that the Regency’s image abroad was made by its own class of 
intermediaries on the other side of the sea, with interests of their own. The friars who 
ransomed captives needed charity and were accused by a consular writer of spreading 
falsehood to get it; the consular writer wanted the Algerines understood and saw his 
book sold in England as a history of pirates. 

And last, what I expected and did not find. I expected an order that controlled what 
its subjects believed about God and the world, because that is what a regime of force 
over a conquered population is assumed to need. The Regency left belief almost 
entirely to others and was content to borrow from it. What it controlled, with the sack 
and the pike, was the single question of who had the right to rule, and what it 
withdrew, over two hundred years, was not the population's knowledge of its acts but 


everyone's knowledge — including its own soldiers’ — of its intentions.

Thirty deys 


Eugéne Plantet, in 1889, printed a chronological list of the rulers of Algiers from

Two violent bands and a quiet middle 


Arranged by date, the known ends fall into three distinct stretches. 

From 1681 to 1724 the office turned over very fast and very violently: fourteen deys 
in forty-three years, and of the five whose ends are recorded, four were killings. From 
1805 to 1817 the same thing happened again: six deys in twelve years, five of whose 
ends are known, and four of those were killings. 

Between them lies a stretch of three-quarters of a century in which the office 
behaved quite differently. From 1724 to 1798 there were seven deys. Abdi reigned eight 
years and died of illness at seventy-one; Ibrahim reigned thirteen years and abdicated on 
his deathbed; Baba Ali reigned twelve and died in his bed; Baba Mohammed reigned, 
on the modern accounts, twenty-five years and died in office; Sidi Hassan reigned seven 
and died in office. Only one of the seven, el-Retorto, is known to have been killed, and 
the other, Ibrahim the nephew, reigned three years and left no recorded end in the 
sources reached. 

And in that quiet middle the office passed in a way the order's rules did not provide 
for. The corps's register conveyed nothing heritable, and a murdered dey's sons, as the 
Laugier account said, went back to a common soldier's pay. Yet in 1745 a dying dey 
abdicated in favour of his nephew, who was his treasurer; the sultan’s firman of 1746, 
already quoted in this study, confirms the new dey as the dead man's "neveu et son 
lieutenant." The modern accounts continue the line: Baba Ali made his treasurer 
Mohammed his successor; Mohammed was succeeded by his adopted son and nephew

The link at the bottom: the soldier 


With the pattern of the top established, the audit can begin where the order's force 
lay, with the men of the register. 

What the ordinary soldier's standing was reckoned in is not in doubt: a name on the 
pay register, a rate that rose by a saime a year toward a ceiling, a place in the queue of 
seniority, precedence over every native, a share in the column's takings. Who could void 
it is equally clear. No dey could cut it — the register belonged to the corps, and a dey 
who touched it would not remain dey long; el-Retorto was killed at the very table 
where the pay was counted out. Only the collapse of the corps itself, or of the chest that 
paid it, could void a soldier's standing. 

If the arrangement had collapsed tomorrow, he would have been far worse off. He 
had no land, often no kin in the country, and his whole livelihood and rank were the 
register's. So he defended the arrangement, and because his standing did not depend on 
any particular dey, he defended the arrangement without defending the dey. A change 
of ruler cost him nothing and paid him a rise. The soldier was the order's most reliable 


defender and the dey’s least reliable one, and both facts have the same cause. 


The conspirators and the man at the top 


For an officer of standing — a senior soldier with a following, a minister, a captain 
— the order offered one prize seniority could not deliver, and "every Turk ... being 
qualified to rise to the Deylik" meant that any of them might take it. The conspiracies 
the Laugier account describes did not threaten the arrangement; they were one of its 
working parts. A successful cabal killed a man and kept everything else: the register, the 
pay scale, the offices, the sultan's investiture, which the new dey would ask for as his 
predecessor had. A failed cabal's leaders were strangled or driven out, and the register 
was untouched. Plotting against the dey was the ambitious officer's way of defending 
the arrangement while trying to rise within it. 

The dey's own position was the most exposed in the order. His standing was 
reckoned in an office voidable at any moment by any cabal the corps would bow to, and 


ultimately by the sultan who invested him. He had no exit: if he fell, he lost his life, his

property went to the treasury, his wives went back to their former condition and his 
sons to the common soldier's pay. The arrangement gave him more than any other man 
and could take it all in an afternoon, and so the dey had the strongest interest of anyone 
in keeping it exactly as it was — including the rule that made him disposable, because 
the same rule was what had put him there. 

The quiet middle of the eighteenth century shows how that exposure could be 
reduced without changing any rule. A dey who made his treasurer his designated 
successor turned the man best placed to kill him into the man with most to lose by his 
murder. The treasurer who expected the office did not need to conspire for it, and had 
every reason to prevent others from doing so. The long reigns of Baba Ali and Baba 


Mohammed are what that looks like in the record. 


The ministers 


The dey's ministers — the treasurer, the agha of the spahis, the minister of marine, 
the officer of inheritances — held offices the dey conferred and could take away. Their 
standing was reckoned in his favour, and his fall was the most dangerous moment in 
their lives, because the new dey’s first act was to remove the leaders of other factions. If 
the arrangement collapsed, they lost office and probably life. They defended it, and in 
ordinary times they defended the dey; but they were also the men most likely to take his 
place, and in the violent periods at least one of them killed the man he served. Shaler 
says that the minister of marine under Omar had been the chamberlain and confidant 


of Hajji Ali, and had murdered Hajji Ali with his own hand as a partisan of Omar. 


The captains 


The corsair captains stood partly outside the register. A captain's standing was 
reckoned in his ship and his shares, and a ship, unlike an office, was property that could 
be sold and left to heirs. That standing survived a change of dey; it did not survive a 
change in the balance of power at sea. The captains’ interest was in war with those who 
had not bought a peace, and in fewer treaties rather than more. For most of the order's 
history that interest ran with the corps's, because prizes paid the pay. After the 
bombardment of 1816 and the loss of the corsair trade, the one group in the ruling 
order whose wealth was reckoned in heritable capital lost the flow that capital had been 


built to capture. No source reached here records what the captains did about it.

The beys 


The governors of the three provinces were the link at which the arrangement came 
nearest to producing the kind of holder that can survive its fall. A bey's standing was 
reckoned in an office conferred by the dey and paid for with a fixed remittance and 
heavy presents, and the dey could void it and take everything in the government house 
when he did. But a bey also commanded the makhzan cavalry of his province, collected 
its taxes through his own agents, and held its revenues for the years he held the office. 
And the office was beginning to behave like land. "The sons of Beys have in some 
instances succeeded their fathers, and Colories have been appointed Kaids, or governors 
of districts, probably through corruption. But these are regarded as aberrations” — so 
Shaler, who saw the tendency and the disapproval of it together. 

If the centre in Algiers had collapsed, a bey who could hold his province with his 
own cavalry would have lost his remittance and his danger, and kept his revenue. Of 
every man with standing and means in the order, he was the one for whom the 


atrangement's collapse was least clearly a loss. 


The corps's sons 


The kouloughlis held standing reckoned outside the register altogether: houses, 
gardens and kin in the country, which survived any change of master, and a place above 
the native population. The register itself was open to them only when the Levantine 
recruits were too few, and its highest places were closed to them. They had once, in the 
1620s or 1630s, tried to take the Kasba and failed. A group with local property, local 
kin and a remembered grievance, excluded from the order's top, is a group that might 
find the arrangement not worth defending. In the reachable record they did not act 


again, and the corps kept them from the Kasba so that they could not. 


The collectors, the men of religion and the brokers 


The makhzan chiefs held privileges reckoned in their relation to whatever power 
held the beylik: exemption from tribute, a share of what they collected, government 
land. That standing survived a change of dey easily and would survive a change of 
regime too, provided the new master needed collectors. They defended the 
arrangement as long as it paid them, and they had no reason to defend it against a 
successor who would pay them as well. 

The men of religion — the gadis, the muftis, the marabout lineages, the 


administrators of endowments — held standing reckoned in lineage, learning and

property placed under a law no dey could revoke. Their standing had survived the 
Spaniards, the brothers and every dey since, and would survive whatever came next. 
They lent the order the sermon and the obedience of the faithful, and they had nothing 
of their own to lose if it fell. 

The Jewish brokers who financed the dey's trade and carried his dealings with 
Europe held standing reckoned in the dey's favour and in their credit abroad, and it 
could be voided by the soldiers in an afternoon. Devoulx records that the influence the 
dey Mustapha allowed his Jewish favourites, and above all Naphtali Busnach, so 
enraged the militia that in June 1805 it massacred many Jews and pillaged their houses, 
and that two months later it killed the dey. The brokers profited from the arrangement 


more than almost anyone and could defend it less than anyone. 


Above them all 


At the top of the chain stood the sultan, whose standing in Algiers was reckoned in 
an investiture the corps requested and paid for, and who could void a dey’s title and 
close the Anatolian ports to the corps's recruiters. The sultan lost nothing when a dey 
was killed; the next one would ask for the caftan. What he stood to lose was the claim 


itself, and the Regency never took it from him. 


Reading the default against the framework 


The first finding is about the man at the top, and it corrects the starting assumption 
of this study rather than confirming it. The office of dey was violent in two periods — 
roughly 1683 to 1724 and 1805 to 1817 — and not in the seventy-five years between, 
when five of the seven deys died in office or left it by abdication and the office passed 
within a family through the treasurer's post. Of the thirty deys, at least ten were killed 
by their own order; at least eight died in office or left it alive; the rest are not established. 
The Regency’s reputation for a dey murdered every few years describes its beginning 
and its end, which are the periods Europe read most about, and not its long middle. 

The second is why the violence, where it happened, did not change the order. Every 
man in the corps held his standing in the register, and the register did not depend on 
who was dey. The soldier gained a rise when a dey was killed and bowed to the winner 
from prudence; the conspirator risked only himself; the defeated faction's leaders died 
and the structure did not. Each man defending his own place defended the whole, and 
the dey was the one piece of the whole that none of them needed to defend. 

The third is what reduced the violence, and it is not what the order's rules would 


predict. An order in which nothing was heritable produced, at its apex, a practice of

designated succession through the treasurer that ran from uncle to nephew and from 
grandfather to grandson. The designation turned the most dangerous man in the dey's 
council into the one most interested in his survival. Heritability, excluded by the 
register at every level, crept back in at the single place where it did the most to quiet the 
order. 

The fourth is where fracture could come from. Two groups with standing and 
means had interests that did not run with the register: the beys, whose offices were 
beginning to pass from father to son and who commanded their own cavalry and 
revenue; and, after 1816, the corsair captains, whose heritable capital had lost its 
purpose. A third, the kouloughlis, had property, kin and a grievance, and were kept out 
of the Kasba. The men of religion and the makhzan chiefs would survive any master. 
And under all of them lay the one condition on which the soldiers’ loyalty to the 
arrangement depended: that the chest in the Kasba could pay the register. In the one 
year whose accounts survive, it was paying more than it received, from a hoard that 
could not be refilled. 

And last, what I expected and did not find. I expected the evidence to confirm that 
the Regency combined constant violence at the top with complete stability 
underneath, because that is what an order with nothing heritable ought to produce. 
The stability underneath is real, and its cause is what I expected. But the violence was 
not constant; it came in two long bands with three-quarters of a century of peaceful 
succession between them, and the peace was bought by exactly what the order's rules 


excluded — a dey who could leave his office to his kin.

A liberty with an address 


A reader looking for liberty in the Regency of Algiers will not find it where the 
word usually points. No inhabitant of the kingdom had a right to speak about 
its government, to print, to assemble for a political purpose, or to be tried by a court 
the rulers did not control; the maxim of its justice, Shaler reported, was that it was 
better to punish the innocent than let the guilty go. And yet the order was not without 
protections. Several bodies of men held real guarantees against the apparatus, some of 
them written down. What they had in common is that none of them was held by the 
population at large, and each had been won, conceded or borrowed by a group that 
could make its displeasure costly. 

This part follows those protections to their sources, and then follows one of them 


— the greatest, and never written — to the moment it was lost. 


The corps's charter 


The fullest statement of rights anywhere in the Regency's own documents was 
made by the corps for itself, and it was made in the aftermath of a quarrel among its 
own members. 

The preamble of the covenant of 1749 explains why it was drawn up. Pride and 
animosity had seized the soldiers, it says, until enmity became general, setting father 
against son, brother against brother and friend against friend, and in the previous year, 
1748, the disorders had been especially grave. To put an end to them, the corps 
convened a divan in the palace which all were to attend. What came out of that divan is 
the order's nearest thing to a charter of liberties, and it begins with an amnesty. "A 
partir de ce jour, tout ce qui a eu lieu précédemment, méme entre le pére et le fils, est 
oublié et pardonné” — from this day, everything that has happened before, even 
between father and son, is forgotten and forgiven.

And the freedom the same charter closed 


The same document that bound the agha to justice closed the members’ mouths. 
"Les correspondances particuliéres répandant des nouvelles, sont interdites ; en cas

cruisers at sea only the cruisers; the carrying of news is nothing but a source of trouble. 
The corps guaranteed its members a hearing and an inquiry, and forbade them, on pain 
of death, to tell one another what was happening elsewhere in the order. Liberty and 
foreclosure were written into the same charter by the same men for the same reason: to

Protections of rank 


Beneath the written charter lay older protections of rank that no document needed 
to state, because every witness saw them. A Turk condemned to death was strangled 
privately, as Shaler records, where a native was hanged or thrown on the hooks in 
public; the soldiers’ punishments, Venture says, were inflicted behind closed doors in 
the agha’s house. Shaler adds that all quarrels among the Turks were prohibited under 
the severest penalties and that he had never heard of one Turk publicly outraging 
another. The corps protected the dignity of its members against the eyes of the 
population it governed, and that protection was worth a great deal to men whose 


standing rested on being seen as born to command.

The townsman and the older law 


The Muslim townsman held protections the corps had not granted and could not 
openly revoke, because they came from a law older than the corps and bound up with 
the title it held. 

He could go to the qadi of his school, whose law the corps did not write. He could 
carry a complaint to the divan, where, Dan says, an interpreter was always present to 
hear the Christians and the Moors who came with a grievance or a petition. He could 
join the crowd that the petitioners of Algiers raised at the gate of the Kasba, crying

corps could not refuse outright without denying the law in whose name it ruled. And

and all the inhabitants. The townsman’s protections were not won; they were the terms 
on which the corps held its title, and they were as strong as the corps's need for that 
title. 

They did not reach the things that mattered most. The townsman had no 
protection for political speech — the marabout of Tlemcen and the principal men who 
listened to him were flayed alive — and no assembly beyond the mosque and the

Bargained from strength 


The remaining protections in the Regency were held by men whose protectors were 
strong enough to impose them. 

The strangers in the city — Mzabis, Biskris, Kabyles and Arabs from tribes the 
Regency could not govern — were, Shaler says, "protected in their rights here, by 
stipulated or implied compacts," under agents of their own nations whom the 
government recognised. Their protection was the price the order paid for their labour, 
and it was secured by their tribes’ independence: a government that could not reach a 
man's home had to bargain for his presence in its city. 

The consuls of Christian powers held the most explicitly written liberties in the 
kingdom, and they held them by treaty. A treaty printed in Shaler's appendix provides 
that a consul's "person, house, or houses, shall be inviolable ; and if any person injure or 
insult him by word or deed, such person shall be severely punished,” and that he might 
choose his own interpreter and servants, free of tax. Those guarantees were worth what 
the fleets behind them were worth, and the Regency could suspend them in war, as the 
French consul chained in the bagnio in 1798 discovered. 

The Christian captives received the largest liberty of all, and it was imposed from 
outside by force. After the bombardment of 1816, Shaler writes, the suppression of 
Christian slavery had changed even the Regency's punishments, which now supplied

no faction gained by it; it was the one protection in the kingdom created by an enemy's

The liberty the corps lost 


The greatest liberty in the Regency was never written anywhere, and it belonged to 
the corps. It was the power to remove the dey. It was the corps's final guarantee against 
every other abuse: a dey who cut the pay, broke the covenant, or ruled against the 
soldiers’ interest could be killed at the pay table or strangled in his palace, and his 
successor would begin by honouring the register. Every written protection the corps 
held rested on it. The agha was bound to justice because the agha’s master could be 
removed by the men he judged. 

The liberty eroded before it was lost. The divan that in 1634 shouted its 
deliberations aloud had become, by the 1780s, a body the dey no longer consulted. But 
the power of removal survived that change, and was used repeatedly in the early 
nineteenth century: Mustapha in 1805, Ali Boursali Khodja in 1809, Hajji Ali and 
Hadji Mohammed in 1815, Omar in 1817. Shaler says that Omar might have been on 
the throne still if he had resolved to depart from custom and move his residence into 
the citadel, where he would have been secure from the sudden attempts of faction. 

His successor did. Ali Khodja, Shaler writes, learning of conspiracies against him, 
"transferred his residence, together with the public treasury,” from the ancient palace of 
the deys "into the citadel, where he organized a guard of natives for his protection, and 
set the Turks at defiance.” Rumour in the city put the treasury carried up on mules at 
fifty million dollars. "This chief appears to have formed the project of suppressing 
entirely the corps of Janissaries, and probably that of rendering the sovereignty 
hereditary in his family," and he persecuted the Turks with violence throughout his 
short reign. He died of the plague in 1818, and his successor, Hussein, "continues to 
prefer the secure residence of the citadel." 

Nothing in the corps's covenant was repealed. The agha was still bound to judge by 
a law no thicker than a hair split in forty; the sons of janissaries could still enrol. What 
had gone was the one guarantee that made all the others enforceable: the soldiers’ 
ability to reach the man above them. The dey had moved the chest that paid them 
behind walls and surrounded it with men from the population the corps had excluded 
for three centuries. In the Regency’'s final factional fight — between the dey and the 
corps that elected him — the outsiders were at last let in, not as holders of rights, but as 


the apex's weapon against the insiders.

Reading liberty against the framework 


The first thing the evidence shows is that there were real protections from the 
apparatus in the Regency, and that every one of them had an address. The members of 
the corps held written guarantees of amnesty, judgment by law, inquiry before 
punishment and a place for their sons; the consuls held treaty rights of inviolability; the 
strangers held compacts; the townsman held the sacred law's courts and the right to cry 
for God's justice at the gate. The population as such held nothing, and nothing in the 
record suggests it ever asked. 

The second is where those protections came from, and the pattern is consistent. The 
corps's charter came from a year of civil disorder among armed equals who could not 
destroy each other without destroying the chest that paid them all. The renegades’ 
place on the register came from a peace between two armed corporations. The 
kouloughlis' partial readmission came from the corps's need for men. The consuls’ 
rights came from navies and the strangers’ from their tribes’ independence. The 
townsman's protections alone were not won by bargaining; they were borrowed from 
the older law on which the corps's own title rested, and were as strong as the corps's 
need to be seen upholding it. 

The third is what made any of them more than words. The corps's written liberties 
held because the corps could remove the dey, and that power in turn rested on the 
soldiers’ physical access to the palace and the pay chest. When the dey carried the 
treasury into the citadel and guarded it with natives, every clause of the covenant 
remained in force and none of them could any longer be enforced upward. A liberty 
against an apparatus is only as durable as the holders’ ability to punish the apex for 
violating it. 

The fourth is the list of what was lost: the converted Jews’ place on the register in 
1580; the kouloughlis' access to the Kasba and the great offices after their failed seizure 
of it; the divan's voice, which went from a hall of shouting soldiers to a body no dey 
consulted; and, in 1817, the corps's power of removal itself. Each loss came, like each 
gain, from a fight inside the hierarchy. 

And last, what I expected and did not find. I expected an order with no liberty in it 
but rhetoric, because a conquest corporation ruling a subject population needs none. 
The corporation needed a great deal of it for itself, wrote it down, and bound its own 
judge to a law he could not bend by a hair. What it did not do was write down the one 
liberty that made the rest real, and it lost that one without a document, a debate or a 


battle — by letting its elected ruler carry the treasury uphill.

Three centuries of evidence 


T he Regency lasted three hundred and fourteen years, and in that time it was 

altered many times and risen against many times. That makes it a good place to 
test a claim about how orders change: that an armed settlement is altered only by armed. 
men — by a quarrel among those who hold it, by the exhaustion of the power that 
sustains it, or by conquest from outside — and never by the anger of those beneath it, 
however many they are. The test is simple. Set down every rising from below the 
evidence records and what became of it; set down every change that actually altered the 


arrangement and who made it; and then look at the end. 


The risings that lost 


The first recorded attempt to overturn the Turkish order in Algiers came from the 
townsmen within a few years of the conquest. The Laugier account, drawing on a 
manuscript history of Barbarossa, describes a plot in which the Algerines, allied with 
the Arabs of the Mitidja and in secret contact with the Spanish commander of the fort 
on the islet, agreed to rise and massacre the Turks and return the city to the King of 
Spain. Moors were to bring fruit and herbs to market with arms under their gowns; 
others were to fire the galleys drawn up on the shore; and when the Turks ran out to 
save their ships, the citizens would shut the gates and the fort's guns would open on 
them. "But the Accomplices were too numerous, and Barbaroussa too vigilant." He 
suspected the plan, put strong guards on the gates and at the galleys on the pretext of 
watching the Spaniards, and the enterprise became impossible without a blow being 
struck. 

The best-documented rising came a century later and far from the capital. In 1627, 
as Pierre Dan recorded it, a marabout of Tlemcen persuaded the principal men of the 
city that the Prophet had revealed to him the means of throwing off the rule of Algiers, 
and promised that his followers’ enemies’ muskets would not fire. He gathered, in 


Dan's figure, an army of some ten thousand Moors and Arabs. The divan sent a force

Dan puts at no more than eleven or twelve hundred janissaries, whose muskets fired; 
the rising broke; and the marabout and thirty-two of the principal men were flayed 
alive and their skins carried through Algiers on pikes. Ten thousand against twelve 
hundred is as clean a statement as the record offers of what numbers are worth against a 
paid and disciplined force. 

The captives of the city never rose at all. The Laugier account says they were 
numerous enough in every age to make themselves masters of the principal towns if 
they could have concerted it, "and were not deterred from any such Thoughts, by the 
dreadful Punishments inflicted" on the least sign of insurrection. 

And the free mountaineers and nomads, who held more of the country than the 
Regency did, resisted it for three centuries without ever changing it. Shaler records that 
the Kabyles "have always maintained their independence of the Algerine government," 
and the French reckoning of 1897 put the lands of the independent confederations at 
more than four times the area the Turks controlled uncontested. Independence kept 
the Regency out of the mountains. It never reached the Kasba, and the Regency never 


needed it to. 


The changes that happened 


Against those failures stand the changes that did alter the arrangement, and every 
one of them was made by armed men inside the order or by a power above it. 

The founding itself was the work of hired captains who killed their employer and 
borrowed an Ottoman warrant. The joining of the corps and the corsairs in the 1560s 
was a peace between two armed corporations, paid for by opening each one's income to 
the other. 

The corps's seizure of the government from the sultan's governors in the 
seventeenth century was, in the Laugier account's version, a quarrel over pay. The 
governors, it says, had taken over the public revenues and were "embezzling the Funds 
destined for the Payment of the Turkish Soldiers, whose Number was always 
deficient," and in the seventeenth century the soldiers sent a secret deputation to the 
Porte complaining of the governors’ tyranny and avarice, of their misuse of the funds 
sent from Constantinople, and of the troops’ pay left unpaid. The deputation asked 
that a man of judgment and experience "should be chosen out of the Troops with the 
Title of Dey,” accountable for the public revenue and for paying the troops in full. The 
account adds that the deputies warned that unless their grievances were met, the 
Ottoman power would soon be brought to an end by the Arabs and Moors, who were 
always plotting with the Christians. The ruled appear in this change only as the danger 


the armed faction named to win its case.

The floor hollowed from within and above 


The end of the Regency was prepared by three blows between 1816 and 1829, and 
none of them came from its subjects. 

The first came from outside. The bombardment of August 1816 by the British and 
Dutch fleets destroyed the navy and ended both Christian slavery and the corsair trade 
that had filled the hoard. Shaler, writing in the 1820s, says that since 1815 the Regency 
had derived no benefit from its corsairs, and his account of the year 1822 shows the 
corps's pay exceeding the treasury's receipts by more than four hundred thousand 
dollars a year. The fleet was rebuilt; the income was not. 

The second came from the apex. In 1817 Ali Khodja moved into the citadel with the 
public treasury and a native guard, "set the Turks at defiance,” and, in Shaler's 
judgment, planned to suppress the janissaries entirely; he persecuted the Turks through 
his short reign, and his successor Hussein kept to the citadel. The dey who faced France 
in 1830 was a ruler who had broken the corps's power over him and sat on the chest 
behind walls — and who therefore commanded a corps whose members no longer held 
the one guarantee that had made the order theirs. Shaler gives the ordinary Turkish 
garrison of Algiers in his day as fifteen hundred to two thousand men, principally 
young recruits and old veterans. 

The third came from above. The sultan who had once been able to close the corps's 
recruiting ports spent the 1820s calling on the Algerine fleet for his own war against the 


Greeks, and no source reached records any help from Istanbul to Algiers in 1830.

How each link priced its exit 


The French army landed at Sidi Ferruch on 14 June 1830, fought at Staoueli on the

founding promise of the conquest — sit beside clauses on the order's own hierarchy, 
and those clauses show what each of its members bargained for when the arrangement 
could no longer be defended. 

The dey bargained for himself. "Le Général en chef de l'armée francaise s'engage

Read against the hierarchy, those clauses say what each man's standing had always 
been reckoned in, and what could survive the order's end. The dey's standing was an 
office; the office was gone, and what he could carry away was the private fortune he 
claimed from the treasury he had sat on. The soldier's standing was a name on a register

provinces, whose offices had been drifting toward their sons; the makhzan chiefs, 
whose privileges any master would need; and the men of religion, whose property and 
lineage no dey had ever issued. What they did with it belongs to the next study.

indépendance," he wrote — I willingly forgive fortune the loss of my power, my wealth 
and my independence — but not, he went on, the opinion that his own temper had

Reading change and collapse against the framework 


The first thing three centuries of evidence show is that no rising from below altered 
the Regency. The townsmen's plot of the 1520s failed before it began, because the 


conspirators were many and the captain vigilant. The rising of Tlemcen in 1627 put 


PAT TERNS OF POWER 114 THE REGENGY OF ALGIERS 


perhaps ten thousand men against twelve hundred and ended with its leaders’ skins on 
pikes in the capital. The captives, numerous enough to seize the towns, never rose, 
because the punishments were known. The free tribes kept their independence for 
three hundred years and never touched the arrangement in Algiers. 

The second is that every change that did alter the order was made by armed men 
within it or by a power above or outside it: the captains who founded it, the two 
corporations that merged, the corps that took the government from unpaying 
governors, the dey who bought the pasha’s title, the factions who wrote the covenant, 
the kouloughlis who tried and failed, the bey of Oran who came within three leagues of 
the capital, the dey who broke the corps from the citadel, the fleets that ended the 
corsair trade, and the army that took the Kasba. Where the ruled appear in these 
changes, they appear as the danger one armed faction names to another, or as a guard 
one faction arms against the rest. 

The third is how the order ended. It was not exhausted fiscally: its treasury held 
nearly forty-nine million francs. It was hollowed in two ways, neither of them by its 
subjects. From outside, its income from the sea was cut off in 1816. From within, its 
apex broke the corps's power over him in 1817 and sat behind walls with the chest and 
a native guard. And then it was conquered by another armed hierarchy. The collapse 
combined a fracture at the top with a conquest from outside, and it came quickly 
because the men who held standing in the order held it in forms that could not survive 
conquest, and each of them bargained for what could. 

And last, what I expected and did not find. I expected the Regency's end to show an 
order held together by nothing but force breaking under the first real blow. What the 
convention shows instead is an order whose members, at the end, behaved exactly as 
they had for three hundred years: each defended what his own position was worth. The 
dey claimed his fortune. The corps that had killed deys for their pay accepted two 
months of it and a ship home. The people under them, who had never been able to 


change the arrangement, played no part in its ending either.

The direction of the instrument

shape. The Regency's own surviving paperwork counts cargoes, shares, offices,

What the price list shows is sharper than any of these counts. The Laugier account 
of the 1720s describes how the trade was organised so that the state's share was hidden

captive was put up again and delivered to the highest bidder. The price of the first sale

Perquisite of the Deylik, and is often equal to the first. Because the Buyers, knowing 
that the second Sale determines the Delivery of the Slaves, do not vie with one another

Price of their Ransom,” besides duties on bringing captives in and sending them out. 
Haedo, a century and a half earlier, describes the same principle in an older form: a 
Christian was cried in the market for three days and then brought to the ruler, who 
might take him for himself at the price the auction had reached. 

A sale that proclaims a human being's quality and trade, runs a first auction that is 
designed to fail so that a second can capture the difference for the treasury, and taxes

seventh.

The metabolic test 


The question whether the Regency lifted a population above what its land could 
feed unaided, and then held the power to destroy it by withdrawing that support, has 
to be asked of two populations separately, because it has different answers for each. 

For the countryside the answer, on the evidence reached, is no. The Regency built 
no works that lifted the rural population. What it did instead was depress it: a general 
prohibition on exporting the country’s produce, sold back as monopolies, left land 
beyond the towns nearly worthless, and the tax columns and the beys' agents took, in 
Shaler's words, all that was tangible. Shaler describes the result: "This intolerable 
oppression has depopulated the country, by driving the inhabitants from the fertile 
plains into less accessible positions in the mountains, and into the borders of the 
desert.” There was no metabolic note to call, because no apparatus had lifted the plains. 
And the disruption the order imposed on them was paid, on Shaler's account, in 
migration rather than in mortality: the people left the plains for the mountains and the 
desert edge, where the order did not follow. That belongs on the credit side of the 
accounting. An order that had closed the mountains to them would have converted the 
same oppression into deaths. 

For the city of Algiers the answer is yes, and the apparatus that did the lifting is on 
the record. A city Shaler put at perhaps fifty thousand people lived on grain delivered in 
kind by the tribes — two hundred thousand measures of wheat a year in 1822 — on 
the flow of prizes and ransoms from the sea, on the tribute of Christian powers, and on 
a harbour that the slaves of the state maintained by hauling stone to the mole every day 
of every year. The note on that lift was serviced in three currencies: coercion in the 
countryside, which produced the grain; the labour of captives, which kept the harbour; 
and the fear of European shipping, which produced the prizes and the tribute. When 
the bombardment of 1816 ended the corsair trade, the third currency failed, and the 
city's support shifted onto the hoard in the Kasba, which by 1822 was paying more 
than four hundred thousand dollars a year beyond what came in. The note was being 
called from outside, and the order did not survive long enough to see it fall due. 

The traffic in captives was itself the operation the framework describes as a transfer. 
A corsait who took a merchant ship broke the vessel that would have carried its crew 
home and moved the crew onto the works of Algiers, where their labour, fed by the 
grain of the tribes, sustained the harbour from which the next ship would be taken. 
The capture and the labour were one act. The captive’s own metabolic support — his 
wage, his household, his place in the economy of Marseilles or Genoa or Palermo — 


was not destroyed so much as converted into Algiers's.

And for the captive, exit was closed in the way that turns disruption into a body 
count. The Laugier account says that owners discouraged adult captives from 
converting, because conversion did not free them and would end the ransom; the door 
out was a price set by the captor and paid, if at all, by strangers. How many captives 
died waiting for it is not recorded in any source reached here. The number is missing 
because the redemptionists who kept lists recorded the names of those they ransomed, 


and the dead were not ransomed. 


The forgetting 


The violence of the Regency does not survive in a monument that could be argued 
with. It survives, in the record reached here, in the name of an hour. 

Venture de Paradis, describing the city in the 1780s, records that the minarets of 
Algiers raised a small flag at each canonical hour of prayer and lowered it when the call 
was done, and that the hour an hour and a half after midday, when the noon flag came 
down, was called bandiera bassa: "c'est le moment oti on fait donner la bastonnade 
dans la maison du dey et ot on appelle ordinairement les Européens” — the moment 
when the bastinado is given in the dey’s house and when Europeans are ordinarily 
summoned, whenever there was business to settle with them. Midday itself was 
bandtera arriva. 

The words are not Turkish or Arabic. They belong to the third language of the city, 
which Haedo described in the 1580s: "la que los moros y turcos llaman franca, o hablar 
franco” — the one the Moors and Turks call Franca, or Frankish speech — not the 
language of any one Christian nation but a mixture of many, through which the Turks 
and Moors of Algiers dealt with Christians. It was the speech of the captives, the 
renegades and the merchants, the language in which a master gave orders to a slave and 
a dey questioned a stranger. And in that language the city named an hour of its day for 
the moment the flag came down and the beating began. 

Everyone in Algiers knew what time bandiera bassa was. No one needed to decode 
it, because it made no claim about anything. It was simply when the afternoon started 
— the hour of the prayer that the government's punishments followed, named by the 
people whose labour kept the harbour, in their own mixed tongue. The violence of the 


order had become a way of telling the time. 


Reading the grim accounting against the framework 


The first finding is about the instrument. The Regency counted cargoes, prize 


shares, offices and pay, and did not count people. Its enemies counted their own: the

British dead of 1816 ship by ship, the ransomed captives by name. The people the

describes and does not number. The best-documented deaths in three centuries are 
foreigners’; the destruction of the governed plains is a single sentence about 
depopulation. No total of the Regency’s dead is possible from the sources reached, and 
none is given. 

The second is that the largest continuous cost of the order was its traffic in captives, 
and that its price list is better evidence of that cost than its counts. The counts are a 
redemptionist's standing figures of about twenty-five thousand in Algiers and thirty-six 
thousand across Barbary in the 1630s, and a modern extrapolation to a million or more 
across two and a half centuries whose replacement rate is disputed. The price list shows 
a market organised to hide the state's share in a designed second auction, a ransom 
taxed at ten per cent, a public proclamation of each captive's quality and trade, and a 
price that rose tenfold in forty years as the supply of captives fell. 

The third is the metabolic test. The Regency did not lift its countryside; it depressed 
it, and the countryside paid in migration to the mountains and the desert rather than in 
deaths, because the order left that exit open. The Regency did lift its capital, on grain 
taken in kind, on the labour of captives and on the fear of European shipping, and the 
capture of a ship's crew and their labour on the mole were a single act of transfer from 
one metabolic system to another. The note on the capital's lift was called from outside 
in 1816. For the captive, whose exit was closed and priced, the same disruption became 
deaths that no one counted. 

The fourth is the forgetting. The violence of the order survives not in any claim but 
in the name of an hour — bandiera bassa, the time the prayer flag came down and the 
beating started, named in the mixed language of the city's slaves. 

And last, what I expected and did not find. I expected the tax columns to be the 
order's largest hidden destruction and hoped the record would let me bound it. The 
record cannot. What it can bound, through its prices, is the trade in human beings, and 
what it counts most exactly is a morning in 1816 on which a hundred and thirty-eight

In Lieu of Slaves 


Convergence and divergence — the Regency read against the Order of St John, the Mamluk

Alike placed, alike answered 


We two orders that never heard of each other arrive at the same arrangement, 
the tempting explanations are a spirit of the age or a hidden law of history. 
Neither is needed. A position poses a problem; the problem has only a few workable 
answers; the men who hold the position can see only some of those; and men placed 
alike, looking at the same short list, choose the same item. This part reads the Regency 
of Algiers against five other studies in the collection where that has happened, says 
where the resemblance ends and what made the difference, and then closes on the best 


case that can be made for the Regency and the answer to it. 


In lieu of slaves 


The first convergence is the plainest, and it concerns what an order does with work 
that slaves used to do when a stronger power forbids the slaves. 

The bombardment of Algiers in 1816 ended Christian slavery there by force. The 
Regency’s harbour still had to be kept up against the northern swell, its dockyards still 
had to be worked, and the captives who had done that work — "the Slaves of the 
Deylik,” as the Laugier account put it, "kept at Work the whole Year" hauling stone to 
the mole — were gone. Shaler records what took their place. Hard labour in chains, he 
writes, had become since the suppression of Christian slavery the commonest 
punishment in practice, "and in a measure furnishes labourers in lieu of slaves upon the 
public works." A category of person whose compulsion no treaty forbade — the 
convicted criminal — was put where the category the treaty forbade had been. 

The collection's study of the American South finds the same move half a century 
later, made by men who had certainly never read Shaler on Algiers. After emancipation, 
Mississippi's amended vagrancy act of November 1865 required every black person to 
carry written evidence of employment each January; one found without it was a 


vagrant; a vagrant could be fined; and a man who could not pay the fine could be hired 


PATTERNS OF POWER 125 THE REGENCY OF ALGIERS 


out to pay it. The study observes that this joined the settlement to the Thirteenth 
Amendment's own exception, which abolished slavery and involuntary servitude except 
as a punishment for crime: the settlement, it concludes, did not need to defy the 
amendment, because it used the clause the amendment left open. The same study 
follows the line into convict leasing. And the collection's study of Haiti finds a third 
version, made by a state founded by former slaves themselves: a republic that forbade 
anyone to own a cultivator nonetheless compelled its cultivators to work, in the Code 
rural of 1826, and that study closed on the recognition that the freedom and its price 
were written in the same instruments. 

The position is the same in all three. An order whose works run on forced labour is 
forbidden, by a stronger power or by its own founding act, to hold people as property. 
Its menu is short: pay wages it cannot or will not afford, let the works decay, or find a 
category of person whose compulsion the prohibition itself still permits. Criminal 
punishment is on every such list, because no prohibition of slavery in any of these 
societies forbade the punishment of crime. Algiers, Mississippi and Port-au-Prince 
found the same door because it was the only one the prohibition left unlocked. 

The divergence is instructive. The prohibition imposed on Algiers covered only 
Christians; the black slaves brought across the Sahara remained slaves until they 
converted, so that convict labour in Algiers replaced only the particular work that 
Christian captives had done on the harbour and in the yards. The prohibition imposed 
on the South covered everyone, and the answer it produced therefore had to be written 
as a law about vagrancy that fell on the population the old law had enslaved. Haiti's 
prohibition was the founders’ own, and its compulsion had to be written as a law about 


agriculture. The narrower the ban, the narrower the substitute. 


The nephew 


The second convergence is the one that most directly tests what this study was built 
to test: what becomes of inheritance in an order whose rules forbid it. 

The Regency’s register conveyed nothing heritable. A murdered dey's sons went 
back to a common soldier's pay. And yet, as the audit of its hierarchy found, the office 
of dey passed in the middle of the eighteenth century by designation within a family. In 
October 1745 a dying dey "abdiqua en faveur de son neveu Ibrahim, alors kheznadji," 
and the sultan's firman of the next year confirmed the new dey as the dead man’s 
"neveu et son lieutenant.” The Laugier account had already named the mechanism in 
the 1720s: when succession was not carried by violence, "a Relation or Confident of the 


deceased" was proclaimed. And the last of the corps's deys but one, having moved into

the citadel with the treasury, appeared to Shaler to have formed the project "of 
rendering the sovereignty hereditary in his family." 

The collection's study of the Teutonic Order in Prussia found the same thing in a 
body whose rule forbade its members to have families at all. Its officers, elected from 
among the brothers, were audited monthly and could be deposed; the office, the study 
says, conferred nothing a man could keep. And it identifies the appearance of a 
transmissible benefit as the leading indicator of that constraint's failure — which 
arrived, in a body founded on the rule that no member had kin, as two uncle-to- 
nephew successions in a row. Its tenth module follows the line to its end: the four men 
who converted a celibate corporation into a dynasty. The study of the Mamluk 
sultanate, whose ruling caste excluded its own sons, states the general principle in a 
sentence: abolishing heredity does not solve the succession problem but distributes it, 
because a loyalty attached to a person dies with him. 

The position is the same. The head of a corporation that forbids inheritance holds 
more than any other member and loses all of it at death; beside him stands a treasurer 
or a lieutenant who is best placed to kill him or succeed him. The menu has three items: 
purge every rival and accept that one will eventually win; turn the most dangerous man 
into an heir by designating him, which in practice means choosing someone bound by 
blood; or, if a warrant can be found that allows it, convert the office into a patrimony 
outright. The deys of Algiers and the grand masters of Prussia found the second item 
independently, and in both the nephew was the form it took, because a nephew was the 
nearest thing to a son that a rule against sons would tolerate. 

The divergence lies in the third item. Prussia's conversion succeeded; Algiers's did 
not. The Teutonic brothers were celibate and had nothing to leave but the office, and 
when the corporation's head finally took the office for himself and his heirs, the 
corporation became a dynasty. The deys married, their soldiers married, and heritability 
leaked into the Regency through many small channels at once — the kouloughlis, the 
beys' sons who "in some instances succeeded their fathers," the children of the great 
men who drew the top rate of pay from birth — without ever reaching the one step 
that would have made the order a dynasty. Ali Khodja's attempt died with him of 
plague in 1818, and the next attempt was preempted by France. And an attempt at 
dynasty in Algiers needed the consent of a warrant-holder in Istanbul who had no 
reason to give it, since a dynasty would have ended the petition for investiture on which 


his title in Algiers rested.

The sons who could not join 


The Mamluk comparison sharpens a finding about the boundary of the ruling 
corporation. In Egypt, the study found, the mamluks' own sons — the awlad al-nas, 
free Muslims by birth — could not join the ranks their fathers held, so that the 
freeborn stood below those who had once been slaves; the ruling caste did not 
reproduce itself, and every generation had to be bought again as boys on a foreign 
market. The boundary was drawn on something no subject could acquire: to have been 
enslaved as a child abroad. 

Algiers faced the same problem — its sons were born of local women and formed by 
their mothers’ world — and reached for the same answer, excluding the kouloughlis 
from the Kasba and the great offices after they tried to seize it. But the Regency's 
boundary was drawn on something that could be acquired from outside, and so it 
moved. Algiers did not buy boys; it recruited grown volunteers from Anatolia and 
admitted Christian converts, who married, had sons and settled. A renegade could 
become an agha because his qualifying event — conversion — was something he could 
do; a kouloughli was readmitted when the corps needed men, and the covenant of 1749 
declared that no objection would be made to the enrolment of serving janissaries' sons. 
What the Regency never admitted was the Arabic- and Berber-speaking native. The 
Mamluk study's principle holds, with a correction: factional residue accumulates 
protections but never admission where the boundary is drawn on what no subject can 
acquire, and in Algiers the boundary was on birth in the Levant or conversion from 


Christendom — both of which any foreigner could satisfy and no native could. 


A floor that sells 


The Haiti study found that pay is the working definition of who owns a floor of 
force, and that the right to appoint its officers is the floor's title deed. The Regency's 
corps is the collection’s clearest instance of a floor that came to own itself by that 
definition: paid first from Istanbul, then by the sultan's governors from Algiers's 
revenue, and finally from a chest in the Kasba that its own covenant called "notre 
demeure,” its officers were appointed by seniority within the corps and its commander 
could be removed by it. 

The Teutonic study adds what happens to a floor whose pay is the only thing 
holding it. The Order's capital, it records, was not stormed; it was sold on 6 June 1457 
by its own unpaid garrison to the enemy, for arrears. A purchased floor, the study 
concludes, does not desert — it sells. The Regency's floor was not unpaid; its chest was 


full in 1830. But when a conqueror had made the register worthless, the corps behaved

exactly as a floor constituted by pay behaves. It sold its position for the best price

Where the fracture sits 


The Teutonic study's reading of change gives a portable rule: the fracture comes in 
the stratum with standing and means whose holdings do not depend on the identity of 
the ruler — find the men who could change sovereigns without changing anything 
they own. In Algiers that rule points at the beys, whose offices were drifting into their 
families and who commanded their own cavalry, and at the makhzan chiefs and men of 
religion, whose standing any master would need. The most dangerous rising of the 
Regency’s last generation was the bey of Oran's, who in 1813-14 "marched an army 
within three leagues of Algiers." And the men whose standing survived the convention 
of July 1830 were precisely those: not the dey or the soldiers, whose standing was office 
and register, but the provincial governors, the collecting tribes and the religious 


lineages, whose holdings did not run through the Kasba. 


The mirror across the water 


The Order of St John is the Regency's structural sibling and its mirror: a corsairing 
corporation on an island, recruiting its fighting members from outside the population

that the boundary will be in the licences rather than in the manifestos — a

manner, because a war that is also a business needs to know which ships it may take. 

The St John study also finds that the two corsos together made the central 
Mediterranean a place where an unprotected boat was cargo, so that a hostile sea served 
each order as a free restraint on its own subjects’ leaving. The Regency’s captives found

Reading the case against the collection 


Set against these others, the Regency's own contributions can be stated briefly. An 
order can settle itself on title to flows alone — the sermon, the coin, tribute, a share of 
prizes — provided the positions it distributes are institutional, even where none is 
heritable. The one claim to which every title runs back binds its holders, who cannot 
set it aside when it is inconvenient without dissolving their own titles. Withheld 
formation is cheap for as long as the formation left in place already honours the 
conqueror's warrant, and costs everything the day the warrant fails. Exit is set by what a 
man is worth to the order held. A state can hide its share of a traffic in human beings 
inside the design of an auction. An order can publish its acts while withdrawing its 
deliberation, even from its own soldiers. Violence at the apex of a non-hereditary order 
can be periodic rather than constant, and quieted by heritability creeping in through 
the treasurer's office. The written liberties of a ruling corporation rest on an unwritten 
one — the power to remove its head — and can be lost without a word by moving the 


treasury uphill. And violence can survive an order as the name of an hour. 


The strongest case for the Regency 


The best defender of the Regency would make the following case, and it deserves to 
be heard at its full weight. 

In 1510 the central Maghrib was being taken by Spain. Oran, Mers el-Kébir and 
Bougie had fallen; a Spanish fort sat at the mouth of Algiers's harbour; the kingdom of 
Tlemcen paid the wages of Spanish soldiers; the Muslim kingdom of Granada, whose 
exiles lived on this coast, had already been destroyed. Within twenty years the brothers 
and the corps they founded had taken the Spanish fort from the mouth of Algiers's 
harbour, and in 1541 they held the city against the armada of the emperor Charles V. 
For three centuries after that, the country remained Muslim, Arabic-speaking and 
governed by its own sacred law, when the most powerful state on the other shore had 


shown what it did with Muslims it ruled.

The order that achieved this did not remake the society it protected. It left the 
country's religion, schools, courts, endowments, saints and calendar exactly as it found 
them. It conducted no forced conversions and imposed no language. It taxed the 
townsman lightly — customs on a trade of more than a million dollars brought in 
twenty thousand — and gave him, in the judgment of an American consul who 
disliked it, better security for person and property than any city in the world. It placed 
pious endowments beyond its own reach. It bound its own commander by written 
covenant to judge by God's law without deviating by a hair split forty times. 

It was open to talent in a way almost no state of its age was. A soldier from Anatolia 
or a sailor from Genoa could rise to command it, and no hereditary aristocracy was 
allowed to settle on the country and devour it. It turned over its rulers often, but its 
institutions endured for three centuries with a standing force that never exceeded a few 
thousand men in the capital — which is to say that most of the people it governed were 
not held down by force at all. 

Its war at sea was the war every Mediterranean power waged: licensed, adjudicated, 
recorded in a register with a published share table, conducted under a flag and limited 
by treaty — the same war the Knights of Malta waged from the other shore under the 
cross. Its captives could be ransomed, and converts among them rose to command. 
And the powers that called it a nest of pirates and slavers were in the same centuries 
carrying Africans across the Atlantic on a scale the Regency never approached: the 
modern Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database reckons some twelve and a half million 
embarked between 1501 and 1866, more than three million of them under the British 
flag, most in the century before a British fleet bombarded Algiers in the name of 
abolition. When the Regency fell, it fell to a foreign army, not to its own people, and 


what replaced it was worse. 


The answer 


Every sentence of that case is true, and none of it can be separated from what it 
defends. 

The fleet that drove Spain off the coast and held Algiers in 1541 was the fleet that 
took ships and sold their crews at the Batistan, where brokers walked them round 
"proclaiming the Quality, Trade, and Price of the Slave.” It was not a defence that also 
happened to trade in captives. The prizes paid the guns, the captives paid the prizes, 
and the captives kept the harbour from which the next ships were taken. The 
protection of the coast and the traffic in human beings were a single operation 


described from the two shores it faced.

The order left the country's world untouched because it had decided never to admit 
the country. Forming the population in the corps's image would have made it eligible 
for the corps, and the settlement's first rule was that the Moors and Arabs, as the 
Laugier account said flatly, "are absolutely excluded.” The light hand on belief, the 
untaxed conscience and the respected endowments were the same decision as the closed 
register, seen from outside instead of from inside. And the same abstention that made 
the order cheap for three centuries left the country, on the day the warrant failed, with 
nothing in its picture of the world that attached it to the men who had worn the 
sultan's title. 

The openness to talent and the closed door were one boundary. A renegade from 
Genoa could become an agha because the qualification was something a foreigner 
could acquire and a native could not; the soldier from Anatolia rose because he was not 
a Kabyle. The covenant that bound the agha by a split hair was written by men who 
kept the country off the register, and its guarantees protected no one who was not one 
of them. 

The security of the townsman and the hooks at Bab Azoun were one police. The 
townsman's safe house and the Jew's permission to pass the gate on two days of the 
week were one city. The light customs duty and the monopoly that made the 
tribesman’s land nearly worthless were one treasury. 

And the comparison with the Atlantic is true and is not an acquittal. The scale of 
one crime does not reduce another; the man chained in the bagnio at Algiers was not 
consoled by the statistics of Liverpool, and the African in the hold of a Liverpool ship 
was not consoled by the price list of the Batistan. What the comparison does establish is 
that the word the tribute-payers used — pirates — described a licensed maritime war 
that their own states conducted too, and that the moral language they aimed at Algiers 
was not available to them on the evidence of their own ships. 

So the case for the Regency and the case against it are not two sets of facts to be 
weighed. They are one order seen twice. The corps that saved the coast from Spain was 
the corps that sold the Sicilian; the self-restraint that preserved the country's faith was 
the exclusion that kept it outside for three hundred years; the discipline that bound the 
agha to law was the brotherhood of men who ruled the country as strangers. The 
Regency held the central Maghrib for three centuries against Christendom by treating 
the sea as a hunting ground and the land as a field it would not enter. It was durable 
because it asked the governed for so little, and it asked for so little because it never 
intended to count them among its own. When it ended, the corps took two months' 
pay and sailed home, and the country it had defended and never admitted was left to 


face what came next without it.

A note on sources. 


Quoted exactly, and how verified. All quotations from the Regency's own sources 
in this part — Shaler on convict labour "in lieu of slaves" and on rendering the 
sovereignty hereditary; Devoulx on the dey who abdicated in favour of his nephew; the 
firman of 1746; the Laugier account on "a Relation or Confident,” on the slave market 
and on the exclusion of Moors and Arabs; Plantet on the janissaries’ gratuity; Shaler on 
the bey of Oran — were verified against the printed page earlier in this study. 

The comparison volumes, and how they were read. Five completed studies in the 
collection were consulted at first hand, by the method of the operational note: the full 
text was written to disk and only the sections bearing on this comparison were read. 
From The American South: the passages on the Mississippi vagrancy act of 1865, the 
Thirteenth Amendment's exception and convict leasing. From The Teutonic Order and 
the State in Prussia: its statement of what the case added to the framework, its passages 
on uncle-to-nephew succession, and the opening and framework reading of its tenth 
module on the sale of Marienburg and the conversion of the corporation into a 
dynasty. From The Order of St John: its statement of what the case added and its 
passages on the corso, the slave market and the boundaries of holy war. From Mamluk 
Egypt: its statement of what the case added, its outline, and its passages on the awlad 
al-nas. From Haiti (in project knowledge): its statements on pay as the ownership of a 
floor and on the Code rural, and its close. These volumes are paraphrased and credited; 
their own quotations of primary sources are not re-quoted here. The studies of 
Bukhara, the Ottoman classical age, Venice and Carthage, all relevant to this case, were 
not read in this part. 

Modern figures. The size of the transatlantic slave trade is taken from summaries of 
the Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database (Voyages), which estimates about 12.5 million 
persons embarked between 1501 and 1866 and about 3.26 million carried under the 
British flag; the database was not queried directly. The comparison between those 
figures and the Barbary estimates of Robert Davis, given with its method in the 
preceding part, is a comparison of incompatible bases and is used only to establish 
order of magnitude, not a ratio. Weak links. The convergences are drawn from 
finished studies whose own sources were not re-examined here; the account of the 
Teutonic Order's conversion into a dynasty rests on that study's summary rather than 
on its primary evidence. Drops. A date for Britain's abolition of its own slave trade was 


not stated, because no source for it was reached in this part.
