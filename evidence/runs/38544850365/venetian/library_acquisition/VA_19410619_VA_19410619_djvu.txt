Domestic Animals 
By-law Passed By’ 
Decisive Majority 

Result of Saturday Poll 
Shows 92 in Favor ’ of By¬ 
law and 45 Against 


The Domesti Animals by-law of the 
Municipal District of Royal, which 
will prohibit domestic animals ^rom 
running at large within > the Munici¬ 
pality, was passed by a decisive ma¬ 
jority when proprietory electors of 
the district voted upon it last Satur¬ 
day. The result of the poll, declared 
by Returning Officer, William Munro, 
was 92 votes in favor of passing the 
by-law and 46 against the by-law. 

Animals which the owners will have 
to restrain from running at large in¬ 
clude horses, asses, mules, cattle, 
goats, geese, sheep and swine. The 
by-law affects the whole municipal 
district and will be in force year- 
round. The new law will enable far¬ 
mers to remove their fences if they so 
desire, allowing them to farm right 
up to the road edge and have better 
control over the weeds which flourish 
so thickly along tie roadsides. At the 
present time, any farmer who re¬ 
moves the fences which protect his 
crop is simply inviting stock which 
may be running at large to come in and 
help themselves. 

The by-law will come into effect 16 
days after it is finally passed by the 
council. .

ment which swept the cotintry, and held political control 
for 14 years. For twenty-five years or more Henry Wise 
Wood was the dynamic exponent of policies for the bet¬ 
terment of agricultural conditions. Though the politi¬ 
cal glory of the U:F.A. waned in the course of'years, 
yet it is significant that the res;>ect and regard for the 
old leader has never diminished. He rounded out an in¬ 
spiring life at the age of 81 years. It may be that he 
suffered disappointments in that long life, and that in 
some respects fulfilment of his dreams was not realized. 
But'few men have ever had such an invigorating influ¬ 
ence on the life of this province. 

Those who opposed the political rise of the United 
Farmers of Alberta, on the charge of “class” govern¬ 
ment were met with Mr. Wood’s frank admission that it 
admittedly was a class group, but that the farmers were 
the majority class in the province and entitled to what 
advantage this might give them. On the whole, they 
gave progressive government, less radical than expected 
and probably more advanced in many respects than any 
other party would have given. And always in the back¬ 
ground was the influence of Henry Wise Wood. 

Although the hopes of this leader may have suffered 
eclipse in the political field, he had reason for more sub¬ 
stantial satisfaction in the success of the Alberta Wheat 
Pool, a pioneer co-operative movement. Other co-opera¬ 
tive projects lagged behind his vision of what might be, 
but he has lived to see a measure of fulfilment in this 
respect also. 

In this world there are many strong leaders who fall 
from high eminence into disrepute and disregard. There 
are others who fight for a life-time for some forlorn 
cause without seeing a gleam of fruition in their dreams 
for humanity. Henry Wise Wood was fortunate. His 
ability and sincerity were never questioned, and his 
name will always be cherished as one who loved this 
province and served it well.
