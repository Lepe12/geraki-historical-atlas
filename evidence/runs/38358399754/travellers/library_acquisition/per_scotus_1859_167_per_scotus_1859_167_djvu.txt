To the Hon. the Dist. Court of the U. S., in and for the east. dist. 
of La., in admiralty sitting: 


Joseph W. Stanton and William L. Palmer, who reside in the 
city of New Orleans, and are there associated in business as com- 
mercial partners under the style of J. W. Stanton & Co., and Hen- 
ry C. Hopper, who also resides in said New Orleans, owners of the 
steam tow-boat ‘“‘Anglo Celt,’’ hereinafter mentioned, exhibit 
their libel against the steamship ‘‘Louisiana,’’ now lying in the 
port of New Orleans, in the district aforesaid, and within the ad- 
miralty and maritime jurisdiction of this hon. court, her engines, 
machinery, boats, tackle, apparel and furniture, and against all 
persons lawfully intervening for their interest therein, in a cause of 
collision, civil and maritime. 

And thereupon your libellants allege and articulately propound 
as follows: 

Ist. That the said tow-boat, at the time when the cause of ac- 
tion hereafter mentioned arose, was lawfully engaged, under the 
laws of the United States, in commerce and navigation, and par-

ticularly in the business of towing vessels up and down the river 
Mississippi, and in and out to sea. 

2d. That on the 21st May, 1855, the said tow-boat, whereof your 
libellant, Henry C. Hopper, was master, and being then tight, 
staunch and strong, well manned and appointed, and in every re- 
spect fitted for the voyage, left the port of New Orleans, at 9 
o'clock p. m., with the ship Neptune in tow on the starboar 
side, and bound down the river Mississippi; that said tow-boat 
proceeded on said voyage, keeping the middle of the river as nearly 
as possible, and pursuing the usual and proper track of tows 
descending the river; that while thus descending the river, and 
when opposite to Wilkinson’s plantation, or thereabouts, between 
2 velo or 3 o’clock a. m. of the 22d May ult., the officers of said 
tow-boat perceived the said steamship ‘‘Louisiana’”’ ascending the 
river on the left or eastern shore, in the bend of the river above 
Point a la Hache. 

That as said vessels neared each other, the said steamship, still 
keeping the eastern shore, gave the usual signal that she wanted 
to keep that shore, and the said tow-boat responded by the proper 
signal, and pursued her course down the middle of the river. 

That the said steamship, instead of keeping the eastern shore 
according to the signal aforesaid, as she approached the point above 
the bend, in which she had been running apparently, took a sheer 
and ran from under said point directly across the track of said 
tow-boat. 

That instantly upon perceiving the change in the direction of 
the said steamship, the said tow-boat put her helm hard a-port, 
stopped her engines, and commenced backing, in order to escape if 
possible the impending collision; but notwithstanding every pos- 
sible effort was made for that purpose, the said steamship came in 
collision with the said tow-boat, striking her on the larboard bow 
with such force as to cut her nearly half through. 

That by the force of the blow thus received, the said tow-boat 
was thrown across the current of the river, and her stern separated 
from the ship ‘‘Neptune,’’ then in tow as aforesaid, so violently as 
to part all the after-fastenings, and let the ship drift entirely free 
of en: That the said tow-boat and the said steamship then drifted 
together to a short distance below the point aforesaid, and the said 
steamship going ahead, forced said tow-boat towards the eastern 
shore, till she finally sunk at the place where she now lies. 

3d. That the said tow-boat was in all things duly provided as 
aforesaid for her intended voyage; that the night was clear and 
favorable for the prosecution of the voyage; the said tow-boat car- 
ried proper and sufficient lights, viz., a red signal-light forward, 
and a white or common signal-light aft; and that everything ap- 
pertaining to the duty of the officers and crew of said flai-boat, 
every diligence and proper precaution, was observed; and that the

gence of the steamship ‘‘Louisiana,’’ and the officers and crew

4th. That the said tow-boat ‘‘Anglo Celt,’’ at the time of the 
collision aforesaid, was of the full and true value of $32,000, and 
libellants have reason to fear that she will prove a total loss; but 
that in the event that the exertions now being made to raise the 
said tow-boat prove successful, libellants reserve the right to amend 
this libel, and to claim such damages as the circumstances may

5th: That the said collision occurred in the river Mississippi, 
where the tide ebbs and flows, and within the admiralty and mari- 
time jurisdiction of this hon. court, and that all and singular the 
premises are true; in verification whereof, if denied, libellants 
crave leave to refer to the proof to be hereafter furnished. 

6th. That by the laws and usages of the admiralty, libellants 
have a lien and privilege on said steamship ‘‘Louisiana,’’ her en- 
gines, machinery, tackle, apparel, and furniture, for said sum of 
thirty-two thousand dollars. : 

Wherefore, the premises being considered, your libellants pray 
that process in due form of law be issued against said steamship 
‘Louisiana,’ her engines, machinery, tackle, apparel, and furni- 
ture, and that after due proceedings this honorable court decree in 
favor of libellants for the damage aforesaid, to wit, thirty-two 
thousand dollars, with interest, and all costs for such other relief 
as justice may require.

To the Hon. Theo. H. McCaleb, the judge of the Dist. Court of 
the U. S. in and for the east’n dist. of La., in admiralty sit- 
ting: 

And now Israel C. Harris and H’y Morgan, of New Orleans, 
composing the commercial firm of Harris & Morgan, and Charles 
Morgan, of New York, and Jeremiah Smith, of Galveston, Texas, 
appear before this hon. court, and for answer to the libel and com- 
plaint of J. W. Stanton and William L. Palmer, merchants and 
co-partners under the style of J. W. Stanton & Co., and of Henry 
C. Hopper, all of New Orleans, in the aforesaid district, against 
the steamship ‘‘ Louisiana,’’ her engines, machinery, &c., against 
all persons lawfully intervening for their interest therein, (these 
respondents being the true and lawful owners of said steamship,) 
do allege and propound as follows: 

Ist. That these respondents are ignorant of the matters con- 
tained in the Ist and 4th articles of the said libel; and as to the 
2d, 3d, 5th, and 6th, and the matters therein contained, they be- 
lieve that the same are in a great part falsely alleged, and that 
the truth is as hereafter alleged. 

2d. That on the 22d day of May, A. D. 1855, between the hours 
of 2 and 3 o’clock a. m., the steamship ‘‘ Louisiana’’ was ascend- 
ing the river Mississippi, bound for the port of New Orleans, and 
was then in good order and well and sufficiently equipped and 
manned ; that whilst said steamship was proceeding on her voy- 
age ascending the river, following the east or right bank, and 
keeping as close thereto as it was prudent and usual for vessels to 
keep, and following the proper and customary track of steamships 
ascending the river; and when she had reached a few miles above 
Point a la Hache, and was close to the said eastern bank, in her 
— position, and between the court-house and church on said 

ank, in said river, the officers and oe on duty and on the 
look-out discovered directly above said steamship an object. close to 
the eastern bank presenting the appearance of trees on said bank, 
and instantly afterwards discovered the yards and masts of a ves- 
sel, and then a form of a tow-boat, bearing right down upon them; 
that immediately the signal was given on the ‘‘ Louisiana,’’ by one 
blast of the whistle, that it was her intention to go to the night, 
and that her helm was at once put hard to port, but that said sig- 
nal was not responded to according to the law and usage of the

river; that said tow-boat continuing to come on towards said steam-

tow-boat fr .-n the ship she had in tow, and causing the said tow- 
boat to sink almost immediately, and before all those on board of 
her had time to escape and be saved, although every effort in the 
power of those on the ‘‘ Louisiana’ was made for their rescue ; 
that the stern of the tow-boat having sunk first, her bows were 
held up by her rigging, which was entangled on the bows of the 
‘¢ Louisiana,’’ and the Louisiana, then lying at right angles with

of making further inroad into the said tow-boat without moving

rigging of the tow-boat, the bows of which thereupon sank ; and 
that thereupon the Louisiana proceeded up the river to her port of 
destination, having on board of her all the officers and those of 
the crew of the said tow-boat (then discovered to be the ‘‘ Anglo 
Celt’’) who had been rescued from the collision. 

3d. That the said ‘‘ Louisiana’’ had on board of her at the time

an insufficient watch on deck, no proper look-out, and had not the 
customary lights or such as were sufficient to make her course and 

sition visible in time to have prevented the said collision, if it had 

een possible so to do. 

4th. That said collision was in no manner whatever attributable 
to the ‘‘ Louisiana,’’ or to the fault of those on board of her, and 
that everything was done by her tc prevent the same; but that the 
same was caused wholly by the fault, negligence, and want of 
skill of those on board said tow-boat ‘‘ Anglo Celt,’’ by their fail- 
ing to keep burning on her the necessary and usual signal-lights ; 
by their being out of the proper track for vessels descending the 
river at that place; by their failing to give or to answer the sig- 
nals proper for boats meeting one another ; by their not having a 
proper watch on the look-out; and by the unskilful manner in which 
she was navigated. 

5th. That all and singular the premises are true, in verification 
whereof, if denied, respondents crave leave to refer to the deposi- 
tions and proofs to be by them exhibited in this cause.

Robert Miller, a witness for the libellants, having been duly 
sworn, deposes: I was employed as pilot on the ‘‘Anglo Celt’’ at 
the time of her collision with the ‘‘Louisiana;’’ I was on duty 
when the collision occurred; the tow-boat left the city at about 4 
past 9 p. m., on the 2d May, 1855; we had the ship Neptune in 
tow on the starboard bow; no other vessel in tow; we were a little 
better than 40 miles below the city when I first saw the lights: of 
the ‘‘Louisiana;’’ I call it 40 miles down to tlie point on the star- 
board shore, and we were just below this point, which is known as 
Witkinson’s Point; we were rather a little nearer then to our star- 
board shore than the middle of the river is; this is the usual and 
proper place for a tow-boat going down; the steamship was a good 
distance down in the bend, apparently close in to her starboard 
shore; she blowed one whistle as she came up; we answered it with 
one tap of the ‘‘Celt’s’’ bell, which tap I gave myself; when I had 
tapped the bell, which was behind the pilot-house, and as I came 
forward, passing the pilot-house, I looked at my watch to see what 
time it was, and it wanted five minutes to three o’clock a. m.; 
just as I was looking at my watch the engineer, Mr. George Parks, 
who was standing forward on the larboard side of the lower deck,

I was engineer of the steamship ‘‘Louisiana’’ on the 22d day of

was sunk. She appeared to me to be near the bank and lying up 
and down the river. After the collision, the next order given to 
me was, I think, to go ahead. I made three revolutions ahead, 
and then I stopped. The next order was to back; the next order was 
to stop, and to go ahead; all of which I obeyed. When I first re-

would have been impossible to have backed her. The reason that

This is a case of collision, which occurred on the Mississippi river, 
opposite Wilkinson’s plantation, between 2 and 3 o’clock a. m. 
on the 22d of May, 1855. The tow-boat ‘‘Anglo Celt,’’ the prop- 
erty of the libellants, having the ship ‘“‘Neptune’’ in tow, left this: 
port between the hours of 9 and 10 o’clock, on the 21st of May, 
and proceeded on her voyage down the river with the intention of 
towing the ship to the Balize; she arrived at a point opposite the 
plantation above mentioned, when, it is contended by the libellants,

gave the usual signal that she intended to keep that shore; and the

I have here given the pleadings at length, in order to show dis- 
tinctly the issues which have been presented to the court, and in 
reference to which the parties have produced their evidence. Some 
points of difference may be at once disposed of, because I consider 
the evidence sufficiently clear to place them beyond a reasonable 


doubt. Iam perfectly satisfied, in the first place, that both the | 


tow-boat and the steamship were provided with signal-lights; I 


am satisfied that the lights from the ship were plainly visible to 


those on the descending tow-boat and her tow, at a proper distance 
off; but it is not so clear that those on the tow-boat were easily 
discernible on board the steamship. Whether this arose from the 
insufficiency of the lights themselves, or the want of a proper 
look-out on the steamship, will be hereafter considered. In the 
second place, I am satisfied that the collision occurred a very short 
distance above the point near the eastern bank of the river, which, 
as appears by the diagrams, is a short distance above the church; 
that after the collision, the tow-boat was prevented from drifting 
with the current of the river over towards the western shore by 
the propelling power of the steamship, which moved her somewhat 
nearer to the eastern shore than the place where the boats came 
together, and that they drifted together to the spot where the tow- 
boat afterwards sank, a short distance below the church. I con- 
sider it also satisfactorily proven that the proper signal was given

by the steamship to indicate her determination to keep close to the 
eastern shore as the vessels approached each other, and that a 
proper response was made by the tow-boat, indicative of her de- 
termination to pass to the right descending; but it is not clear 
from the evidence that the response was heard by those in charge 
of the steamship. 

It would be idle for this court to rely solely upon the testimony 
of those who were in charge of the respective boats, in its anxious 
endeavors to arrive at a satisfactory conclusion. From evidence of 
so conflicting a character we naturally turn to the testimony of 
witnesses who are entirely disinterested, and who viewed the dis- 
aster from points more favorable to correct observations under 
circumstances to be less liable to be influenced or affected by the 
excitement and confusion which such a disaster would necessarily 
produce. But even here the task of the court has not been ren- 
dered plain and easy, for the witnesses on shore and on the ship 
“‘Neptune’’ have failed to remove all doubts as to the real cause of 
the collision. I do not hesitate to say, however, that in reference 
to material facts I have been governed by the corroborative evidence 
of persons on shore, rather than by the unsupported declarations 
of the officers and crew of the respective vessels, however positive. 

The point above the church, or perhaps a little above that point, 
is doubtless the spot where the collision occurred. It is sucha 
spot at which collisions commonly do, and may be expected to 
occur, and it is therefore just the locality where prudence and pre- 
caution should be particularly observed by both descending and 
ascending vessels. Vessels above and below such a point are not 
always easily discernible to one another, and therefore it is the 
more important that they should keep constant and vigilant ‘‘look- 
outs’’ stationed in their proper places while they are passing it up 
or down. In such localities it is particularly important that the 
descending boat should keep her proper place in the middle of the 
river, and for the manifest reason, that an ascending boat in round- 
ing a point is necessarily compelled by shoal water ona bar to pass 
further out shore than when she is running where no such obsta- 
cles intervene. In such localities it is also particularly important 
that ascending boats should lessen their speed and increase their 
vigilance, because of the difficulty of discerning descending ves- 
sels, and their liability to encounter them too suddenly to avoid a 
collision. I am satisfied from experience that these rules of pru- 
dence and precaution cannot be too strictly enforced in the naviga- 
tion of the Mississippi river. 

After weighing with the greatest attention all the evidence ad- 
duced in this cause, I cannot resist the conclusion that both vessels 
were in fault. Notwithstanding the positive declarations of the 
officers crew of the tow-boat, supported by the testimony of wit- 
nesses Robert Johnson and wife, Iam convinced that that vessel

I certainly would not be understood as contending that it was 
the indispensable duty of the tow-boat to have kept as near the

the ‘“‘Anglo Celt’’ was out of her proper place, and much too near

contributed to produce the disaster from which her owners have 
been the sufferers. It is not enough in my judgment to say that 
there was room enough for the ascending vessel to pass between 
the descending boat and the bank of the river. No speculation 
should be indulged, no risks run, by persons engaged in the navi- 
gation of a river as broad as the Mississippi. Abundant caution 
should be observed, and all necessary precautions taken to avoid a 
collision. 

I have already intimated my opinion, that both the vessels in 
this case were to blame. The course pursued by those in charge 
of the steamship can neither be excused nor justified upon the evi- 
dence in this record. If the tow-boat was out of her proper place, 
and too far in the track of ascending vessels, the steamship was 
equally reprehensible in failing to observe the necessary precau- 
tions in running around and near the point. She was running at 
the rate of 10 or 11 knots an hour, and never attempted to slacken 
her speed until the tow-boat was so near that it was impossible to 
avoid the collision. All the evidence of respondents in relation to

tory. That these lights were up and seen by persons on shore, 
and by the witness Mansfield, who was on the steamship, are facts 
which cannot admit of a reasonable doubt. If they were not ob- 
served and duly attended to, it was because there was no proper 
look-out, and no suitable person at the helm. The fact that the 
captain, who was the only licensed pilot on board, was absent from

This is a cause of collision, in which the district court pro- 
nounced a sentence of condemnation against both the parties, and

charged them equally with the loss occasioned by the disaster. 
The collision occurred in the Mississippi river, near the court-house 
of the parish of Plaquemines, between the ocean steamer Louisiana 
and the steam tow-boat Anglo Celt, on the morning (3 a. m.) of 
the 22d May, 1855, while the former was ascending, and the latter 
descending, the river. The Anglo Celt was so injured that she 
sunk a few minutes after the collision, and is a total loss. The 
collision happened when the weather was fair and the stars shin- 
ing brightly, and at a place in the river at which there was no ob- 
struction to the navigation, and nothing to prevent the officers of 
both the vessels from having a full view of their respective motions. 
It is not supposed to have been the result of an inevitable accident; 
but each of the parties has exhausted his resources in an effort to 
criminate the other. The district court was of opinion that both 
had succeeded, and in this opinion I concur. 

The 3d rule ‘‘for the government of pilots,’’ framed by the in- 
spectors appointed under the authority of the act of Congress of 
the 30th August, 1852, is, that ‘‘when steamers are about to meet 
each other in narrow channels or in a fog, it shall be the duty of 
the descending boat to keep the channel and check her engine, 
using only sufficient steam to give her steerage, until the follow- 
ing signals are given and answered: It shall be the duty of the 
pilot of the ascending boat, so soon as the other shall be in sight 
and hearing, to sound his bell once if he shall wish to keep his 
boat to the right; and it shall be the duty of the pilot of the de- 
scending boat to answer the same promptly by one stroke of the 
bell; if not answered the pilot of the ascending boat shall strike 
his bell again and again at short intervals, until heard and an- 
swered by the pilot of the other boat; but if the pilot of the ascend- 
ing boat should wish to keep his boat to the left, he shall strike 
his bell twice, and it shall be the duty of the pilot of the descend- 
ing boat to answer the same by two strokes of his bell, and both 
boats shall be steered accordingly. The first signal shal} be given 
by the pilot of the ascending boat, and it shall be the duty of the 
pilot of the other to answer promptly; but in case the pilot of the 
ascending boat does not make the signal in proper time, the pilot 
of the descending boat shall make the signal, and the other shall 
answer promptly.’’ 

The 7th rule is, that ‘‘the signals by ringing bells shall be ob- 
served by all steamers in meeting and passing each other, whether 
by night or day, or whether in a narrow or wide river.’’ It is 
impossible to misconceive the object of these regulations. They 
were designed to prevent the danger of collisions in river naviga- 
tion, by imposing upon the master and pilot of steam-vessels the 
duty of alertness and vigilance while their vessel was approach- 
ing others of the same description from an opposite direction. They 
were required to give a timely warning of their presence, approach,

and the direction they were to take. The obligation to do this was 
independent of the opinion those officers might have of the neces- 
sity of the precaution, or of the existence of any hazard. It was 
to be given at any time, night or day, and at any place, whether 
in a wide or narrow river, in which vessels might be in the act of 
passing each other. It is a very comprehensive regulation, and 
one, if efficiently enforced in river navigation, must be productive 
of great benefit. The evidence in this case shows a neglect of this 
rule in its spirit and significance. 

The first signal came from the Louisiana, the ascending boat 
under these circumstances. The steersman, McCabe, says, “‘whem

On his cross-examination this witness answers, ‘‘that about one

The officer in charge of the Louisiana, and who controlled her 
movement at the time of the collision, was her mate. He was not 
a licensed pilot, and had not been accustomed to conduct the 
Louisiana in her voyages on the Mississippi river. He says: ‘I 
was on deck, and saw an object ahead of our ship, and seeing it, I 
stopped to examine what it might be. My first thought was that 
it was a vessel ascending the river, but soon discovered that we 
were getting too close to each other. I immediately called to the 
man to put his helm hard to port, then started aft and blew the 
whistle, the handle of which is in the pilot-house. At the door I 
found the captain at the wheel, assisting the man to put the wheel 
a-port. We were too near at the time to avoid the collision, and 
— came together. The vessel had not time to answer her

The mate explains the cause of his error to have been that he 
could not ascertain what the object before him was; that he could 
not procure any lights, and supposed it might be a shade upon the 
water from the trees, or an ascending vessel, but that the object 
was so near the bank that he did not suppose it to be a descending 
vessel, as that would not have been her proper place. He testifies 
that the Louisiana ascended the river near to the east bank, and 
that she did not leave that ordinary track.

These two persons (mate and steersman) were those upon whose 
efficiency and vigilance the guidance of this vessel at the time of 
the collision depended. The master, who alone was competent to 
act as a pilot, was disabled, and had retired to his room. He was 
drawn from it by the hurried and anxious order of the mate to the

ee the tow-boat they gave him no information of her ap-

This evidence shows that the mate saw before him an object on 
the waters, apparently moving in his track; probably a vessel; 
possibly a descending vessel. He continues his course without 
adopting a single efficient measure to satisfy himself so as to avoid 
a possible catastrophe. 

His vessel is moving at the rate of ten or twelve miles an hour; 
his master, the regular pilot, is not on duty, and he is himself an 
inexperienced officer. 

He still goes forward without any check upon his speed, without 
calling for information for the look-outs, or taking counsel of the 
master and pilot, or affording any signal to the vessel before him, 
until the destruction of one or of both vessels becomes unavoidable. 

The law does not permit vessels like the Louisiana to pass over 
a great thoroughfare, like the Mississippi river below New Orleans, 
on any uncertain or dubious conditions. It requires that they shall 
be equipped with an efficient body of skilful officers and men, fitted 
for every necessity of the navigation, and who shall constantly con- 
duct them with caution and vigilance. I agree with the district 
court that these were not displayed in the management of the 
Louisiana when this collision took place. The steersman of the 
Anglo Celt (James Atkins) testifies: “‘When I first J first saw the

I have placed the evidence of these witnesses in just a position 
in order to ascertain whether at the time the disaster had become 
inevitable the officers of the Anglo Celt had performed the duty 
the law imposed upon them when approaching another vessel. It 
appears from the evidence before stated that the danger was dis- 
covered by the mate of the Louisiana before any other person. 
His hurried order to ‘‘put the helm hard a-port,’’ expressed his 
consciousness of an imminent peril, and immediately after he 
sounded the whistle, when it was too late to avert the calamity 
that ensued. The pilot of the Anglo Celt was the last to discover 
the presence of danger among those on the look-out on his vessel. 
The engineer and the steersman had both exclaimed that a colli- 
sion was impending before he gave orders for any precautionary 
movement. The duty of avoiding a collision is not thrown upon 
either an ascending or a descending vessel exclusively, but both

are required to act concurrently in the measures necessary for that 
purpose. 

The rules of the inspectors require that the descending vessel 
shall check its engine, using only sufficient steam to give her 
steerage while she is waiting for a signal, and in the event that 
the ascending boat does not give one the pilot of the descending 
boat shall make the signal. In the case before me it is clear that 
if these directions have been followed a collision would not have 
ensued. 

It is no answer for the libellants to say that they did not 
anticipate a collision, and therefore did not comply with the 
regulations of the inspectors. It may happen that from the dark- 
ness of the night the shade cast on the water by objects on 
shore, the prevalence of haze or fogs, that one of the vessels may 
be concealed from the view of the other. The lights of the one 
may be brilliant, and those of the other dim and obscure, as in 
this instance. The regulations of the inspectors contemplate such 
a state of facts, and provide that the officers of both vessels shall be 
vigilant, and imposes the duty of giving signals upon both parties. 
The ascending boat is required, ‘‘so soon as the other shall be in 
sight and hearing,’’ to announce her approach and direction, but 
if she neglects this task it is devolved upon the descending boat to 
perform a corresponding duty. In the present instance this re- 
quirement was not efficiently performed by either of the parties. 

The great question made in the evidence and the arguments of 
the proctors in reference to the place of the collision, and whether 
the Louisiana was too near the channel, or the Anglo Celt too near 
the eastern shore, has not appeared to me to be so important in the 
view I have taken of the cause. If any reliance can be placed 
upon the testimony of the officers in charge of the Louisiana, they 
had no expectation of meeting the Anglo Celt until all attempts 
to avoid a collision were necessarily abortive. And if I can credit 
the officers of the other vessel, their fears were not at all aroused 
until their situation was desperate. The fault must therefore be 
looked for in some antecedent act or omission. The state of the 
evidence does not allow me to say with confidence whether the one 
party or the other was in his proper place. The preponderance of 
numbers, and perhaps of credit, is in favor of the witnesses who tes- 
tify on behalf of the steamship. But the liability to error is so 
great, even when witnesses have no disposition to prevaricate, in 
determining on a fact of the kind under such circumstances, indis- 
poses me to decide a case upon such a fact, if there are other 
grounds upon which it can be properly decided. 


Decree affirmed; costs to be divided. 
(Signed) J. A. CAMPBELL, 


Associate Justice of the Supreme Court.
