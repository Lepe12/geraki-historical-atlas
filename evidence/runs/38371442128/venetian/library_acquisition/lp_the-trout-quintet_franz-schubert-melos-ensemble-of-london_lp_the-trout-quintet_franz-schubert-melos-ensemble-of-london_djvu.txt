Schubert’s only piano quintet was written during a 
walking holiday in Upper Austria in the summer of 
1819, when he was twenty-two. He had managed to save 
up a little money during the preceding months, and so, 
early in July, he set out from Vienna with his friend 
Michael Vogl, the great baritone for whom he wrote 
many of his songs. Their first call was at Vogl’s birth- 
place, Steyr, a town not far from Linz. In spite of thun- 
derstorms during the first days of the visit, which put a 
stop to country walks, Schubert did not find life there 
dull. “At the house where I lodge there are eight girls, 
nearly all. pretty; he wrote to his brother Ferdinand. 
“Plenty to do, you see.’ 

Musical evenings at Steyr were spent in the house of 
Silvester Paumgartner, a hospitable mining director and 
an amateur cellist who, according to Albert Stadler, 
asked Schubert to write a quintet for the same instru- 
ments as those featured in Hummel’s Quintet for piano, 
violin, viola, cello and double bass, which had been 
published a year or two earlier. If this is untrue, the 
only way to explain the unusual instrumentation of 
Schubert’s piece is to suppose that just such a combina- 
tion happened to be available at the time. In the free- 
and-easy atmosphere of domestic music-making among 
Schubert’s family and friends,there-would-have_been

The Allegro vivace is dominated by the upward ar- 
peggio in triplets with which the piano opens the move- 
ment, and which forms an essential part of the first sub- 
ject. The theme is discussed at characteristic Schuber- 
tian length. The second subject, in E, is in two parts: a 
lyrical duet for cello and violin, leading to a lilting tune 
on the piano. The development is based on the first 
theme, with the triplets more in evidence than ever. 

The F major Andante is in two sections, the second of 
which is a repetition (with much variation of key and 
texture) of the first. Each section has three distinct parts 
—a rhythmic theme in octaves on the piano (first in F, 
later in A flat); a wistful tune for viola and cello (first in 
F sharp minor, later in A minor), and another rhythmic 
piano theme (first in D, later in F). The third movement 
is a jaunty Scherzo, full of sudden accents and dynamic 
changes, enclosing a Trio (in D, but with a delightful 
shift to B flat in its second half) which is virtually a dia- 
logue between strings and piano. 

The five variations of which the Forelle theme — or 
rather, a variant of it — are the subject in the fourth 
movement, never obscure the outline of the tune; the 
first two are in D, the third (and most adventurous) in 
D minor, the fourth in B flat. Finally the theme reap- 
pears in an Allegretto coda, with the rippling triplet ac- 
companiment of the song itself. The finale is ostensibly 
in sonata form, but with the double bar coming imme- 

iately before the recapitulation and after such develop- 
ment as there is. The principal theme, whose gypsy tang 
reminds us that Schubert paid a visit to Hungary in 
1818, is in sharp contrast with the insouciant lilt of the

The Adagio and Rondo for piano, violin, viola and 
cello was composed in October 1816, probably for Hein- 
rich Grob, an amateur cellist (though the-cello part>is 
not-in-any-way-spectacular). In style the work is more 
like a concerto movement for piano and strings than a 
piano quartet,fand Schubert would certainly have been 
the last person to object to the cello part being rein- 
forced if—as in this recording—a double bass happened 
to be present; iftdééd it nray well have been played in 
this way together with the “Trout” Quintet at Steyr 
during the happy summer of 1819.£areful listeners will 
also observe that some very slight adjustments have been 
made to Schubert’s own “orchestration;’ but again one 
feels sure that the composer could only have regarded 
these as an improvement. The Adagio is the most Schu- 
bertian part of the work. The Rondo’to which it serves 
as a preface is not in fact a rondo at all but a sonata 
movement; the cut of its themes is inescapably 
Mozartian but their development has a distinct taste of- 
Hummel and Weber, if not the full flavor of the Schu-

THE MELOS ENSEMBLE of London, formed in 1951, takes its 
name from the Greek word melos meaning tune or melody. Exten- 
sively recorded in Europe the Ensemble has gained a distinguished 
reputation among the world’s chamber music groups. Its engage- 


- ments include appearances at the festivals at Edinburgh, Chelten- 


ham, Leeds, York, Aldeburgh, Venice, Warsaw and Holland as well 
as concert tours in Great Britain, the European Continent, and the 
United States. Consisting of nine players in all — clarinet, bassoon, 
horn, bass, piano and a string quartet — the Melos Ensemble’s rep- 
ertoire includes music written for trios, quintets, septets and octets. 


Members of the Ensemble heard on this recording are as follows: 


LAMAR CROWSON, American by birth, was a student of Arthur 
Benjamin at the Royal College and won many awards including 
the Chappell Gold Medal and the Harriet Cohen International 
Award. Since last January a Professor at the University of Cape 
Town, he returns frequently to play with the Melos Ensemble in 
England. 

EMANUEL HURWITZ, the violinist, is a prominent figure in 
London musical circles, the leader of the English Chamber Orches- 
tra and a distinguished soloist and chamber music player. 

CECIL ARONOWITZ, one of the leading viola players of Europe, 
is Professor of Viola and Chamber Music at the Royal College of 
Music. He is a well known soloist and member of many chamber 
music groups. 


TERENCE WEIL, the cellist, winner of many prizes at the Royal 
Academy of Music, is a specialist in chamber music and has been 
the principal cellist with London’s major orchestras. 


ADRIAN BEERS, a Scot, is Professor of the Double Bass at the 


' Royal College of Music. He is at present principal bass of the New 


Philharmonia Orchestra and of the English Chamber Orchestra.
